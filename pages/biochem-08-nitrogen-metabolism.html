Biochemistry · Note 08

Nitrogen Metabolism: Amino Acids, the Urea Cycle and Nucleotides

One asymmetry organises this whole note: carbon and nitrogen have completely different exits. Burn a fat or a sugar and the carbon leaves as CO₂, which is a gas, is buffered, and needs no machinery. Burn an amino acid and you are left holding a nitrogen atom that becomes ammonia — a small, membrane-permeant, neurotoxic base that no tissue can store and only one organ can dispose of. Every pathway on this page is a consequence of that problem: nitrogen is never released where it is produced, it is handed along a chain of carriers to the liver, and there four high-energy phosphates are spent to lock it into urea. The same logic runs the other way for nucleotides, where nitrogen is not waste but the most expensive raw material in the cell — which is why almost every drug that kills a dividing cell attacks this chemistry.

§ 01

Nitrogen balance and the ammonia problem

The ideaWhen you burn sugar or fat, the leftovers are water and a gas you breathe out, and nothing has to be organised. When you burn protein there is one extra atom per building block that has nowhere to go. Left to itself it turns into a small molecule that slips through every membrane, poisons the brain, and cannot be stored anywhere. So the body never lets it loose. It is passed from hand to hand on carrier molecules, all the way to the liver, and only there is it converted — at real expense — into something bland and soluble that the kidney can throw away.

Definition & key facts

Nitrogen balance
Nitrogen taken in as dietary protein minus nitrogen lost in urine, faeces and skin. Positive in growth, pregnancy and recovery; negative in starvation, trauma, sepsis, burns and any state where an essential amino acid is missing.
protein ≈ 16 % nitrogen by mass → 1 g N ≡ 6.25 g protein the conversion factor behind every clinical nitrogen calculation
Full definition, symbols and units
Amino acid pool
The free amino acids of the body — roughly 100 g in an adult, turning over completely in hours. There is no storage form of protein: every amino acid in the pool is either being built into protein, being used to make something (haem, nucleotides, neurotransmitters), or being taken apart. An excess is not saved; it is deaminated and the skeleton burnt or stored as fat.
Ammonia / ammonium
NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are the same substance in two protonation states, in equilibrium with pKa 9.25. Clinical assays report the sum ("ammonia"). Only the uncharged NH₃ crosses membranes freely, which makes the toxicity pH-dependent.

Why there is a problem at all

Compare the three fuels honestly. Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆: oxidise it completely and you get CO₂ and H₂O. Palmitate is C₁₆H₃₂O₂: same two products. Alanine is C₃H₇NO₂, and that single nitrogen is the whole story. It cannot be oxidised to a gas, it cannot be stored, and in its simplest free form — ammonia — it is a base that is toxic at micromolar concentrations. Every other waste product of metabolism is either volatile or inert; this one is neither.

So evolution took the expensive route. Terrestrial mammals are ureotelic: they spend ATP to condense two nitrogens and one carbon into urea, a small, uncharged, extremely soluble, chemically inert molecule that the kidney filters freely. Fish are ammonotelic — they simply let ammonia diffuse into the surrounding water, which is free but requires an infinite sink. Birds and reptiles are uricotelic, excreting uric acid as a semi-solid paste, which costs the most energy but the least water — the right answer if you must develop inside a shell with no way to dispose of a liquid, or if every gram of carried water costs you flight. That last choice comes back to haunt us in § 11.

The one number that pays for itself

Protein is 16 % nitrogen, so 1 g of urinary nitrogen came from 6.25 g of protein. That factor turns a 24-hour urine collection into a measurement of protein catabolism, and it is the basis of every nitrogen-balance question you will meet. The partner number is that urea is 47 % nitrogen (28 of 60 g·mol⁻¹) — so a gram of urea is not a gram of nitrogen, and the commonest arithmetic slip on this topic is treating them as if they were.

Exam technique · nitrogen-balance arithmetic in four moves

  1. Recognise the type in five seconds — the stem hands you grams of protein, grams of nitrogen, or a 24-hour urine, and asks for urea, for ~P, or for protein catabolised. Nothing conceptual is being tested. Every mark is in the unit conversions, so do not re-read the physiology.
  2. Run one chain, always in the same direction — protein ×0.16 → g N; ÷28.02 → mol urea (2 N per urea); ×60.06 → g urea; ×4 → mol ~P. Going the other way, divide instead. Write the chain down before you look at the options; improvising it costs three minutes, running it costs forty seconds.
  3. Sanity-check with one ratio — grams of urea must come out at ≈2.14 × grams of nitrogen (60.06 / 28.02). Any option whose urea mass equals or undershoots the nitrogen mass is physically impossible and can be struck off before you calculate anything. Equivalently, 1 g of protein yields ≈0.16 g N and ≈0.34 g urea.
  4. Check which number the stem actually asked for — "3 ATP" and "4 high-energy phosphates" are both true of the same cycle (§ 05), and option sets are built out of that pair. So are "g N" versus "g urea". Distractors here are lifted straight from correct textbook sentences; the error is that they answer a different question.
Worked example 1 — from a plate of food to a molecule of urea

A 70 kg adult eats 1.2 g protein per kg per day and is in nitrogen balance. (a) How much nitrogen must he excrete? (b) If essentially all of it left as urea, how many grams and how many moles would that be? (c) What does making it cost, and how does that compare with his total daily ATP turnover of roughly 60 kg?

  1. (a) Protein intake = 70 × 1.2 = 84 g/day. Nitrogen = 84 × 0.16 = 13.44 g N/day — identically 84 / 6.25.
  2. (b) Urea carries 2 N, so moles of urea = 13.44 / (2 × 14.01) = 0.480 mol/day.
  3. Mass of urea = 0.480 × 60.06 = 28.8 g/day. Note it is more than twice the nitrogen mass, because urea is only 47 % N.
  4. (c) The urea cycle spends 4 high-energy phosphates per urea (§ 05), so 4 × 0.480 = 1.92 mol ~P/day.
  5. At 507 g·mol⁻¹ that is 1.92 × 507 ≈ 973 g of ATP equivalents — about 973 / 60 000 ≈ 1.6 % of daily turnover.
  6. Reality check: about 2 g of nitrogen leaves in faeces and skin, and urea is ≈85 % of what the kidney excretes, so the real figure is nearer 21 g of urea, 347 mmol, per day. The order of magnitude is what matters — a few hundred millimoles of urea a day, costing one to two per cent of the body's entire energy budget. Nitrogen disposal is not free, but it is not ruinous either; the cell buys safety cheaply.
13.4 g N → 0.48 mol = 28.8 g urea · 1.9 mol ~P ≈ 1.6 % of daily ATP turnover

What ammonia actually does to the brain

"Ammonia is neurotoxic" is a statement, not an explanation. The mechanism is worth knowing because it is built entirely out of reactions you already have, and because it is examined as a vignette rather than as a fact.

  1. It drains the citric acid cycle. Brain has no urea cycle. Its only large-capacity ammonia sink is to run glutamate dehydrogenase backwards — reductive amination of α-ketoglutarate — and then glutamine synthetase. Every ammonia fixed consumes one α-ketoglutarate, which is a citric acid cycle intermediate. Deplete α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate cannot be regenerated, flux through the cycle falls, and ATP production drops in the organ least able to tolerate it.
  2. It swells the astrocytes. Glutamine synthetase in the brain lives almost exclusively in astrocytes. Glutamine accumulates there and is osmotically active; water follows; astrocytes swell. In acute hyperammonaemia this is frank cerebral oedema, and it is what kills.
  3. It distorts neurotransmission. Glutamate is the principal excitatory transmitter and the precursor of GABA, the principal inhibitory one. Consuming glutamate to make glutamine perturbs both.
  4. It makes itself worse. Ammonia stimulates the respiratory centre → hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis → a higher blood pH → a larger fraction of the total ammonia existing as the freely diffusible NH₃ → more entering the brain. Worked example 2 puts numbers on that loop, and it also explains why an acute hyperammonaemic neonate has an alkalosis while an organic acidaemia presents with an anion-gap acidosis — a discriminator that is worth marks.
Worked example 2 — why alkalosis makes hyperammonaemia worse

A patient with cirrhosis has a plasma total ammonia of 200 µmol/L (normal ≈10–40). He hyperventilates and arterial pH rises from 7.40 to 7.70. Given pKa(NH₄⁺/NH₃) = 9.25, what happens to the concentration of the species that can actually cross the blood–brain barrier?

  1. Henderson–Hasselbalch, rearranged for the ratio: [NH₄⁺]/[NH₃] = 10(pKa − pH).
  2. At pH 7.40: 101.85 = 70.8, so the NH₃ fraction is 1/(1 + 70.8) = 1.393 %, i.e. 200 × 0.01393 = 2.79 µmol/L of free NH₃.
  3. At pH 7.70: 101.55 = 35.5, so the fraction is 1/36.5 = 2.741 %, i.e. 5.48 µmol/L.
  4. Ratio: 2.741 / 1.393 = 1.97. The permeant species has almost exactly doubled while the measured "ammonia" has not changed at all.
  5. The general rule falls out of the algebra: well below the pKa, the free-base fraction is proportional to 10pH, so every 0.3 pH units doubles it.

This is not a curiosity. It is why lactulose works in hepatic encephalopathy: colonic bacteria ferment it to short-chain acids, the luminal pH falls, NH₃ diffusing in from the blood is protonated to NH₄⁺, and a charged ion cannot diffuse back out — a classic ion trap, plus a cathartic effect. It is also why correcting the alkalosis matters, and why a gastrointestinal bleed (a large protein meal delivered to urease-producing gut flora) precipitates encephalopathy in a cirrhotic.

free NH₃ 2.79 → 5.48 µmol/L, a 1.97-fold rise, on an unchanged total

Total ammonia is not free ammonia

A laboratory "ammonia" of 200 µmol/L sounds enormous next to a normal of 40, and it is — but it is a total. Only about 1.4 % of it is the neutral molecule that moves. Two questions turn on keeping those apart: why the toxicity is pH-sensitive when the measured value is not, and why the kidney can trap ammonium in acid urine and excrete it (which is also how the kidney exports acid — see § 04).

§ 02

Transamination, PLP and the clinical aminotransferases

The ideaThe first thing that happens to an amino acid being taken apart is not that its nitrogen is set free — it is that the nitrogen is handed over to a different molecule. Nothing is released, nothing is oxidised, nothing is spent; an amino group and a carbonyl group simply swap places between two partners. The point of the exercise is bookkeeping: whatever amino acid you started with, the nitrogen ends up on one particular carrier, so the cell only ever needs one machine for the dangerous step that follows. The enzymes that do this are also the ones on a liver-function test, which is why they show up in clinical questions.

Definition & reaction

Transamination（轉胺作用）
Reversible transfer of an α-amino group from an amino acid to an α-keto acid, catalysed by an aminotransferase (transaminase) using pyridoxal 5′-phosphate as cofactor. No nitrogen is released and no net oxidation occurs.
amino acid + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ α-keto acid + glutamate α-ketoglutarate is the universal acceptor; glutamate is the universal collector
Full definition, symbols and units
Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)
The active form of vitamin B₆ (pyridoxine). An aldehyde that forms a Schiff base with substrate amines and acts as an electron sink: the protonated pyridine ring nitrogen delocalises the negative charge of the carbanion intermediate, which is what makes the α-carbon chemistry possible at all.
Ping-pong bi-bi kinetics
The mechanistic signature of transamination. The first substrate binds, leaves the enzyme modified (as pyridoxamine phosphate) and departs before the second substrate binds. On a double-reciprocal plot this gives a family of parallel lines.

Why funnel everything through glutamate?

There are nineteen amino acids to dismantle and one dangerous product. If each were deaminated directly, the cell would need nineteen deaminases, nineteen places where free ammonia appears, and nineteen chances to poison itself. Instead it uses a collector. Transamination is thermodynamically free (ΔG°′ ≈ 0, Keq ≈ 1), so a whole family of aminotransferases can run either way at no energetic cost, all converging on the same acceptor, α-ketoglutarate. The nitrogen arrives on glutamate, and glutamate — one molecule, one enzyme, one organelle — is where the actual release happens (§ 03).

That is the entire architecture: collect reversibly and everywhere; release irreversibly and in one place. It is worth noticing how much of metabolism is built this way.

Transamination does not produce ammonia

This is the single most reliable trap on the topic and it appears in some form most years. A transaminase moves a nitrogen atom from one carbon skeleton to another; the total number of amino groups in the room is unchanged, nothing is oxidised, and no NH₄⁺ appears. Free ammonia is produced by deaminases and dehydrogenases — glutamate dehydrogenase, glutaminase, serine and threonine dehydratase, histidase, AMP deaminase. If an option says "transamination releases ammonia", it is wrong however plausible the rest of the sentence is.

Go deeper · what PLP is actually doing, step by step

PLP appears in this note four separate times — transamination, serine and threonine dehydratase (§ 03), serine hydroxymethyltransferase (§ 09) and cystathionine β-synthase (§ 08) — so the mechanism is worth one careful read rather than four separate memorisations. Every PLP enzyme exploits the same trick.

  1. Resting state: the internal aldimine. In the free enzyme, PLP is already bound as a Schiff base to the ε-amino group of an active-site lysine. It is not floating loose.
  2. Transaldimination. The incoming amino acid's α-amino group attacks that Schiff base and displaces the lysine, giving the external aldimine — now PLP is covalently attached to the substrate, and the freed lysine is perfectly placed to act as the general base for the next step.
  3. Carbanion formation. The lysine abstracts the α-hydrogen. Ordinarily a carbanion on that carbon would be impossibly unstable; here the negative charge delocalises into the conjugated pyridine ring, whose protonated ring nitrogen is the ultimate electron sink. The resonance-stabilised species is the quinonoid intermediate, and it is the branch point of all PLP chemistry.
  4. Reprotonation, then hydrolysis. Reprotonate the quinonoid at the cofactor's C4′ (the carbon that was the aldehyde) instead of at the α-carbon and you get a ketimine; hydrolyse that and out comes the α-keto acid, leaving the nitrogen behind on the cofactor as pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP). The first half-reaction is complete and the first product has left — this is why the kinetics are ping-pong.
  5. The second half-reaction runs the identical chemistry backwards with α-ketoglutarate, regenerating PLP and releasing glutamate.

The reason this matters conceptually: the quinonoid intermediate can be resolved in more than one way, and which bond around the α-carbon is broken is decided by the enzyme, not by the cofactor — by which bond is held perpendicular to the ring plane, where orbital overlap is best. Break the C–H bond and you get transamination or racemisation; break the C–COOH bond and you get decarboxylation (histidine → histamine, DOPA → dopamine, glutamate → GABA, serine → ethanolamine); break the C–R bond and you get a side-chain cleavage (serine → glycine). One cofactor, one intermediate, four reaction classes. That is also why isoniazid, which reacts with pyridoxal, causes both a peripheral neuropathy and — in overdose — seizures from failed GABA synthesis, and why the antidote is pyridoxine.

ALT and AST: why these two, on every liver panel

Aminotransferases are cytosolic (AST also has a mitochondrial isoform) and are present in cells at concentrations enormously higher than in plasma. They are not secreted. So a raised serum activity means one thing: cells have leaked or died. The clinical value is not the enzyme's function but its tissue distribution and its half-life.

The De Ritis ratio (AST/ALT) is the piece examiners actually use. A ratio below 1 with both enzymes high in the hundreds or thousands is the pattern of viral or toxic hepatitis and of non-alcoholic fatty liver. A ratio above 2, with both enzymes only modestly raised, is close to characteristic of alcoholic liver disease. Two reasons are usually given, and both are mechanistic rather than arbitrary: alcohol injures mitochondria, releasing the mitochondrial AST isoform that ALT has no counterpart for; and chronic alcohol use depletes pyridoxal phosphate, which limits ALT more than AST. Note also what a falling transaminase means in fulminant hepatic failure — often not recovery but that there are few hepatocytes left to leak.

Which old name goes with which enzyme, and which one is the liver's

G·P·T → Pyruvate → ALT → Liver · G·O·T → OAA → AST → everything else

This is not arbitrary and it is not really a mnemonic — it is the naming convention read out loud. The old names are glutamate–pyruvate transaminase and glutamate–oxaloacetate transaminase, and the middle letter is the α-keto acid the enzyme produces. GPT makes pyruvate, which only alanine can give, so GPT = ALT. GOT makes oxaloacetate, which only aspartate can give, so GOT = AST. Then one extra letter carries the clinical half: ALT is the Liver-selective one; AST is also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells. Worth the shelf space because Taiwanese laboratory reports still print GOT/GPT while the paper is written in English and will say AST/ALT. It does not encode the half-lives — ALT ≈47 h, AST ≈17 h — which you have to hold separately, and which is why AST falls first as an injury settles.

Exam technique · reading a transaminase pattern

  1. Ratio above 2, both only modestly raised — alcohol. This is close to a recognition rule; if the stem also mentions macrocytosis or a raised GGT you can stop reading the options and answer.
  2. Ratio below 1, both in the hundreds or thousands — viral or toxic hepatitis, or non-alcoholic fatty liver. The magnitude, not the ratio, is doing the work here.
  3. AST raised with a normal ALT — think about whether the liver is involved at all. Rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis and myocardial injury all do this. Any option that concludes "hepatocellular injury" from AST alone is beatable on tissue distribution.
  4. A falling transaminase is not automatically improvement — in fulminant failure it can mean there are few hepatocytes left to leak. If the stem pairs falling enzymes with worsening synthetic function or encephalopathy, the "recovering" option is the trap.
  5. Kill anything that makes a transaminase release ammonia — see the warning box above. It is the single most reliable free elimination on this topic and it recurs in stems that are otherwise about something else entirely.

The exceptions worth carrying

Lysine and threonine do not undergo transamination. Lysine's α-amino group is committed in the first step of its own degradation (saccharopine pathway), and threonine is handled by a dehydratase or an aldolase instead. Everything else feeds the glutamate collector. A second exception worth the same shelf space: the branched-chain amino acids are transaminated in muscle, not liver (liver has little branched-chain aminotransferase), which is why BCAAs are the one group whose catabolism begins peripherally — and why maple syrup urine disease in § 08 blocks the step after that.

§ 03

Deamination: releasing the nitrogen at last

The ideaSooner or later the nitrogen has to come off, and that step really does make free ammonia. The cell arranges for it to happen at one enzyme, on one molecule, inside the mitochondrion — the same compartment as the first step of the disposal machinery, so the ammonia is born a few nanometres from the enzyme that will lock it away. The enzyme is also reversible, and which way it runs depends on how much energy the cell has: short of ATP, it dismantles the carrier and feeds the skeleton into the citric acid cycle; flush with ATP, it stops.

Definition & reaction

Oxidative deamination
Removal of an amino group as free ammonia, with the carbon being oxidised in the same step. In mammals essentially one enzyme matters: glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), in the mitochondrial matrix.
glutamate + H₂O + NAD(P)⁺ ⇌ α-ketoglutarate + NH₄⁺ + NAD(P)H + H⁺ the only high-capacity route from an amino group to free ammonia
Full definition, symbols and units
Transdeamination
The two-step combination that actually happens in vivo: aminotransferase moves the nitrogen onto glutamate, then GDH releases it. The net effect is deamination of the original amino acid without any enzyme ever touching it with an oxidant.
Non-oxidative deamination
A handful of amino acids release ammonia by elimination rather than oxidation: serine and threonine dehydratase (PLP), histidase, and the amidases glutaminase and asparaginase, which hydrolyse a side-chain amide.

Two features of GDH repay attention. First, it is freely reversible and uses either nicotinamide cofactor: in the oxidative, catabolic direction, NADPH in the reductive direction that fixes ammonia back onto α-ketoglutarate. That is not sloppiness — it is the standard biochemical signature of an enzyme that must serve both a catabolic and an anabolic pathway, and it is exactly how the brain disposes of ammonia in § 01 and how bacteria and plants assimilate it.

Second, its allosteric regulation is by purine nucleotides, not by amino acids: GTP and ATP inhibit, GDP and ADP activate. Read that as an energy-charge sensor. When ATP is plentiful the cell has no reason to burn amino acids for fuel, so GDH is switched off and the nitrogen stays on glutamate. When ADP accumulates, GDH opens and the carbon skeleton is delivered into the citric acid cycle as α-ketoglutarate. The GTP signal is particularly neat, because GTP is made by succinyl-CoA synthetase within the citric acid cycle — a direct report from the cycle that it does not need more substrate.

Leucine, GDH and a real disease

Leucine is an allosteric activator of GDH. Gain-of-function mutations in GLUD1 that abolish GTP inhibition cause the hyperinsulinism–hyperammonaemia syndrome: unrestrained GDH in the pancreatic β-cell raises the ATP/ADP ratio, closes the KATP channel and drives insulin release — so the child has protein-induced, specifically leucine-induced, hypoglycaemia — while unrestrained GDH in the liver produces ammonia faster than the urea cycle is set up to take it. One enzyme, two organs, two apparently unrelated symptoms, both derivable. This is the shape of a good Part II vignette.

§ 04

Carrying nitrogen in the blood: glutamine and alanine

The ideaMuscle has no way of making urea, so when muscle protein is broken down the nitrogen has to travel. It does so on two molecules, chosen for different reasons. One of them is a pure courier that happens to carry two nitrogens at once and is also the fuel of the gut and the acid-handling substrate of the kidney. The other is cleverer: it carries one nitrogen to the liver and the carbon skeleton of a glucose precursor, so the liver can send back the very sugar the muscle needs. Nothing is wasted except energy, and the energy is spent by the organ that can afford it.

Why two carriers rather than one

Glutamine is the general-purpose answer. Its amide nitrogen is uncharged and unreactive, it moves two nitrogens for the price of one carrier, and — crucially — glutamine is also a biosynthetic nitrogen donor in its own right: the amide nitrogen ends up in purines (N3 and N9, § 10), in pyrimidines, in amino sugars, in asparagine, and in NAD⁺. It is simultaneously the safest way to store nitrogen and the currency in which nitrogen is spent. That dual role is why rapidly dividing tissues — enterocytes, activated lymphocytes, and tumours — consume glutamine avidly, and why asparaginase, used in acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, has hyperammonaemia as a predictable side effect.

Alanine solves a narrower and more elegant problem. Exercising muscle produces pyruvate from glycolysis and needs to dispose of amino groups from protein breakdown. Loading one onto the other costs nothing (ΔG°′ ≈ 0) and produces a molecule that carries both cargoes to the same destination. The liver strips the nitrogen for urea and returns the carbon as glucose. The arithmetic is deliberately unfair: gluconeogenesis from two pyruvates costs 6 high-energy phosphates in the liver, while the muscle recovers only 2 ATP from glycolysis. The liver is subsidising the muscle, exactly as it does in the Cori cycle — the difference being that the Cori cycle returns lactate and no nitrogen, while the Cahill cycle returns alanine and clears one nitrogen per turn.

The kidney's version, and why acidosis changes it

Renal tubular cells take up glutamine and run glutaminase then glutamate dehydrogenase, releasing two NH₄⁺ and leaving α-ketoglutarate, which is metabolised to bicarbonate. Secreting the NH₄⁺ into the tubular lumen exports a proton in a form that cannot diffuse back (the ion trap of § 01 again) while the bicarbonate is returned to blood. In chronic metabolic acidosis renal glutamine extraction is strongly upregulated — which is why "the kidney excretes acid as ammonium" and "the liver detoxifies ammonia as urea" are not in conflict: they are two organs using the same substrate for two different jobs, and in acidosis the split shifts towards the kidney, because urea synthesis consumes bicarbonate.

§ 05

The urea cycle, step by step

The ideaThe disposal machine is a loop of five reactions that starts and ends with the same molecule, so nothing is used up except the two nitrogens, a carbon, and some ATP. What makes it more than a list is that the loop is cut in half by a membrane: the first two reactions happen inside the mitochondrion, where the ammonia is made, and the last three happen outside it. Everything awkward about the pathway — why two transporters exist, why one inherited defect causes a strange urinary metabolite, why the cycle is welded to the citric acid cycle — follows from that one line drawn across the middle.

Reading the five steps as a story, not a list

Step 1 — CPS I is the only step that touches free ammonia, and it is deliberately expensive: two ATP to make one carbamoyl phosphate. Why two? Because the reaction has to be irreversible. Fixing a toxic metabolite is not a place for an equilibrium; the cell pays double to make sure the ammonia never comes back. This is the committed step, and — see § 06 — the only one under acute allosteric control.

Step 2 — OTC attaches the carbamoyl group to ornithine, giving citrulline. Ornithine is doing the same job as oxaloacetate in the citric acid cycle or CoA in β-oxidation: a carrier that comes back unchanged. The name to fix is that this is a transcarbamoylase, a group transfer, not a synthesis; the energy was already paid in step 1.

Step 3 — ASS is where the second nitrogen arrives, and it arrives on aspartate, not as ammonia. Look at the cost: → AMP + , which is two high-energy phosphates, not one, and the pyrophosphate is then hydrolysed by inorganic pyrophosphatase to make the step effectively irreversible — the identical trick used by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and fatty acyl-CoA synthetase. This is the slowest of the cytosolic steps and the one that limits flux there.

Step 4 — ASL cleaves argininosuccinate into arginine and fumarate. That fumarate is the hinge of the whole page: hydrate it to malate, oxidise it to oxaloacetate, transaminate it with glutamate (by AST) and you have regenerated aspartate. Nitrogen therefore enters the cycle twice from the same glutamate pool — once as ammonia via GDH, once as aspartate via AST — and the four-carbon skeleton is simply borrowed and returned.

Step 5 — arginase hydrolyses the guanidinium group off arginine, releasing urea and regenerating ornithine. Only this last step actually produces urea; the other four are construction. Arginase is why arginine is the one urea-cycle intermediate that is also a dietary amino acid, and why arginine supplementation is used in most urea cycle defects (§ 06).

The order of the ring, including what enters and what leaves

The standard English mnemonic, and the one you will meet in 補習班 and USMLE material, so use it rather than anything of your own: Ornithine → Carbamoyl phosphate → Citrulline → Aspartate → Argininosuccinate → Fumarate → Arginine → Urea. Eight words, eight items, in the order you would draw them. Two things it does not tell you, and both are examined. (1) Aspartate and fumarate are not links in the ring — aspartate enters at ASS carrying nitrogen ②, and fumarate leaves at ASL towards malate and oxaloacetate. Only ornithine, citrulline, argininosuccinate and arginine are actually in the loop. (2) It says nothing about the membrane; use the line below for that.

Which half of the cycle is inside the mitochondrion

Cops and Otters keep to the Matrix · Citrulline out, Ornithine in

Our own line, not a standard hook — but one sentence carries five enzyme locations and a transporter direction. Cops = CPS I and Otters = OTC: those two, and only those two, keep to the matrix. Five enzymes in the ring, so the remaining three — ASS, ASL and arginase-1 — are cytosolic; you do not have to store them separately, they are whatever is left over. Citrulline out, ornithine in: the two carriers that cross, on the same ORNT1 antiporter, one each way, which is why a single transporter suffices. Say the two directions in that order — citrulline is made inside and used outside, ornithine the reverse. One trap in the wording: the cop in the matrix is CPS I; CPS II, which builds pyrimidines (§ 10), is cytosolic. And derive it if the phrase deserts you: the mitochondrial half is exactly the two steps that handle carbamoyl phosphate, because ammonia is released in the matrix by GDH and the cell will not let a fixed-but-still-reactive intermediate wander. Everything after citrulline is outside. That derivation is also the whole of the orotic-aciduria story in § 06 — when OTC is missing, the carbamoyl phosphate that should never have left the matrix does exactly that.

Worked example 3 — counting the cost of a urea molecule properly

How many high-energy phosphates does one urea cost? How many per nitrogen disposed of? And is the usual figure an overestimate?

  1. CPS I: 2 ATP → 2 ADP + Pi. Each ATP loses one phosphoanhydride bond, so this is 2 ~P.
  2. ASS: ATP → AMP + PPi. Going to AMP breaks two anhydride bonds, so this is 2 ~P, not 1 — and the PPi is then hydrolysed, which is what pulls the reaction over.
  3. Total: 2 + 2 = 4 ~P per urea. Only 3 ATP molecules are consumed, which is why the overall equation shows 3 ATP but the answer to "how many high-energy phosphates" is 4. Both numbers are correct answers to different questions — read the stem.
  4. Per nitrogen: urea carries 2 N, so 4 / 2 = 2 ~P per nitrogen.
  5. Is 4 the true cost? Not quite. The fumarate leaving step 4 becomes malate and then oxaloacetate, and that oxidation makes one NADH, worth about 2.5 ATP. If those electrons reach the respiratory chain, the net cost falls to roughly 4 − 2.5 = 1.5 ~P per urea.
  6. So the honest statement is: 4 high-energy phosphates are spent, up to 2.5 ATP can be recovered, and the cell is not paying nearly as much as the bare stoichiometry suggests. On a single-best-answer paper, though, "4 high-energy phosphate bonds per urea" is the expected answer unless the stem explicitly mentions the fumarate.
4 ~P per urea (3 ATP hydrolysed) = 2 ~P per nitrogen · net ≈1.5 ~P after the NADH

Three things to keep straight about this cycle

(1) "Three ATP" and "four high-energy phosphates" are both right; the difference is whether you count the ATP → AMP step once or twice. (2) Ornithine and citrulline are not proteinogenic amino acids — they have no codon and appear in no protein. (3) The urea cycle is not a way of making energy or a way of making arginine for protein; it is a detoxification pathway, and the only tissue with all five enzymes is the liver. The kidney expresses ASS and ASL, which is why it can make arginine from the citrulline the gut exports but cannot make urea from ammonia.

Exam technique · attacking any urea-cycle question

  1. Draw the line before you read the options — a vertical stroke, CPS I and OTC on the left, ASS / ASL / arginase on the right, citrulline crossing rightwards and ornithine back. Ten seconds, and it answers compartment, transporter and orotate questions outright.
  2. Kill on nitrogen source — only nitrogen ① is free NH₄⁺; nitrogen ② arrives as the α-amino group of aspartate; the carbon and both oxygens come from bicarbonate. Any option saying both nitrogens come from ammonia, or that aspartate supplies the carbon, is dead without further thought.
  3. Kill on stoichiometry — CPS I spends 2 ~P and ASS spends 2 ~P, so 4 ~P from 3 ATP molecules. An option offering "3 high-energy phosphates" has simply read the 3 off the overall equation; an option offering 2 has forgotten that ATP → AMP breaks two anhydride bonds. Those two are the classic distractor pair.
  4. Kill on tissue — only liver has all five enzymes. Options that let muscle, brain or kidney make urea are wrong; kidney has ASS and ASL only, so it makes arginine, not urea.
  5. Kill on proteinogenicity — ornithine and citrulline have no codon and appear in no protein. Any option describing them as protein constituents, or as "non-essential amino acids of the diet", is wrong.
  6. Where the time goes — a compartment or stoichiometry question should be finished in twenty seconds because you drew the picture. Bank that time for the § 06 vignettes, which genuinely reward slow reading.
§ 06

Regulating the cycle, and what happens when it breaks

The ideaA pathway that only has to run when there is protein to get rid of needs a signal that means "protein is being eaten". The cell uses a beautifully indirect one: a small molecule that is made from the very things that pile up when amino acids are abundant, and which does nothing at all except switch on the first enzyme. When one of the five steps is missing from birth, the picture is always the same — a well baby who begins feeding, accumulates ammonia over a day or two, and collapses — and the only way to tell the defects apart is by asking which intermediate is stuck behind the block.

Why arginine is the signal

It looks strange at first that a product of the cycle should stimulate its first step — feedback is usually inhibitory. But arginine is not the end product; urea is. Arginine is the last intermediate before the exit, so arginine rising means substrate is queueing inside the loop, which means nitrogen is arriving faster than it is leaving. Turning up CPS I is the correct response. Meanwhile the two substrates of NAG synthase, glutamate and , are precisely the molecules that accumulate when amino acids are being catabolised in quantity. So NAG concentration is a direct, chemically honest readout of "there is a lot of nitrogen about", and CPS I reads it. This is a good example of a general principle: allosteric signals in metabolism are usually molecules whose concentration already reports the thing being sensed, not dedicated messengers.

The orotic acid logic — learn this as a mechanism, not a fact

Block OTC and carbamoyl phosphate has nowhere to go. It builds up in the matrix, leaks into the cytosol, and there it meets the pyrimidine pathway, which uses carbamoyl phosphate as its own first intermediate (§ 10). It is pushed straight through to orotate, which spills into the urine. So: hyperammonaemia + orotic aciduria = the block is at or below OTC. Block CPS I instead and there is no carbamoyl phosphate to leak, so orotate is low. That one contrast separates the two commonest urea cycle defects using a single urine test, and it is exactly the sort of two-pathway link examiners like. The third member of the set — hereditary orotic aciduria from UMP synthase deficiency — has orotic aciduria with megaloblastic anaemia and no hyperammonaemia, because the lesion is inside pyrimidine synthesis itself.

The clinical shape is worth carrying because it recurs. Exam technique · the hyperammonaemic newborn, in three questions

  1. Blood gas first, before any biochemistry — respiratory alkalosis says urea cycle; a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis says organic acidaemia. One line of the stem, and two or three options are gone — which on Part II marking is exactly the threshold at which answering stops being worse than a blank (see § 10 for the arithmetic).
  2. Then urinary orotate — raised puts the block at or below OTC; low or normal puts it at CPS I or NAG synthase, which have no carbamoyl phosphate to spill.
  3.   4. Check for the impostor before you commit — orotic aciduria with megaloblastic anaemia and normal ammonia is hereditary orotic aciduria (UMP synthase), not a urea cycle defect at all. The anaemia is the giveaway, and it is planted in option lists precisely because it shares the orotate.
  5. Use sex and family history as a free prior — OTC is the only X-linked defect and the commonest. An affected boy, or a pedigree with affected males and a mildly affected mother, points there before you have read a single laboratory value.
  6. Where the time goes — these vignettes are long but they resolve on two numbers. Read the gas and the orotate, answer, and move on; re-reading the social history costs you a question elsewhere.

Why phenylbutyrate beats benzoate on paper

Benzoate is conjugated with glycine → hippurate, which is excreted; that removes one nitrogen per molecule of drug. Phenylacetate (given orally as the prodrug phenylbutyrate) is conjugated with glutamine → phenylacetylglutamine; glutamine carries two nitrogens, so the same molar dose removes twice as much. Both work by creating an excretable nitrogen-containing molecule that the kidney will filter — they do not fix the enzyme, they build a bypass. That is the general principle behind treating a metabolic block: divert the substrate to a harmless, excretable derivative.

§ 07

The ideaStrip the nitrogen off an amino acid and what is left is an ordinary carbon compound that has to be fed into metabolism somewhere. There are only seven places it can go, and which one it goes to decides everything. Land in the part of the map that can be run backwards to sugar, and that amino acid can keep your blood glucose up when you are starving. Land in the part that cannot — because one particular reaction in the body is irreversible — and the carbon can only become fat or ketone bodies. That single irreversible step is the whole reason the classification exists.

Derive it rather than memorise it

The question "is this amino acid glucogenic?" is really the question "does its skeleton give a net increase in oxaloacetate?" Everything that arrives as pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate or oxaloacetate does, because all of those can be carried round to oxaloacetate and then pulled out by PEPCK. So the classification is a statement about anaplerosis, and if you can say which of the seven doorways an amino acid uses, you never need the list.

Two consequences follow immediately and both are examinable. First, the odd-chain fatty acid story is the same story: propionyl-CoA is a three-carbon unit that becomes succinyl-CoA and is therefore glucogenic, which is why odd-chain fatty acids are the exception to "fat cannot become sugar". Where this appears on the paper

Rarely as "list the ketogenic amino acids". Usually as an isotope-labelling question — "a rat is given 14C-labelled leucine; can it be used to make glucose?" (no net glucose, because its skeleton arrives only as acetyl-CoA and acetoacetyl-CoA — but see the caveat at the end of this box before answering a stem phrased in terms of individual labelled atoms) — or embedded in a starvation-physiology stem, or as part of a vitamin question about propionyl-CoA. The version that catches people is "can label from acetyl-CoA ever appear in glucose?": strictly, individual carbon atoms can, because acetyl-CoA carbons scramble into the cycle's four-carbon intermediates, but there is no net conversion. If the option says "no net synthesis of glucose from acetyl-CoA", it is right; if it says "no carbon atom from acetyl-CoA can ever be found in glucose", it is wrong.

The ideaEach of these diseases is one missing enzyme in a pathway you have just learnt, and each of them makes the same argument: when a step is blocked, the substrate backs up, spills into whatever side reaction can take it, and the products of that side reaction are what you see, smell or measure. The product downstream of the block is missing, so anything the body needed it for must now be supplied in the diet. Every treatment is one of three moves — take the substrate out of the diet, put the missing product back, or open a bypass.

It says nothing about which BCAA is the neurotoxic one — that is leucine, and it has to be held separately.

PKU, and why the pathway matters more than the name

Phenylalanine hydroxylase converts phenylalanine to tyrosine. Block it and three things follow, and you can derive all of them. (1) Phenylalanine accumulates and is transaminated into the phenylketones — phenylpyruvate and its relatives — which is what "phenylketonuria" refers to and what gives the musty, mousy odour. (2) Phenylalanine competes with the other large neutral amino acids for the LAT1 transporter at the blood–brain barrier, starving the brain of tyrosine and tryptophan; that competition, more than direct toxicity, is what damages the developing brain. (3) Tyrosine can no longer be made, so it becomes a dietary essential, and everything downstream of tyrosine is short — including melanin, which is why untreated children are fair-haired and pale-skinned.

The 2 % of cases caused by defects in BH₄ rather than in PAH itself are the ones that matter conceptually, because BH₄ serves three hydroxylases: phenylalanine hydroxylase, tyrosine hydroxylase (making L-DOPA and thence catecholamines) and tryptophan hydroxylase (making serotonin). So-called malignant PKU therefore adds a neurotransmitter deficiency that a low-phenylalanine diet cannot touch — the child needs BH₄, L-DOPA and 5-hydroxytryptophan. If a stem describes a screen-positive infant who deteriorates despite good dietary control, that is the answer.

Worked example 4 — reading a newborn screening result in two unit systems

A newborn screening laboratory reports blood phenylalanine as 20 mg/dL, with a cutoff for referral of 2 mg/dL and a normal of about 1 mg/dL. Phenylalanine has M = 165.2 g/mol. Express these in µmol/L, and say what the numbers imply.

  1. Conversion: 1 mg/dL = 10 mg/L, so 10 / 165.2 = 0.0605 mmol/L = 60.5 µmol/L. Useful to know as a single factor: 1 mg/dL Phe ≈ 60 µmol/L.
  2. Cutoff: 2 × 60.5 = 121 µmol/L. Normal: ≈60 µmol/L.
  3. This infant: 20 × 60.5 ≈ 1210 µmol/L — about 1210 / 60 = 20-fold the normal concentration. That is the classical PKU range (conventionally >1200 µmol/L, or >20 mg/dL).
  4. Why the timing of the sample matters: the placenta cleared phenylalanine in utero, so a sample taken before feeding has begun can be falsely normal. Screening is done at 24–72 hours after milk feeding is established, for the same reason a urea cycle defect declares itself only after feeding.
  5. The therapeutic target is usually 120–360 µmol/L, i.e. 2–6 mg/dL — deliberately not zero, because phenylalanine is an essential amino acid and a diet containing none of it is lethal. This is the general rule for every one of these diseases: restrict, never abolish.

Four pairs that are constantly confused

Homocystinuria vs Marfan syndrome: both marfanoid, both with dislocated lenses — but homocystinuria dislocates the lens downwards and adds thrombosis and intellectual disability, while Marfan dislocates it upwards and adds aortic root dilatation. Homocystinuria vs cystinuria: completely unrelated — cystinuria is a defect of the renal tubular transporter for the dibasic amino acids (COLA: cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine), producing hexagonal crystals and stones, with no systemic metabolic disease. Alkaptonuria vs porphyria: both darken urine on standing, but alkaptonuria is painless in childhood and gives ochronosis. Tyrosinaemia type I vs alkaptonuria: both are in the tyrosine pathway, but tyrosinaemia I (fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase) causes liver failure and renal Fanconi syndrome and is treated with nitisinone, which blocks 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase higher up so that the toxic fumarylacetoacetate is never formed — deliberately converting a lethal disease into a chemically induced tyrosinaemia type III, which is comparatively benign. (Note the direction: nitisinone acts above homogentisate, so it lowers homogentisate rather than causing alkaptonuria — which is exactly why the same drug is also used in alkaptonuria.)

§ 09

One-carbon metabolism, SAM and the folate trap

The ideaSome jobs need a single carbon atom moved from one molecule to another — building the ring of a nucleotide, putting a methyl group onto DNA. A bare carbon fragment cannot be allowed to float around, so the cell keeps one on a carrier and hands it over directly. There are two such carriers, and the difference between them is the whole section: one is a general handler that can carry the carbon at three different levels of oxidation, and the other carries it only in the most reduced form but hands it over far more forcefully. Between them sits a single reaction that is one-way — and because it is one-way, a vitamin deficiency two steps downstream can strand the entire folate supply in a form nothing can use.

Definition & the two carriers

Tetrahydrofolate (THF)
The general one-carbon carrier, made from dietary folate by dihydrofolate reductase acting twice (folate → DHF → THF, both using NADPH). It carries the carbon on N5, on N10, or bridging both, at three oxidation levels: methyl (most reduced), methylene, and formyl/methenyl (most oxidised).

Where the carbons come from and where they go

The dominant source is serine: serine hydroxymethyltransferase, another PLP enzyme, takes the β-carbon off serine to give glycine and loads it onto as N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF. Smaller contributions come from the glycine cleavage system, from formate, and from histidine (whose intermediate FIGLU accumulates in folate deficiency — the basis of the old FIGLU excretion test).

The three destinations are worth memorising as a set, because each is a drug target:

  • N¹⁰-formyl-THF → purine C2 and C8. Two of the nine ring atoms of every purine come from folate (§ 10).
  • N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF → dTMP, by thymidylate synthase. This is the reaction that makes DNA's thymine, and it is unique: the folate is not merely a carrier here, it is also the reductant, so it leaves oxidised as DHF and must be reduced back by DHFR before it can be used again. That is why blocking DHFR starves the cell of thymidylate specifically.
  • N⁵-methyl-THF → methionine, by methionine synthase, which requires methylcobalamin. This is the only reaction in human metabolism that consumes N⁵-methyl-THF.

Three oxidation levels, three destinations — in the right pairing

Methyl makes Methionine · Methylene methylates dUMP · Formyl forms the purine ring

Our own line rather than a circulating hook, but it holds the one pairing people invert, and two thirds of it check themselves — the donor names its own product. Methyl makes methionine: N⁵-methyl-THF, the most reduced form, hands its carbon to homocysteine at methionine synthase (B₁₂), the sole exit from that form. Methylene methylates dUMP: N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF is the substrate of thymidylate synthase, and the carbon it delivers ends up as the methyl of dTMP — the folate itself supplying the reduction, which is why it leaves as DHF. Formyl forms the ring: N¹⁰-formyl-THF, the most oxidised form, puts in purine C2 and C8. Read left to right and you are also reading the oxidation ladder from most reduced to most oxidised. If the phrase goes, derive it from oxidation level: a methyl transfer needs a carbon already at the methyl level, a purine ring carbon flanked by two nitrogens needs one at the formate level, and thymine's methyl group needs the level in between plus a reduction — which is exactly why the folate leaves that reaction oxidised as DHF and why DHFR is a drug target at all. Attach the drugs to the same three lines: methotrexate stops DHF returning to THF, so both the dTMP and the purine arms fail; 5-FU hits the middle line only.

The folate trap, and the one test that separates the two vitamins

Now put the mechanism to work. B₁₂ has exactly two jobs in human metabolism:

  1. As methylcobalamin, cofactor of methionine synthase — the folate door.
  2. As adenosylcobalamin, cofactor of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase — the step that converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA on the propionyl-CoA route of § 07.

Lose B₁₂ and both fail. The first failure produces a megaloblastic anaemia indistinguishable from folate deficiency, because it is functionally a folate deficiency: dividing cells cannot make dTMP, DNA replication stalls while RNA and protein synthesis carry on, and the result is a large cell with an immature nucleus. The second failure allows methylmalonyl-CoA to accumulate; it is thought to disturb the synthesis of myelin lipids, and it produces the subacute combined degeneration of the posterior and lateral columns.

Hence the clinically decisive point. Give a B₁₂-deficient patient large doses of folic acid and you bypass the trap — enough folate enters the pool as reduced forms other than N⁵-methyl-THF that thymidylate synthesis restarts, and the anaemia corrects. But nothing you have done touches methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, so the neurological damage continues, now without its warning sign. That is why folate is not given blind for megaloblastic anaemia, why fortified flour was debated, and why the laboratory discriminator is methylmalonic acid: raised in B₁₂ deficiency, normal in folate deficiency, while homocysteine is raised in both.

Go deeper · the transsulfuration arm, and why homocysteine is measured at all

Homocysteine sits at a genuine three-way junction, and each branch has a vitamin attached — which is the real reason a raised homocysteine is a useful screening result rather than merely a risk marker.

  • Remethylation by methionine synthase — needs B₁₂ and N⁵-methyl-THF (i.e. folate). Deficiency of either raises homocysteine.
  • Remethylation by betaine–homocysteine methyltransferase — liver and kidney only, uses betaine (from choline) as the methyl donor and needs no vitamin at all. This is why betaine is a treatment for homocystinuria: it opens a vitamin-independent exit.
  • Transsulfuration — cystathionine β-synthase then cystathionine γ-lyase, both PLP (vitamin B₆) enzymes, converting homocysteine irreversibly to cysteine plus α-ketobutyrate. This is the only route by which the sulfur of methionine becomes cysteine, and it is why cysteine is conditionally essential when methionine is short.

Read the pattern backwards and you have a diagnostic algorithm. Homocysteine raised with high methionine points to a block below methionine, i.e. classical CBS deficiency. Homocysteine raised with low or normal methionine points to a block in remethylation — B₁₂ deficiency, methionine synthase deficiency, or severe MTHFR deficiency. The common C677T MTHFR polymorphism produces a thermolabile enzyme and a mildly raised homocysteine, especially when folate intake is low; it is a textbook example of a gene–nutrient interaction rather than a disease.

One more mechanistic thread worth pulling: SAH is a product inhibitor of almost every SAM-dependent methyltransferase. So anything that raises homocysteine also raises SAH (the hydrolase reaction is readily reversible) and therefore inhibits methylation globally — which is a plausible link between hyperhomocysteinaemia and vascular disease, and the reason nitrous oxide, which irreversibly oxidises the cobalt of methylcobalamin, can precipitate a myeloneuropathy in someone with marginal B₁₂ stores after a single long anaesthetic.

Antifolates, and why each is selective

Methotrexate inhibits human DHFR with picomolar affinity, so THF cannot be regenerated after thymidylate synthase has oxidised it — the cell dies of "thymineless death", and the selectivity is only that tumour cells divide faster. Leucovorin (folinic acid, N⁵-formyl-THF) rescues normal tissue because it is already reduced and does not need DHFR. Trimethoprim inhibits bacterial DHFR some tens of thousands of times more tightly than the human enzyme, and pyrimethamine does the same for protozoal DHFR — same target, selectivity from the enzyme's sequence. Sulfonamides act one step earlier still, on dihydropteroate synthase, an enzyme humans do not possess at all because we absorb folate rather than synthesise it: that is the cleanest selective toxicity in the whole of chemotherapy. Note the trap in the other direction — with 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin does not rescue, it potentiates, because the folate is part of the inhibitory complex (§ 10).

§ 10

Building nucleotides: de novo, salvage and the drugs

The ideaNow the argument runs the other way. Nitrogen stops being waste and becomes the most expensive material the cell buys: a single purine ring costs four nitrogen atoms, two folate-carried carbons and a handful of ATP. Because it is so expensive, there are two routes — build the ring from scratch, or pick up a discarded base and re-attach it to a sugar for a fraction of the price — and the cell prefers the cheap one whenever it can. And because a dividing cell cannot divide without dNTPs, this is the pathway that almost every cancer drug, immunosuppressant and antiviral attacks. The two rings are built in opposite orders, and that single structural fact explains most of what follows.

Definition & the two strategies

Purine vs pyrimidine construction

Regulation: three layers, all of them worth knowing

(1) The committed step. Glutamine–PRPP amidotransferase is inhibited by the end products IMP, AMP and GMP, and activated by its substrate PRPP. Classic feedback. (2) The branch point. IMP sits at a fork: adenylosuccinate synthetase takes it to AMP, IMP dehydrogenase takes it to GMP. AMP inhibits the first branch and GMP inhibits the second, so each product limits its own production. (3) The cross-regulation, which is the elegant part and the one examiners like: making AMP from IMP consumes , while making GMP from IMP consumes ATP. Each branch is therefore driven by the other nucleotide. If adenine nucleotides are abundant, GMP synthesis is favoured; if guanine nucleotides are abundant, AMP synthesis is favoured. The pools self-balance without any dedicated sensor.

Go deeper · the pyrimidine pathway in full, and ribonucleotide reductase

Six steps take bicarbonate to UMP, and it is worth walking them because three drugs and two diseases sit on them.

  1. CPS II (cytosolic): glutamine + 2 ATP + HCO₃⁻ → carbamoyl phosphate. The regulated step in animals.
  2. Aspartate transcarbamoylase: + aspartate → carbamoyl aspartate. In E. coli this is the committed and allosterically regulated step — inhibited by CTP, activated by ATP — and it is the textbook example of allostery. In mammals it is simply a domain of CAD and the control has moved to CPS II. Both statements are true of different organisms, and options exploit the confusion.
  3. Dihydroorotase: ring closure. (Steps 1–3 are the three activities of CAD.)
  4. Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase — the one mitochondrial step, sitting in the inner membrane and passing its electrons to ubiquinone. So pyrimidine synthesis is physically wired to the respiratory chain, and this enzyme is the target of leflunomide and teriflunomide.
  5. Orotate phosphoribosyltransferase: orotate + PRPP → OMP. Only now does the sugar arrive.
  6. OMP decarboxylase → UMP. Steps 5 and 6 are the two activities of the bifunctional UMP synthase, whose deficiency is hereditary orotic aciduria — orotate in the urine, megaloblastic anaemia that does not respond to B₁₂ or folate, and a specific cure: oral uridine, which bypasses the block.

UMP is then phosphorylated to UTP, and CTP synthetase aminates UTP to CTP using glutamine and ATP. Note that cytosine, uniquely, is only ever made at the triphosphate level.

Ribonucleotide reductase is the single gateway to DNA precursors: it reduces the 2′-OH of nucleoside diphosphates using a radical mechanism, with electrons supplied by thioredoxin → thioredoxin reductase → NADPH. It has two allosteric sites. The activity site is switched on by ATP and off by dATP; the specificity site reads the current dNTP and chooses which substrate is accepted next, which is how the four dNTP pools are kept balanced. That dATP switch is not a curiosity: in adenosine deaminase deficiency (§ 11) dATP accumulates, shuts down the reductase, and lymphocytes — which are the most dependent on it — die, giving severe combined immunodeficiency. Hydroxyurea inhibits the same enzyme by quenching its tyrosyl radical.

Exam technique · drug-and-target option sets

  1. Find the swap first — methotrexate → DHFR and 5-fluorouracil → thymidylate synthase. Crossing those two is the commonest single distractor on this material, and spotting it removes an option in two seconds without solving anything. The other habitual swap is hydroxyurea (ribonucleotide reductase) with allopurinol (xanthine oxidase).
  2. Then check the rescue direction — leucovorin rescues methotrexate, because it is already-reduced folate and needs no DHFR; it potentiates 5-FU, because the folate is part of the covalent complex that traps the enzyme. Any option that has leucovorin rescuing 5-FU is wrong, and it is a sentence that sounds entirely reasonable.
  3. Recognise the drug from the cell type in the stem — selective for lymphocytes → mycophenolate (IMP dehydrogenase) or leflunomide (dihydroorotate dehydrogenase); empties all four dNTP pools and raises HbF → hydroxyurea; needs the pathogen's own kinase to become active → acyclovir; needs the patient's salvage enzyme HGPRT to become active → 6-mercaptopurine.
  4. Two drug pairs are questions in themselves — allopurinol blocks the xanthine oxidase that inactivates 6-mercaptopurine, so co-prescription raises 6-MP toxicity; and DPD deficiency plus 5-FU is catastrophic. If a stem troubles to name both drugs, that interaction is the answer.
  5. Use the two-ring rule as a free filter — a drug named as blocking a purine step cannot be the answer to a stem about thymidine or pyrimidines, and vice versa. Sort the options into "purine", "pyrimidine" and "folate" before you read them closely; usually one column has a single member and it is either obviously right or obviously planted.
  6. Know the actual break-even before you guess — biochemistry is Part II, where a wrong answer costs 0.5. With all five options live the expected value of a blind guess is −0.2; with four left it is still −0.125; with three left it is exactly 0; with two left it is +0.25. So the working rule on this paper is eliminate two options and it is worth answering, eliminate three and you should never leave it blank — one elimination is not enough here, which is the opposite of the Part I biology rule where a five-way guess is already break-even. Steps 1 and 2 above usually get you two.
Worked example 5 — reading an isotope-labelling experiment on the purine ring

Cultured cells are supplied with (a) glutamine labelled with 15N in its amide nitrogen, (b) glycine labelled with 14C in its carboxyl carbon, and (c) 15N-aspartate. Where does each label appear in newly made adenine nucleotides, and how many labelled nitrogens does a single AMP end up with in experiment (a)?

  1. (a) Glutamine's amide nitrogen is donated twice during ring construction: at the committed step (becoming N9) and at the FGAM synthetase step (becoming N3). So both N3 and N9 are labelled — two ring nitrogens.
  2. But adenine has an exocyclic 6-amino group as well, and that comes from aspartate at the adenylosuccinate step, not from glutamine. So AMP carries 2 labelled nitrogens out of 5 total. (GMP would carry 3, because its 2-amino group is added by GMP synthetase using glutamine.)
  3. (b) Glycine enters intact and contributes C4, C5 and N7. Its carboxyl carbon becomes C4; its α-carbon becomes C5. So only C4 is labelled.
  4. (c) Aspartate donates N1 of the ring. In adenine nucleotides it additionally donates the 6-amino nitrogen, so an AMP has two aspartate-derived nitrogens, whereas an IMP or a GMP ring has only one.
  5. Cross-check the total: purine ring nitrogens = 4. Glutamine 2 (N3, N9) + aspartate 1 (N1) + glycine 1 (N7) = 4 ✓. Ring carbons = 5. Glycine 2 (C4, C5) + folate 2 (C2, C8) + CO₂ 1 (C6) = 5 ✓.
(a) N3 + N9 → 2 labelled N in AMP, 3 in GMP · (b) C4 only · (c) N1, plus the 6-NH₂ of adenine

The three orderings people reverse

Purines: ring built on the sugar. Pyrimidines: ring built first, sugar later. Get that the wrong way round and half a dozen downstream facts invert — including why orotate (a free ring, no sugar) is the metabolite that spills in OTC deficiency, and why PRPP shortage hits purine synthesis at the very first step but pyrimidine synthesis only at step 5. Second reversal: CPS I uses ammonia and is mitochondrial; CPS II uses glutamine and is cytosolic. Third: ribonucleotide reductase works on diphosphates (NDP → dNDP), not triphosphates, and dTMP is made by methylating dUMP — thymine is never reduced from a ribonucleotide of its own, because the pathway never makes a thymine ribonucleotide to reduce.

§ 11

Purine catabolism, uric acid and gout

The ideaTaking a purine apart runs into the same problem as building it: the ring is stable and full of nitrogen, and the body cannot open it. Most mammals oxidise the finished waste product one step further into something freely soluble; humans lost that enzyme, so our end product is a compound that is barely soluble at body temperature and pH. Everything about gout — why it appears in cool peripheral joints, why crystals form at all, why some drugs help and others make it worse — follows from a solubility limit that we sit uncomfortably close to all the time. Pyrimidines, by contrast, break down into small soluble molecules and cause no disease at all, which is the cleanest possible proof that the problem is chemical, not biological.

Gout is a solubility problem before it is an inflammatory one

Plasma is saturated with monosodium urate at about 6.8 mg/dL, and a great many normal adults sit within a milligram of that. Two things then decide whether crystals form. Temperature: urate is less soluble when cooler, and the first metatarsophalangeal joint is one of the coolest structures in the body — which is why the classic first attack is podagra in the big toe, and why attacks often begin at night. pH: in the kidney the fluid is acidic, so the species present is un-ionised uric acid, which is much less soluble than the urate salt — hence uric acid stones, and hence the point of alkalinising the urine. The inflammation itself comes afterwards: crystals are engulfed by macrophages, activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, release IL-1β and recruit neutrophils. That is why colchicine (which disrupts microtubules and blocks neutrophil migration and inflammasome assembly) treats the attack but does nothing at all to the urate.

Worked example 6 — why urine pH matters more than urate concentration for stones

A patient forms recurrent radiolucent renal stones and has a persistent urine pH of 5.0. Uric acid has pKa ≈ 5.5, and it is the un-ionised acid that is poorly soluble. What fraction of the urinary urate is in that form, and what does alkalinising the urine to pH 6.5 achieve?

  1. Henderson–Hasselbalch: [urate⁻]/[uric acid] = 10(pH − pKa).
  2. At pH 5.0: 10−0.5 = 0.316, so the un-ionised fraction is 1/(1 + 0.316) = 76.0 %. Three quarters of the urate in this urine is in the form that will not dissolve.
  3. At pH 6.5: 10+1.0 = 10, so the un-ionised fraction is 1/11 = 9.1 %.
  4. Fold change: 76.0 / 9.1 = 8.4. Raising the urine pH by 1.5 units cuts the poorly soluble species more than eight-fold — without changing the amount of urate excreted by a single milligram.
  5. Compare what you could achieve by lowering production: a good response to allopurinol might cut urate excretion by a third. Alkalinisation is roughly an order of magnitude more effective for stones — which is why potassium citrate is first-line there, while allopurinol is first-line for tophaceous gout, where the target is the plasma concentration and the joint pH is fixed at 7.4.
  6. Sanity check the other direction: at blood pH 7.4 the un-ionised fraction is only 1/(1 + 79.4) = 1.2 %. In blood essentially everything is the urate anion, so the relevant solubility is that of monosodium urate, ≈6.8 mg/dL — a completely different number from the solubility of uric acid in urine. Both facts are true; they apply at different pH values.

Complete HGPRT deficiency, X-linked recessive. Two consequences follow from one missing enzyme, and both push the same way. (1) Hypoxanthine and guanine cannot be salvaged, so they are shunted to xanthine oxidase → massive urate overproduction: orange crystal "sand" in the nappy, urate stones, gouty arthritis in a child. (2) Salvage normally consumes PRPP and produces IMP and GMP, both of which inhibit the committed step of de novo synthesis. With HGPRT gone, PRPP accumulates and the feedback inhibitors are not made — so de novo purine synthesis runs at full speed, feeding still more purine into catabolism. The neurological picture — choreoathetosis, spasticity, intellectual disability and the characteristic compulsive self-mutilation — is not caused by urate, which is why allopurinol treats the stones and the gout but not the behaviour; the brain depends heavily on salvage and shows a dopaminergic deficit in the basal ganglia. Partial HGPRT deficiency gives Kelley–Seegmiller syndrome: severe early gout, no neurological disease.

Go deeper · pyrimidine catabolism, the purine nucleotide cycle, and two immunodeficiencies

Why there is no pyrimidine gout. Pyrimidine rings are opened and degraded to β-alanine (from cytosine and uracil) or β-aminoisobutyrate (from thymine), plus NH₃ and CO₂. Every one of those is freely soluble, so there is no crystal disease to have. The first enzyme, dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), matters clinically for a different reason: it is also the enzyme that clears 5-fluorouracil, and partial DPD deficiency — present in roughly 3–5 % of people — causes life-threatening toxicity from a standard dose. It is one of the few pharmacogenetic tests now used routinely before chemotherapy.

The purine nucleotide cycle. Working skeletal muscle runs a small loop: AMP deaminase converts AMP to IMP and free NH₃; adenylosuccinate synthetase and lyase then rebuild AMP from IMP using aspartate, releasing fumarate. Net effect: aspartate → fumarate + NH₃. It is an anaplerotic route that tops up the citric acid cycle during exercise and disposes of amino nitrogen without a urea cycle. Inherited myoadenylate deaminase deficiency causes exercise-induced cramps and myalgia with a flat ammonia response to the ischaemic forearm test.

Two enzymes of this pathway are immunological. Adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency causes about 15 % of severe combined immunodeficiency: deoxyadenosine accumulates, is phosphorylated to dATP, and dATP shuts off ribonucleotide reductase, so no cell can make DNA — but lymphocytes, which must undergo enormous clonal expansion, are killed first. It was the target of the first approved human gene therapy. PNP deficiency does the analogous thing with dGTP and produces a T-cell deficiency with relatively preserved B cells. Both are, at bottom, the same lesson as gout: the danger in purine metabolism is almost never the pathway itself but the accumulation of an intermediate the cell has no way to dispose of.

Crystals under polarised light — the one comparison worth memorising

Gout: monosodium urate, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent — yellow when the crystal lies parallel to the axis of the red compensator. Pseudogout: calcium pyrophosphate, rhomboid, positively birefringent — blue when parallel. Two mnemonics that survive contact with an exam: "needle = negative", and ABC — Aligned Blue is Calcium. Note also what a normal serum urate does not tell you: urate often falls during an acute attack, so a normal value never excludes gout, and only crystals in the joint fluid confirm it.
