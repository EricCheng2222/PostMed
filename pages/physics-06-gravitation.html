Physics · Note 06

Gravitation and Orbital Motion

Every result in this chapter comes out of one line: F = GMm/r², an attraction that falls off as the square of the distance. Integrate that force once and you get an energy that falls off as 1/r and carries a minus sign — and from those two facts alone follow g at the surface, escape speed, the radius of the geostationary ring, all three of Kepler's laws, and the reason an astronaut floats even though gravity there is still 89 % of what it is on the ground. Do not memorise nine formulas. Memorise one force law, one energy, and the habit of asking which mass, measured from which centre, at what distance.

§ 01

Newton's law of universal gravitation

The ideaEvery lump of matter pulls on every other lump of matter. Always pulling, never pushing, and always both ways round — you tug on the Earth exactly as hard as it tugs on you. The pull gets stronger the more matter is involved, and it dies away quickly as the two things move apart: go twice as far away and the pull drops to a quarter, not a half. That one rule, applied over and over to bigger and bigger things, is the whole of this note.

Definition & formula

Universal gravitation
An always-attractive force that every particle of matter exerts on every other particle, proportional to the product of the two masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
F = G · M mr² point masses, or spherically symmetric bodies (§2); r is centre-to-centre
Full definition, symbols and units
Gravitational constant G
The universal proportionality constant that converts "kilograms per metre squared" into newtons — the same number for every pair of masses anywhere in the universe.
G = 6.674×10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻² ≈ 6.67×10⁻¹¹measured, not derived
Fnet = Σ Fisuperposition: add the forces as vectors, one source at a time
Fgravitational force magnitude (N)
Ggravitational constant (N·m²·kg⁻²)
M, mthe two masses (kg)
rseparation of the centres (m)

Three features of this law do all the work later, so read them as claims rather than decoration.

(1) It is always attractive, and it is a mutual pair. The Earth pulls you with 686 N if you have a mass of 70 kg; you pull the Earth with 686 N as well. Newton's third law is built in — the formula is symmetric in M and m. The reason the Earth does not visibly move is a = F/m, not any asymmetry in the force.

(2) It is inverse-square. Double the separation and the force drops to a quarter, not a half; triple it and the force drops to a ninth. The exponent is exactly 2 — not 1.99 — and that exactness is what makes §2, §5 and §9 come out clean. Physically the exponent is 2 because the "influence" spreads over the surface of a sphere, and a sphere's area grows as r².

(3) It obeys superposition. Gravity from several bodies is the vector sum of the individual forces; each source contributes as though the others were absent. This is why you can compute the pull of a whole planet by adding up the pull of its pieces, and why the force of the Moon on you and the force of the Earth on you never "interfere".

Trap: r is measured from the centre, not from the ground

A satellite "at an altitude of 400 km" is at r = 6.37×10⁶ + 4.00×10⁵ = 6.77×10⁶ m from Earth's centre, not 4.00×10⁵ m. Every question that gives you an altitude is testing whether you add the planetary radius. And r is a distance between centres, never a surface-to-surface gap.

Go deeper · how weak gravity really is, and why G is the worst-measured constant

G is the weakest-acting and worst-measured of the fundamental constants — Cavendish first pinned it down in 1798 with a torsion balance, and after two more centuries we still know it only to about two parts in 10⁵; e and h, by contrast, are now exact by definition in the SI, while G still has to be weighed. The reason is the same reason gravity feels irrelevant in a laboratory: two 70 kg people standing 1 m apart attract each other with about 3×10⁻⁷ N. Between two protons, the electric repulsion beats the gravitational attraction by a factor of roughly 1.2×10³⁶. Gravity only dominates on astronomical scales because it has no negative charge to cancel it: pile up 10²⁴ kg of matter and every kilogram adds.

Exam point · scaling questions

Most gravitation questions are ratio questions in disguise. "A planet has twice Earth's mass and three times its radius — what is g?" needs no constants: g ∝ M/R², so g = 9.81 × 2/9 = 2.18 m·s⁻². Learn which power each quantity carries: F, g ∝ 1/r²; U, V ∝ 1/r; vorbit, vesc ∝ 1/√r; T ∝ r3/2; tidal stretch ∝ 1/r³.

§ 02

The shell theorem: why a sphere is a point

The ideaA planet is not a dot, yet we cheerfully treat it as one. The licence to do that is a small miracle of arithmetic: for anything shaped like a ball, the pull from every scrap of it adds up to exactly what you would get if the whole ball were squashed into a single point at the centre. There is a second half that is stranger still. Burrow inside, and every layer of rock further out than you stops counting — not counting a little, counting not at all. Only what is below your feet is left.

Definition & formula

Shell theorem
Two exact results about a thin, uniform spherical shell of mass: (a) at any external point it pulls exactly as if its whole mass sat at its centre; (b) at any internal point it pulls with exactly zero net force.
g(r) = G·Menc(r)r² any spherically symmetric mass distribution
Full definition, symbols and units
Enclosed mass
For a spherically symmetric body, the gravity at radius r is produced by the mass inside radius r only; everything outside that radius contributes nothing.
r ≥ R: Menc = M ⟹ g = GM/r²outside — behaves as a point mass
r < R (uniform ρ): Menc = M(r/R)³ ⟹ g = GMrR³inside a sphere of constant density
Mencmass enclosed within radius r (kg)
Rradius of the body (m)
rdistance from the centre (m)
ρdensity (kg·m⁻³)
ggravitational field strength (N·kg⁻¹ = m·s⁻²)

Newton wrote F = GMm/r² for point masses. The Earth is not a point; it is 6 371 km of rock spread over your head, under your feet and to every side. Why is it legitimate to treat the whole thing as a single dot at the centre 6 371 km below you? That is the shell theorem, and Newton reportedly sat on the Principia for years until he could prove it.

Go deeper · the double-cone proof that a shell pulls you nowhere

The interior result is the more surprising one, and there is a clean argument for it. Stand somewhere inside a uniform shell and look out along a narrow double cone in opposite directions. The cone cuts a small patch of shell at distance r₁ on one side and a larger patch at distance r₂ on the other. Patch area grows as the square of the distance, so the far patch has (r₂/r₁)² times more mass — but its pull is weaker by exactly (r₁/r₂)². The two cancel precisely. Sweep the cone over the whole shell and every direction cancels: net force zero. Notice that this cancellation only works because the force exponent is exactly 2. With 1/r³ gravity you would feel a net pull towards the nearest wall.

A tunnel through the Earth is a spring

For a uniform sphere, g = (GM/R³)·r — a restoring force strictly proportional to displacement. That is Hooke's law, so an object dropped into a frictionless tunnel through the centre executes simple harmonic motion (note 07) with period T = 2π√(R³/GM) = 2π√(R/g₀) ≈ 84.4 min. Remarkably, that is the same number as the period of a satellite skimming the surface — the same √(R³/GM) appears in both.

Trap: "the shells above you pull you up"

Going down a mine, students often reason that the rock now overhead pulls upward and reduces your weight. The correct statement is stronger and simpler: the shell of matter outside your radius exerts exactly zero net force — not a small upward one. Only Menc matters. Equally, at the exact centre of a planet g = 0, but U is at its minimum (most negative), not zero.

§ 03

g at the surface — and why it is not constant

The ideaThe 9.8 you have used since school is not a law of nature. It is simply what the rule from §01 gives when you feed in this particular planet's amount of matter and this particular planet's size — and if either of those changes, the number changes with it. Climb a mountain, go down a mine, stand at the equator instead of the pole, or move to another planet, and it shifts. This section is about how much it shifts, and which of those effects are big enough to care about.

Definition & formula

Surface gravitational field g₀
The gravitational force per kilogram at the surface of a body — equivalently, the acceleration of a freely falling object there, once rotation is ignored.
g₀ = G MR² non-rotating, spherically symmetric body
Full definition, symbols and units

The famous 9.8 m·s⁻² is not a constant of nature. It is a derived quantity — one particular mass divided by one particular radius squared — and every term in it can change. Using G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹, ME = 5.97×10²⁴ kg and RE = 6.37×10⁶ m:

g₀ = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 5.97×10²⁴(6.37×10⁶)² = 3.98×10¹⁴4.06×10¹³ = 9.81 m·s⁻²GME = 3.98×10¹⁴ m³·s⁻² is worth memorising as one number

Read g₀ = GM/R² backwards and it becomes a weighing machine for planets: measure g and R at the surface, and M follows. That is the only practical way we know the mass of the Earth, and it is why Cavendish's torsion-balance measurement of G was described at the time as "weighing the world".

Worked example 1 · g at the altitude of the ISS

The International Space Station orbits at h ≈ 400 km. Find g there, and express it as a fraction of the surface value.

  1. Convert altitude to radius: r = R + h = 6.37×10⁶ + 4.00×10⁵ = 6.77×10⁶ m.
  2. Use the ratio form so no constants are needed: g = g₀ (R/r)².
  3. R/r = 6.37/6.77 = 0.9409; squaring, (R/r)² = 0.8853.
  4. g = 9.81 × 0.8853 = 8.68 m·s⁻².
  5. Sanity check with the crude linear rule 1 − 2h/R = 1 − 800/6370 = 0.874 — close to 0.885 but already 1 % off, because h/R = 0.063 is not really small.
g ≈ 8.68 m·s⁻² — about 89 % of the surface value

Hold on to this number. It is the single fact that kills the phrase "zero gravity" (§10).

Trap: the rotation term is not "a centrifugal force pushing you off"

In an inertial frame there is no outward force. What happens is that a person standing on the equator is moving in a circle of radius R once per day, so a slice of the true gravitational pull (magnitude ω²R = 0.034 m·s⁻²) is consumed as the net centripetal force and is therefore not available to press them onto the scale. The scale reads the remainder. That is why the effect is largest at the equator (full circle of radius R) and vanishes at the poles (radius zero), and why it goes as cos²λ rather than cosλ: one cosine for the smaller circle radius R cosλ, one for taking the component along the local vertical.

Exam point

"Going up" and "going down" are not symmetric. Rising 10 km costs you 2h/R ≈ 0.31 % of g; descending 10 km (uniform model) costs d/R ≈ 0.16 % — exactly half the rate, because outside you fight the square while inside you gain enclosed mass. Any question offering "the same" as an option is testing this.

§ 04

The gravitational field: force per unit mass

The ideaRather than asking how hard the Earth pulls one particular object, ask how hard it would pull any object placed here, per kilogram of it. That small change of question turns gravity from a relationship between two bodies into a property of the empty space around one: the Earth labels every point around it in advance, and whatever you drop in later simply reads off the local label. Doing the same for stored energy gives a number you can add up with ordinary arithmetic instead of with arrows, which is why it is worth the trouble.

Definition & formula

Gravitational field g
The gravitational force per unit mass that a source would exert on a small test mass placed at a point — a vector property of the point in space, independent of whether anything is there to feel it.
g = F/m = G Mr² (directed towards M) definition; the test mass m cancels out
Full definition, symbols and units
Gravitational potential V
The gravitational potential energy per unit mass at a point — a scalar, so fields from several sources can be added arithmetically rather than as vectors.
V = U/m = − G Mrscalar potential, zero at infinity
gnet = Σ gi and Vnet = Σ Visuperposition — vectors for g, plain sum for V
g = − dV/drthe field is the (negative) slope of the potential
gfield strength (N·kg⁻¹, identical to m·s⁻²)
Vgravitational potential (J·kg⁻¹)
Upotential energy of mass m in the field (J)
Msource mass (kg)
rdistance from the source centre (m)

Dividing out the test mass is not bookkeeping; it changes what the object is. F requires two masses and describes an interaction. g requires one mass and describes a condition of space: the Earth writes a field everywhere, and whatever you later drop into it experiences F = mg. This is the reason g has two apparently different units. N·kg⁻¹ is the field reading; m·s⁻² is what that field does to something free to move. They are dimensionally identical (1 N = 1 kg·m·s⁻²), and the numerical coincidence "field strength = free-fall acceleration" is exactly the statement that gravitational mass equals inertial mass — the equivalence principle, and the reason a feather and a hammer fall together on the Moon.

Worked example 2 · superposition: where does the Earth–Moon field vanish?

On the line joining Earth and Moon, find the point where the two fields cancel. Take ME = 5.97×10²⁴ kg, MM = 7.35×10²² kg, d = 3.84×10⁸ m between the centres.

  1. Let x be the distance from Earth's centre. The fields point in opposite directions there, so set the magnitudes equal: GME/x² = GMM/(d−x)².
  2. G cancels. Take the square root of both sides: √ME/x = √MM/(d−x), so (d−x)/x = √(MM/ME).
  3. MM/ME = 7.35×10²²/5.97×10²⁴ = 0.01231; √0.01231 = 0.1110.
  4. d/x = 1.1110, so x = 3.84×10⁸ / 1.1110 = 3.46×10⁸ m.
  5. That is 90 % of the way to the Moon — only 3.8×10⁴ km from the Moon's centre (3.7×10⁴ km above its surface). The Moon is 81 times lighter, and √81 = 9, so the null point sits 9 times closer to it.
x ≈ 3.46×10⁸ m from Earth's centre (90 % of the Earth–Moon distance)

Note this is not the L1 Lagrange point (≈3.26×10⁸ m). L1 is where the fields plus the rotating-frame term balance for a body co-orbiting with the Moon; here we only balanced the two static fields.

Exam point · when to use V instead of g

g is a vector: combining three sources means resolving components. V is a scalar: you just add signed numbers, and every term is negative. If a question asks for energy, work done or escape speed in a multi-body situation, go through V and never touch a component. A useful corollary: at the null point of Worked example 2 the field is zero but the potential is decidedly not zero — it is a maximum along that line (V ≈ −1.28×10⁶ J·kg⁻¹). Zero field never implies zero potential energy.

§ 05

Potential energy: the minus sign and the 1/r

The ideaLifting something away from a planet costs you effort, and letting it fall gives that effort back. This section is the bookkeeping for that stored effort, and two things about the bookkeeping catch people out. We agree to call the stored amount zero when the two bodies are infinitely far apart, which forces every real, finite situation to be a negative number — a hole you are sitting in and would have to pay to climb out of. And the stored amount fades with distance far more slowly than the pull does, so you can drift a very long way, feel almost nothing, and still be firmly trapped.

Definition & formula

Gravitational potential energy U
The work an external agent must do to bring the two masses from infinite separation to a separation r, moving them quasi-statically — a property of the pair, not of either body alone.
U = − G M mr U → 0 as r → ∞; valid at all r outside the bodies
Full definition, symbols and units
Reference (zero) point
The separation at which U is defined to be zero. For the 1/r law the only convenient choice is r → ∞, where the interaction genuinely vanishes.
ΔU = U₂ − U₁ = GMm (1/r₁ − 1/r₂)the only physically meaningful quantity
ΔU = mgh · R(R+h) ≈ mghnear-surface limit, h ≪ R
F = − dU/dr = − d(−GMm/r)/dr = − GMm/r²force is the negative slope of U; the minus means "attractive"
Ugravitational potential energy of the pair (J)
ΔUchange in potential energy (J)
hheight risen near the surface (m)
glocal surface field, 9.81 m·s⁻² (m·s⁻²)

Why the exponent drops from 2 to 1

Energy is force integrated over distance, and integrating a power always raises its exponent by one — here, ∫ r⁻² dr = −r⁻¹. So a 1/r² force must give a 1/r energy. This is not an extra fact to memorise; it is the same fact seen through an integral sign. It also tells you which one dies away faster: at ten Earth radii the force has fallen to 1 % of its surface value, but |U| is still 10 % — which is why you can be almost weightless and still very deeply bound.

Why the sign is negative

Set the zero at infinity, where two masses do not interact at all. Now let them approach. Gravity pulls them together, so gravity does positive work, and the potential energy of the pair must therefore fall below its starting value of zero. Every finite separation is a negative number. Equivalently: to separate them again you must supply energy, so any bound configuration sits in a hole. The minus sign is not a convention you could flip — given U(∞) = 0 and an attractive force, it is forced.

Why the reference is at infinity

Only ΔU is measurable, so the zero is ours to choose. But U = −GMm/r diverges to −∞ at r = 0, so "zero at the centre" is unusable, and "zero at the surface" would force a different formula for every planet. Infinity is the one place where the physics itself says "nothing is happening here", so it gives a single universal formula and makes the sign of U immediately meaningful: U < 0 ⟹ bound; U + K < 0 ⟹ cannot escape; E ≥ 0 ⟹ free.

How mgh falls out of it

Raise a mass from R to R + h:

ΔU = GMm(1/R − 1/(R+h)) = GMm·hR(R+h) = GMmR² · h · R(R+h) = mgh · R(R+h)exact

The factor R/(R+h) is the entire difference between the "school" formula and the exact one, and it tends to 1 when h ≪ R. Conceptually, mgh assumes the field does not weaken as you climb — true for a staircase, false for a rocket.

Worked example 3 · how wrong is mgh on Everest?

A 70 kg climber ascends from sea level to 8 850 m. Compare mgh with the exact ΔU. Take g = 9.81 m·s⁻², R = 6.37×10⁶ m.

  1. Approximate: mgh = 70 × 9.81 × 8 850 = 6.077×10⁶ J.
  2. Correction factor: R/(R+h) = 6.370×10⁶ / 6.3789×10⁶ = 0.99861.
  3. Exact: ΔU = 6.077×10⁶ × 0.99861 = 6.069×10⁶ J.
  4. Overestimate = 8.4×10³ J, i.e. 0.14 % — the same order as the 0.10 % you introduce by writing g = 9.8 instead of 9.81, and utterly swamped by the ~80 % of metabolic energy that is lost as heat.
  5. By contrast, at the ISS (h = 400 km) the factor is R/(R+h) = 0.941, so mgh would be 6 % wrong — and the naive mgh answer for lifting to infinity would be infinite instead of the correct GMm/R.
mgh = 6.08 MJ; exact = 6.07 MJ; error 0.14 %

Trap: "U is bigger closer in, because gravity is stronger"

No. As r decreases, U becomes more negative, i.e. smaller. Falling towards a planet loses potential energy and gains kinetic energy, exactly as at ground level. The confusion comes from comparing magnitudes instead of signed values: |U| grows, U shrinks. In an exam, "which point has the greatest potential energy?" means the least negative one — the point furthest from the mass.

§ 06

Escape speed

The ideaThrow a ball upwards and it comes back. Throw it harder and it comes back later. The question this section answers is whether there is a launch speed so great that the ball never comes back at all — and there is, because the pull weakens with distance quickly enough that a big enough head start can outrun it forever. What is surprising is that the answer does not depend at all on how heavy the thrown thing is, nor on which direction you aim it.

Definition & formula

Escape speed
The minimum launch speed at which a projectile, given no further propulsion and meeting no resistance, will never fall back — arriving at infinite distance with exactly zero speed left.
vesc = 2 G MR = 2 g₀ R from the surface of a body of mass M, radius R; no atmosphere, no thrust
Full definition, symbols and units
½ m v² − GMmR = 0the defining condition: total energy exactly zero
vesc = √2 · vcircat the same radius — always a factor of 1.414
vescescape speed (m·s⁻¹)
vcirccircular-orbit speed at the same radius (m·s⁻¹)
Mmass of the body escaped from (kg)
Rlaunch radius from the centre (m)
g₀field strength at that radius (m·s⁻²)

The derivation is one line of energy conservation. "Just barely escapes" means arriving at r = ∞ with v = 0, and both terms of the energy are zero there, so the total energy must have been zero at launch too: ½mv² + (−GMm/R) = 0, giving v = √(2GM/R).

The escaping mass m appears in both terms and cancels. Kinetic energy is proportional to m; potential energy is proportional to m; the condition that they sum to zero is therefore a statement about speed alone. A bacterium, a person and a battleship all need 11.2 km·s⁻¹ from Earth's surface. (What differs enormously is the energy — ½mv² — which is why launching a battleship is not a realistic proposition.) This is the same cancellation that makes all objects fall at the same rate: gravitational effects on a body's motion never depend on its own mass.

Worked example 4 · escape speed from Earth and from the Moon
  1. Earth. v = √(2GM/R) = √(2 × 3.98×10¹⁴ / 6.37×10⁶).
  2. Inside the root: 7.96×10¹⁴ / 6.37×10⁶ = 1.250×10⁸ m²·s⁻².
  3. v = √(1.250×10⁸) = 1.118×10⁴ m·s⁻¹ = 11.2 km·s⁻¹.
  4. Cross-check with √(2g₀R) = √(2 × 9.81 × 6.37×10⁶) = √(1.250×10⁸) — identical, as it must be.
  5. Moon. M = 7.35×10²² kg, R = 1.74×10⁶ m. 2GM/R = 2 × 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 7.35×10²² / 1.74×10⁶ = 5.64×10⁶; v = 2.37 km·s⁻¹ — under a quarter of Earth's, which is why the lunar module could take off on a single small engine, and why the Moon has no atmosphere: at 300 K the faster tail of any gas distribution exceeds 2.4 km·s⁻¹ and leaks away over geological time.
Earth: 11.2 km·s⁻¹ · Moon: 2.37 km·s⁻¹

Three traps in one formula

① "Escape velocity" is a speed. Ignoring air and terrain, the launch direction is irrelevant — energy is a scalar. Straight up, at 45°, sideways: same threshold.

② It does not mean "leaving Earth's gravity". The field GM/r² is never zero at any finite r. Escape means never returning, not reaching a boundary. A rocket that keeps thrusting can leave at 1 m·s⁻¹ if it likes; escape speed only applies to unpowered projectiles.

③ Escape from Earth ≠ escape from the Solar System. Leaving the Sun's well from Earth's orbit requires √(2GM☉/a) = 42.1 km·s⁻¹; happily, Earth already carries you at 29.8 km·s⁻¹, so probes launch in the direction of Earth's motion.

Exam point · the √2 relation

At any given radius, vesc = √2 × vcirc. From a surface-grazing orbit, vcirc = 7.90 km·s⁻¹ and 7.90 × 1.414 = 11.2 km·s⁻¹ ✓. This lets you convert between the two in one step, and it also says that a satellite in circular orbit needs its kinetic energy doubled (speed × √2, so K × 2) to escape from where it is.

§ 07

Circular orbits and the geostationary ring

The ideaA satellite is not held up by anything. It is falling, exactly as a dropped stone falls — but it is also travelling sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it just as fast as it drops, so it never arrives. Getting that match right leaves no room for choice: settle on a height and the speed and the time to go once round are both decided for you. One particular height gives a lap time of exactly one day, which is why a dish on a roof can be bolted down and never aimed again.

Circular orbit
Free fall in which gravity is exactly the centripetal force required to bend the path into a circle of that radius — the satellite is falling continuously and continuously missing.
G M mr² = m v²r ⟹ v = G Mr circular orbit only; m cancels — orbits do not care about the satellite's mass

Everything about circular orbits comes from equating two things you already know: the gravitational force GMm/r² and the centripetal requirement mv²/r. The satellite mass cancels immediately, which is why a bolt and a space station released together stay side by side. What is left is a rigid link between radius, speed and period: choose any one and the other two are fixed. There is no such thing as "a satellite in a 400 km orbit going slowly".

Worked example 5 · the geostationary orbit, from scratch

Find the radius, altitude and speed of a geostationary satellite. GME = 3.98×10¹⁴ m³·s⁻², RE = 6.37×10⁶ m.

  1. Use the sidereal day, not the solar day. The satellite must match Earth's rotation relative to the fixed stars: T = 23 h 56 min 4 s = 86 164 s. (Using the 86 400 s solar day inflates r by ~77 km, since Δr/r = ⅔·ΔT/T — small, but the reasoning is the point.)
  2. T² = (8.6164×10⁴)² = 7.424×10⁹ s².
  3. r³ = GMT²/4π² = (3.98×10¹⁴ × 7.424×10⁹)/39.48 = 2.955×10²⁴/39.48 = 7.485×10²² m³.
  4. r = (74.85×10²¹)1/3 = 4.21×10⁷ m (since 4.21³ = 74.6, 4.22³ = 75.2).
  5. Altitude: h = 4.21×10⁷ − 0.637×10⁷ = 3.58×10⁷ m ≈ 35 800 km — about 5.6 Earth radii up, roughly a tenth of the way to the Moon.
  6. Speed: v = 2πr/T = (6.283 × 4.21×10⁷)/86 164 = 3.07×10³ m·s⁻¹.
r = 4.21×10⁷ m · h ≈ 35 800 km · v = 3.07 km·s⁻¹

Informatics footnote: 35 800 km up and back is 71 600 km of vacuum, so one hop through a geostationary satellite costs 7.16×10⁷/3.00×10⁸ = 0.24 s of pure light-speed delay, and a request-plus-reply costs ≈0.48 s. No amount of bandwidth removes it. That is precisely why low-Earth-orbit constellations exist — at 550 km the same delay is ~4 ms.

Exam point · three conditions, not one

A geostationary orbit must be (1) at r = 4.21×10⁷ m, (2) in the equatorial plane and (3) prograde and circular. Miss any one and the satellite is merely geosynchronous — it returns to the same place once a day but wanders in between, tracing a figure-of-eight (an "analemma") if it is inclined. There is exactly one geostationary ring, and it is a scarce, internationally allocated resource.

Relativity peeks in — and it matters for GPS

GPS satellites sit high in a shallower part of the well, so their clocks run fast by about 45 μs per day (gravitational time dilation); their 3.87 km·s⁻¹ speed makes them run slow by about 7 μs per day (special relativity). The net +38 μs·day⁻¹ is corrected in the satellite clock's design frequency. Uncorrected, position errors would accumulate at roughly 11 km per day. Beyond the syllabus, but it is the cleanest everyday proof that gravitational potential is physically real.

§ 08

Orbital energy and the satellite paradox

The ideaEvery orbit runs on a fixed budget: part of it is the motion the satellite has right now, part of it is the debt it owes for sitting close to the planet. Add the two and the single number you get says how tightly the satellite is trapped and how far out it circles — change that number and you change the orbit, leave it alone and the orbit lasts forever. The strange consequence is that taking energy away from a satellite makes it go faster: thin air high up drags on it, it slips to a lower path, and lower paths are quicker ones.

Definition & formula

Total mechanical energy of an orbit
The sum of the satellite's kinetic energy and the pair's gravitational potential energy — a constant of the motion, and negative for every bound orbit.
E = K + U = ½ m v² − GMmr = − G M m2r circular orbit of radius r; for an ellipse replace r by the semi-major axis a
Full definition, symbols and units
Bound vs unbound
E < 0 is an ellipse (a circle is the special case), E = 0 is a parabola (the escape threshold), E > 0 is a hyperbola (a fly-by that never returns).
K = + GMm2r, U = − GMmrso U = −2K and E = −K = U/2 (the virial theorem)
ΔE to move from r₁ to r₂ = (GMm/2)(1/r₁ − 1/r₂)positive when r₂ > r₁ — climbing costs energy

asemi-major axis of an elliptical orbit (m)

Substitute the orbit condition v² = GM/r into the kinetic energy: K = ½m(GM/r) = GMm/2r. Add U = −GMm/r and the potential energy wins by a factor of two:

E = GMm2r − GMmr = − GMm2rnegative ⟹ bound, always

Three consequences are worth stating explicitly. (i) E is negative, so the satellite is trapped; it becomes free exactly when E reaches zero, which reproduces §6. (ii) |E| = K: the energy still needed to escape from a circular orbit equals the kinetic energy the satellite already has — hence the √2 speed factor. (iii) A smaller r means a more negative E, i.e. less total energy. Low orbits are the low ones energetically as well as geometrically.

The paradox: drag makes a satellite speed up

A satellite in low Earth orbit brushes the residual atmosphere. Drag removes mechanical energy, so E becomes more negative, so r shrinks — and since v = √(GM/r), the satellite gets faster. Friction accelerates it. This is genuinely counter-intuitive and is examined regularly.

The bookkeeping resolves it. As r falls by a small amount, K rises by GMm/2r² · |Δr| while U falls by GMm/r² · |Δr| — exactly twice as much. U changes twice as fast as K, so the total still decreases even though the speed increases. Drag has taken energy out of the system; gravity has converted more than that amount of potential energy into kinetic. The general statement is the virial theorem: for an inverse-square force, U = −2K at all times in a circular orbit, so E always follows U rather than K.

Trap: "fire the engine forwards to catch the target ahead of you"

Thrusting prograde adds energy, raising the orbit — and a higher orbit is slower and has a longer period, so you fall further behind. To catch something ahead of you in the same orbit you must brake, drop to a lower and faster orbit, get ahead, then climb back. In orbital mechanics "speed up to go faster" is false; the working intuition is lower = faster, higher = slower, and radius is set by energy.

Exam point · sign audit

For a bound circular orbit at radius r: U = −GMm/r (negative, largest magnitude), K = +GMm/2r (positive, half the magnitude of U), E = −GMm/2r (negative, equal to −K). Options offering −GMm/r or +GMm/2r for the total energy are the standard distractors: the first is U alone, the second is K alone.

§ 09

Kepler's three laws

The ideaLong before anyone knew why, one man spent years fitting curves to naked-eye records of where the planets had been, and came away with three rules that simply worked. Orbits are slightly squashed circles rather than true ones, with the Sun off to one side rather than in the middle. A planet hurries when it is near the Sun and dawdles when it is far. And the further out a planet lies, the disproportionately longer its year. Newton's contribution was to show that all three drop out of the single force this note began with — they were never three separate facts, only three faces of one.

Definition & formula

Kepler's first law (orbits)
Every planetary orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus — not at the centre, and there is nothing at the other focus.
T² = 4π²G M · a³ M is the central mass; strictly M + m, negligible when m ≪ M

Kepler's second law (areas)
The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
Kepler's third law (periods)
The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, with a constant that depends only on the central mass.

Deriving the third law from gravitation

Kepler found T² ∝ a³ empirically in 1619 by fitting Tycho Brahe's data; it was a rule with no reason. Newton supplied the reason in two lines. For a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force and the speed is 2πr/T:

GMmr² = m(2πr/T)²r = 4π²mrT² ⟹ T² = 4π²GM r³circular case; the full ellipse gives the same with r → a

The satellite mass cancels, the constant is 4π²/GM, and the exponents 2 and 3 come directly from the inverse square. Change the force law and the exponents change — Kepler's third law is a fingerprint of 1/r².

Worked example 6 · weighing the Sun with Kepler's third law

Earth's orbit: a = 1.496×10¹¹ m, T = 1 yr = 3.156×10⁷ s. Find the Sun's mass.

  1. Rearrange: M = 4π² a³ / (G T²). Note this gives the mass of the central body; Earth's own mass has cancelled and cannot be found this way.
  2. a³ = (1.496×10¹¹)³ = 3.348×10³³ m³.
  3. T² = (3.156×10⁷)² = 9.960×10¹⁴ s².
  4. Numerator: 4π² × 3.348×10³³ = 39.48 × 3.348×10³³ = 1.322×10³⁵.
  5. Denominator: 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 9.960×10¹⁴ = 6.643×10⁴.
  6. M = 1.322×10³⁵ / 6.643×10⁴ = 1.99×10³⁰ kg — about 333 000 Earths.
M☉ ≈ 1.99×10³⁰ kg

The same trick with the Moon's orbit (r = 3.84×10⁸ m, T = 27.3 d) gives Earth's mass, and with a star's wobble it gives the masses of exoplanets. Orbits are the astronomer's only balance.

The second law is angular momentum in disguise

Gravity is a central force: it always points from the planet straight at the Sun. Torque is τ = r F sinθ and θ = 180° for a central force, so the torque about the Sun is identically zero and the angular momentum L = m v r sinθ is conserved. In a small time dt the radius vector sweeps a thin triangle of area dA = ½ r · (v dt) sinθ = (L/2m) dt. Since L and m are constants, dA/dt is constant — which is the second law. Kepler's empirical rule and a conservation law are the same statement.

At perihelion and aphelion (and nowhere else) the velocity is perpendicular to the radius, so sinθ = 1 and the conservation law collapses to the pleasant form rp vp = ra va. For Earth, vp/va = (1+e)/(1−e) = 1.0167/0.9833 = 1.034 — we move 3.4 % faster in early January (perihelion) than in early July. Note that this has nothing to do with the seasons, which are caused by axial tilt.

2 · Areas Equal areas swept in equal times Conservation of angular momentum — gravity is central, so zero torque about the Sun Not "equal arc lengths" and not "constant speed". Also true for any central force, not only 1/r².
3 · Periods T² = (4π²/GM) a³ Setting gravity equal to the centripetal requirement; the exponents come from 1/r² Not T ∝ a³. The constant is not universal: it depends on the central mass, so Earth's satellites and the Sun's planets use different constants.

Ratio shortcut for the third law

Around the same central body, (T₂/T₁)² = (a₂/a₁)³. In astronomical units and years around the Sun the constant is exactly 1, so a = 4 AU gives T = 43/2 = 8 yr, and Mars at 1.52 AU gives 1.521.5 = 1.87 yr. No G, no masses, no calculator.

§ 10

Apparent weightlessness

The ideaAn astronaut floating inside a space station has not escaped gravity. Gravity up there is very nearly as strong as it is on the ground. What has gone missing is the floor. When you, the room around you and everything in it are all falling together, nothing has to press on anything else — and it is that pressing you actually feel and call your weight, not the pull itself. The same thing happens for a second when you jump off a wall; being in orbit only arranges for the falling never to end.

Definition & formula

True weight
The gravitational force on a body, W = mg. It is never zero anywhere in the Solar System.
N = m ( g − a ) a = downward acceleration of the frame; N is the scale reading
Full definition, symbols and units
Apparent weight
The magnitude of the contact (normal) force supporting the body — what a bathroom scale, a seat or the soles of your feet actually register.
Apparent weightlessness (free fall)
The state in which the support force is zero because the body and its surroundings are accelerating together under gravity alone.
a = g ⟹ N = 0free fall: lift with a cut cable, parabolic flight, or orbit
gISS = 8.68 m·s⁻² = 0.89 g₀gravity at 400 km — nowhere near zero
Nnormal / support force = apparent weight (N)
Wtrue weight, mg (N)
aacceleration of the frame, downward positive (m·s⁻²)
mmass — unchanged by any of this (kg)

A satellite in orbit is not beyond gravity's reach; it is obeying gravity completely. From Worked example 1, the field at the ISS is 8.68 m·s⁻², about 89 % of its value on the ground. If gravity there were really zero the station would fly off in a straight line, which is precisely what it does not do. What is missing is not gravity but the floor: the station, the astronaut and the loose pen all accelerate at the same 8.68 m·s⁻² towards Earth's centre, so nothing presses on anything else and N = 0. The correct term is free fall, and NASA's own word is microgravity — residual accelerations of order 10⁻⁶ g from drag, crew movement and the tidal gradient across the 100 m station.

The same state is reachable without leaving the atmosphere. In a lift whose cable is cut, a parabolic-flight aircraft (about 22 s of free fall per arc), or the moment after you jump off a wall, N = 0 and you are "weightless" in exactly the sense an astronaut is. Orbit only adds enough sideways speed that the fall never terminates in a floor.

Trap: "centrifugal force balances gravity in orbit"

In an inertial frame there is no outward force to balance anything — gravity is unopposed, which is exactly why the satellite accelerates (centripetally) instead of travelling straight. You may work in the rotating frame and introduce a centrifugal pseudo-force, in which case it does cancel gravity, but then you must say so and accept a non-inertial frame. Answer options that assert a real outward force in an inertial frame are always wrong. Likewise, "weightless because there is no air" confuses buoyancy with weight.

Exam point · what changes and what does not

In free fall, mass is unchanged, true weight mg is almost unchanged and apparent weight is zero. Anything that needs a support force stops working: sedimentation, convection, pouring liquid, a mercury barometer, using a spring balance. Anything driven by mass or by intermolecular forces continues normally: inertia (a floating hammer still hurts), surface tension (water forms spheres), diffusion, and every chemical reaction.

Why physiologists care

The human cardiovascular system is calibrated to a hydrostatic column of roughly 120 cm of blood — a pressure difference of about 13 kPa (≈100 mmHg) between foot and head when standing. Remove g's support and that gradient vanishes: fluid shifts cephalad within hours (puffy face, "bird legs"), central baroreceptors read the volume as excessive, and plasma volume falls by 10–15 % in the first days. On return, orthostatic intolerance follows. Weight-bearing bone, deprived of load, demineralises at roughly 1–1.5 % per month at the hip and spine, and the spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (optic disc oedema, hyperopic shift) is thought to follow from the same headward shift. All of it is the physiology of N = 0 — the same reason prolonged bed rest is itself a clinical problem.

§ 11

Tides: the gradient of gravity

The ideaThe Moon pulls on the whole Earth, and because the Earth is a wide object rather than a dot, it does not pull on every part of it equally — the near face is tugged a little harder than the middle, the far face a little less. Whatever amount of pull they all share simply carries the entire planet along together and goes unfelt, exactly as free fall always does. Only the small leftover differences do anything, and those leftovers point outwards at both ends: hence a bulge of water on the side facing the Moon, a second bulge on the side facing away, and two high tides a day on most coasts rather than one.

Definition & formula

Tidal (differential) acceleration
The difference between the gravitational field of a distant body at a point on Earth and its field at Earth's centre — what is left over after removing the acceleration the whole Earth already shares.
Δg ≈ 2 G M dr³ stretch at the near and far points, for d ≪ r
Full definition, symbols and units
Tidal bulge
The equilibrium deformation of the oceans produced by that residual field: two bulges, one facing the perturbing body and one directly opposite.
tidal strength ∝ M / r³ (not M / r²)it is the gradient of the field, so one extra power of r
ΔgMoon / ΔgSun ≈ 2.2although the Sun pulls Earth 178× harder overall

Mmass of the perturbing body — Moon or Sun (kg)
rdistance to the perturbing body (m)
dradius of the body being stretched, here RE (m)

The Moon does not "lift" the near ocean while somehow flinging the far one away. The clean statement is this: the whole Earth is in free fall towards the Moon, and free fall is unnoticeable. What the oceans respond to is only the part of the Moon's field that differs from the field at Earth's centre. On the near side the Moon's pull is a little stronger than average, so the residual points towards the Moon; on the far side it is a little weaker, so the residual points away from the Moon. Both residuals point outward from Earth's centre — hence two bulges, on opposite sides. Around the flanks the residual points inward, which is where the water comes from.

Worked example 7 · Moon versus Sun

The Sun is 2.7×10⁷ times more massive than the Moon. Why does the Moon dominate the tides? MM = 7.35×10²² kg at 3.84×10⁸ m; M☉ = 1.99×10³⁰ kg at 1.496×10¹¹ m.

  1. Direct pull goes as M/r²: (M☉/MM)(rM/r☉)² = 2.71×10⁷ × (2.567×10⁻³)² = 2.71×10⁷ × 6.59×10⁻⁶ = 178. The Sun pulls Earth 178 times harder than the Moon does.
  2. Tidal effect goes as M/r³. Moon: 7.35×10²² / (3.84×10⁸)³ = 7.35×10²² / 5.66×10²⁵ = 1.30×10⁻³.
  3. Sun: 1.99×10³⁰ / (1.496×10¹¹)³ = 1.99×10³⁰ / 3.35×10³³ = 5.94×10⁻⁴.
  4. Ratio: 1.30×10⁻³ / 5.94×10⁻⁴ = 2.2. The Moon's tidal effect is 2.2 times the Sun's, even though its pull is 178 times weaker — the one extra power of r reverses the verdict.
  5. Magnitude check: Δg = 2GMMRE/r³ = 2 × 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 1.30×10⁻³ × 6.37×10⁶ = 1.1×10⁻⁶ m·s⁻², about 10⁻⁷ of g. Tides are enormous only because the ocean is a fluid covering a whole hemisphere and can respond resonantly to a persistent tiny force.
Moon : Sun tidal effect = 2.2 : 1, despite a 1 : 178 ratio of direct pull

The rest of tidal behaviour follows. Earth rotates through the two bulges, so most coasts see two high tides per lunar day of 24 h 50 min — one every 12 h 25 min, and about 50 minutes later each day because the Moon has moved on in its orbit. When Sun and Moon line up (new and full moon) the two tidal fields add and give spring tides; at first and last quarter they oppose and give neap tides, roughly (1 + 0.46) against (1 − 0.46), a range ratio of about 2.7. Actual local tides depart wildly from this equilibrium picture because ocean basins have their own resonances and coastlines funnel water — which is why the Bay of Fundy manages 16 m and the Mediterranean barely 0.3 m.

Trap: the far-side bulge

Two wrong stories circulate. "The Moon repels the far side" — gravity is never repulsive. "Centrifugal force from Earth's rotation throws the water out" — Earth's own spin is symmetric about the axis and cannot produce a bulge that tracks the Moon. The correct account needs only subtraction: the far side is pulled less than the centre, and being pulled less than your surroundings is indistinguishable from being pushed outward.

Exam point · 1/r³ shows up elsewhere

The same differential-gravity logic explains why a body falling into a black hole is "spaghettified", why Saturn's rings sit inside the Roche limit where tidal stretch beats the moonlet's self-gravity, and why the Moon is tidally locked — the bulges Earth raises on the Moon dissipated its spin energy until one face was fixed. The Moon returns the favour: tidal friction is slowing Earth's rotation by about 2 ms per century and pushing the Moon outward by ~3.8 cm per year, angular momentum being conserved between the two.
