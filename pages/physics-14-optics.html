Physics · Note 14

Geometric and Wave Optics

There is only one physical object on this page — a wavefront — and only one question: how big is the thing the wavefront has to get past, compared with its own wavelength? When apertures, mirrors and pupils are enormously larger than λ, the wavefront's normals behave like straight lines and you may draw rays; every mirror and lens formula follows. When the obstacle shrinks towards λ, the ray picture collapses and interference and diffraction take over. Learn where that boundary sits and the rest of optics stops being a list of equations.

Notation trap · f is not f, m is not m, n is not n

f is frequency in c = fλ (Hz) but focal length in 1/do + 1/di = 1/f (m). m is lateral magnification in m = −di/do (dimensionless) but the integer fringe order in d sin θ = mλ. n is refractive index in Snell's law but a counting integer in the thin-film conditions. Read the units and the context, not the letter.

§ 01

Light as an electromagnetic wave — and when it is a ray

The ideaLight is a ripple in electric and magnetic fields that carries itself along and needs nothing at all to travel through. Whether you can get away with drawing it as a straight line depends on one comparison: how wide the opening or obstacle in its way is, next to the length of a single ripple. Doorways, mirrors and pupils are enormous by that standard, so light seems to go in straight lines and cast sharp shadows — but shrink the gap towards the size of one ripple and light starts to spread and fan out the way water waves do round a harbour wall. Everything on this page is one side of that comparison or the other.

Definition & formula

Electromagnetic wave
A self-sustaining, transverse oscillation of coupled electric and magnetic fields, with E ⟂ B ⟂ direction of travel, requiring no medium and moving through vacuum at exactly c.
c = f λ = 3.00×10⁸ m·s⁻¹ in vacuum; in a medium replace c by v

An accelerating charge radiates. The radiated E and B fields regenerate each other through Faraday's and Maxwell–Ampère's laws, so the disturbance propagates without anything to propagate in — and Maxwell's equations fix its speed at c = 1/√(ε₀μ₀). Substituting ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² F·m⁻¹ and μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m·A⁻¹ gives 3.00×10⁸ m·s⁻¹, which is the single strongest piece of evidence that light is an electromagnetic wave.

Everything on the spectrum below is the same phenomenon. Only frequency distinguishes a radio wave from a γ-ray, and frequency is set by the source. Visible light is the narrow octave your retinal pigments happen to absorb — roughly 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red).

Ordering hook — "Radio Microwaves Inflame Very Unhappy X-ray Gamers"

Radio → Micro → Infrared → Visible → UV → X-ray → γ. Wavelength falls left to right; frequency and photon energy rise. Inside the visible band, ROYGBIV runs long-λ red to short-λ violet.

When you may draw rays

A wavefront bends around an obstacle by an angle of order λ/a. For a 1 mm pupil and 550 nm light that is 5.5×10⁻⁴ rad — negligible, so light appears to travel in straight lines and casts sharp shadows. Shrink the aperture to 5 µm and the spreading is 0.1 rad, which is not negligible at all. The whole of §§2–8 is the a ≫ λ limit; §§9–11 are what happens when that fails.

Exam point

Geometric optics is not a different theory from wave optics — it is the short-wavelength limit of it, exactly as Newtonian mechanics is the low-speed limit of relativity. Both regimes obey Fermat's principle: light takes the path whose travel time is stationary. The law of reflection and Snell's law are both single-line consequences of it.

§ 02

Reflection and the plane mirror

The ideaLight that bounces off a surface leaves at the same slant it arrived at, measured against a line drawn straight out of the surface. Whether you end up seeing a picture or just a lit-up wall has nothing to do with that rule and everything to do with how smooth the surface is: a smooth one keeps neighbouring bits of light in step, so the picture survives the bounce, while a rough one throws them off in all directions. That is why you can see yourself in still water, and why you can see this page but never yourself in it.

Definition & formula

Reflection
The return of a wave into the medium it came from when it meets a boundary, with the frequency, wavelength and speed all unchanged.
θi = θr angles measured from the normal, both rays and the normal coplanar

The law of reflection is a statement about a single ray at a single point. What distinguishes a mirror from a wall is not the law — both obey it — but the surface:

Specular reflection

Surface irregularities ≪ λ. Parallel incoming rays stay parallel on the way out, so the angular information in the beam survives and an image forms. Polished metal, still water, glass.

Diffuse reflection

Surface irregularities ≫ λ. Each patch of surface has its own normal, so parallel rays leave in all directions. No image — but this is why you can see this page from any angle, and why an unpolished wall looks equally bright from everywhere.

A plane mirror obeys 1/do + 1/di = 1/f with f = ∞, which forces di = −do and therefore m = −di/do = +1. So the image is: virtual, upright, the same size, and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. All four facts come from one equation — do not memorise them separately.

Trap · "the mirror reverses left and right"

It does not. A plane mirror reverses the axis perpendicular to its surface (front–back). Your mirror image's left hand appears on your right only because you imagine rotating yourself about a vertical axis to face it — a rotation the mirror never performed. Consequence worth remembering: to see your whole body you need a mirror only half your height, mounted with its top edge halfway between your eyes and the top of your head, and this is independent of how far away you stand.

§ 03

Spherical mirrors and the sign convention

The ideaDish a mirror inwards and it gathers light towards a point; bulge it outwards and it throws light apart. Where the picture ends up, how big it is and whether it is the right way up all fall out of one small equation linking three distances — how far away the object is, how far away the picture lands, and how strongly the mirror bends light. The catch is bookkeeping. That equation only works if you are ruthless about which distances count as positive and which as negative, which is why this section spends more time on plus and minus signs than on mirrors.

The point on the axis through which paraxial rays arriving parallel to the axis pass after reflection (concave), or from which they appear to diverge (convex).
1/do + 1/di = 1/f , f = R/2 paraxial rays only (small angles to the axis)

Go deeper · deriving f = R/2, and where spherical aberration comes from

Why f = R/2: take a paraxial ray parallel to the axis striking a concave mirror of radius R at height y. The normal at that point is the radius, so the ray makes an angle α ≈ y/R with it, and reflects through 2α. It therefore crosses the axis at a distance y/(2α) = R/2 from the mirror — independent of y, which is exactly why a focus exists. That independence only holds while y ≪ R: outside the paraxial region, outer rays cross closer to the mirror, which is spherical aberration. A parabolic mirror removes it exactly, which is why telescope and headlight reflectors are parabolic.

The sign convention — write this out before every problem

One convention covers mirrors and lenses. The organising rule is: positive means real — real objects, real images, converging elements.

Trap · the two meanings of "positive di"

For a mirror, a real image is on the same side as the object; for a lens, a real image is on the opposite side. Students who memorise "real = in front" and apply it to lenses get every sign wrong. The convention that unifies them: light must actually go to a real image, so a real image is always on the side the light leaves from.

Worked example 1 · the same object, two mirrors

A 4.0 cm tall object stands 30 cm in front of (a) a concave mirror of radius 40 cm, then (b) a convex mirror of the same radius. Locate and describe both images.

  1. (a) Concave. f = R/2 = +20 cm. 1/di = 1/f − 1/do = 1/20 − 1/30 = (3 − 2)/60 = 1/60 → di = +60 cm.
  2. Positive → in front of the mirror → real. m = −di/do = −60/30 = −2.0: inverted, twice as large, so hi = −8.0 cm.
  3. (b) Convex. Now R is negative: f = −20 cm. 1/di = −1/20 − 1/30 = −(3 + 2)/60 = −5/60 → di = −12 cm.
  4. Negative → behind the mirror → virtual. m = −(−12)/30 = +0.40: upright and reduced, hi = +1.6 cm.
  5. Sanity check: the object sits beyond C = 40 cm? No — 30 cm is between F (20) and C (40), and the table says the concave image should be real, inverted and enlarged beyond C. di = 60 cm > 40 cm ✓.

§ 04

Refraction, refractive index and Snell's law

The ideaLight moves more slowly through glass or water than through empty space, and that slowing down is the whole reason a beam changes direction when it crosses from one to the other. Picture a marching band striding at an angle off tarmac onto mud: the near end of the row is held back first, so the whole line swings round. A single number for each material — how much it slows light — is all you need to predict how sharply the beam turns. What never changes on the way in is how rapidly the light oscillates, and that is what your eye reads as colour.

Definition & formula

Refraction
The change of direction of a wave crossing a boundary, caused by the change in its propagation speed.
n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ angles from the normal; both rays and the normal coplanar
Full definition, symbols and units
Index of refraction
The factor by which a medium slows light: the ratio of the vacuum speed to the phase speed in the medium. It is dimensionless and never less than 1 for ordinary matter.
n = c / v ≥ 1definition; v is the phase speed in the medium
λn = λvac / n , f unchangedalways true at a boundary
nrefractive index (dimensionless)
vspeed of light in the medium (m·s⁻¹)
θ₁, θ₂angles of incidence / refraction, from the normal (° or rad)
λnwavelength inside the medium (m)
ffrequency, set by the source (Hz)

Here is the whole of refraction in one sentence: the frequency cannot change, so if the speed changes the wavelength must change, and a wavefront whose two ends are travelling at different speeds must swing round. The frequency is fixed because the fields on the two sides of the boundary have to oscillate in step at every instant — if they did not, the wave would have to tear at the interface, which Maxwell's boundary conditions forbid.

Follow the wavefront rather than the ray. Its leading edge enters the slower medium first and is held back while the trailing edge is still moving fast; the front therefore pivots towards the normal. That is why light entering a denser (higher-n) medium bends towards the normal — and, reversibly, bends away from the normal on leaving it. Snell's law is just this statement with the geometry done.

Worked example 2 · what actually changes when light enters water

Sodium light (λ = 589 nm in vacuum) strikes a still pond at 40° to the normal. nwater = 1.33. Find the refraction angle, the speed, the wavelength and the frequency in water.

  1. Angle. n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ → sin θ₂ = (1.00)(sin 40°)/1.33 = 0.6428/1.33 = 0.4833. θ₂ = arcsin 0.4833 = 28.9° — bent towards the normal, as it must be.
  2. Speed. v = c/n = (3.00×10⁸)/1.33 = 2.26×10⁸ m·s⁻¹.
  3. Frequency. Fixed by the source and unchanged at the boundary: f = c/λvac = (3.00×10⁸)/(589×10⁻⁹) = 5.09×10¹⁴ Hz.
  4. Wavelength in water. λn = λvac/n = 589/1.33 = 443 nm.
  5. Cross-check: v/λn = (2.26×10⁸)/(443×10⁻⁹) = 5.10×10¹⁴ Hz ✓ (the 0.2 % gap is rounding).
θ₂ = 28.9° · v = 2.26×10⁸ m·s⁻¹ · λ = 443 nm · f = 5.09×10¹⁴ Hz (unchanged)

Trap · "the colour changes under water"

It does not. Your eye responds to frequency, and frequency is the one thing refraction leaves alone. The 443 nm wavelength inside the water would be blue if you saw it in air — but you never do, because the light re-expands to 589 nm on the way out. Any question that says "speed decreases, therefore frequency decreases" is wrong; v = fλ is satisfied by λ falling instead.

§ 05

Total internal reflection, fibres and dispersion

The ideaLight trying to leave glass or water for air swings away from the straight-out direction, and if it is already slanted far enough it cannot get out at all: it turns round and stays inside, losing nothing whatever. Light trapped that way is what runs the length of an optical fibre and follows every bend of an endoscope. The same crossing does something else too — it slows some colours slightly more than others, so one bend can sort white light into a fan, which is all a prism is and all a rainbow is.

Definition & formula

Total internal reflection (TIR)
The complete return of light into the denser medium when it meets a boundary with a rarer medium at or beyond the critical angle — the only reflection in optics with 100 % efficiency.
sin θc = n₂ / n₁ requires n₁ > n₂ — dense to rare only
Full definition, symbols and units
Dispersion
The dependence of refractive index on wavelength, so that a single refraction sorts white light by colour.
θ ≥ θc → no refracted ray, R = 100 %energy conservation: transmission is zero
n(violet) > n(red)normal dispersion: short λ is slowed more
θccritical angle, from the normal (°)
n₁index of the denser incident medium (dimensionless)
n₂index of the rarer medium (dimensionless)
Rreflectance, fraction of incident power reflected (dimensionless)

Set θ₂ = 90° in Snell's law — the refracted ray lying flat along the surface — and you get n₁ sin θc = n₂ sin 90° = n₂. Push the incidence past θc and Snell's law would demand sin θ₂ > 1, which has no solution: there is no refracted ray to carry energy away, so every joule goes into the reflected ray. This is the only mirror in physics with no losses — silvered mirrors reflect 90–95 %, TIR reflects 100 %.

Two immediate consequences of the n₁ > n₂ condition: TIR is impossible going from air into glass, and the higher the index contrast the smaller θc. Diamond's θc = 24.4° means light entering the stone bounces internally many times before it finds a facet steep enough to escape — that, plus strong dispersion, is the "fire".

Optical fibres and endoscopy

A step-index fibre is a glass core (n ≈ 1.62) inside a cladding of slightly lower index (n ≈ 1.52). Light launched within a small acceptance cone strikes the core–cladding wall well beyond θc = arcsin(1.52/1.62) = 69.8° and is totally reflected thousands of times per metre, following the fibre round bends with almost no loss. Because the cladding — not the outer surface — sets the condition, the fibre keeps working when it is touched, bundled or immersed in body fluid.

An endoscope exploits this twice: an incoherent bundle (fibres in any order) carries illumination down, and a coherent bundle (fibres held in the same relative position at both ends, so each fibre is one pixel) carries the image back. The same principle carries laser energy to the tissue in endoscopic surgery, and modern video endoscopes replace the imaging bundle with a chip at the tip while keeping the fibre illumination.

Exam point

Three facts about TIR are examined over and over: it happens only from dense to rare; the critical angle is measured from the normal; and the reflection is total, i.e. 100 %, not merely strong. A question giving n₁ < n₂ and asking for θc is testing whether you notice that no critical angle exists.

Dispersion and the rainbow

An electron bound in a molecule has resonant frequencies in the ultraviolet. Visible light drives that oscillator below resonance, and the closer the driving frequency comes to resonance the larger the response — so blue and violet are slowed more than red. For water, n runs from ≈1.331 at 700 nm to ≈1.343 at 400 nm. A prism therefore refracts violet through the larger angle at both surfaces, and white light fans out.

In a raindrop the light refracts on entry, reflects once off the back, and refracts again on exit. The total deviation has a minimum, which piles up rays at one angle and makes the bow bright there: ≈42° from the antisolar point for red, ≈40° for violet — so the primary bow has red on the outside. Drops that reflect twice internally give the fainter secondary bow at ≈51°, with the colours reversed (red inside), and the sky between the two bows is genuinely darker (Alexander's dark band) because no drop deviates light into that annulus.

Trap · dispersion versus diffraction

Both split white light, by opposite orderings. A prism (dispersion) deviates violet most, because n is largest there. A grating (diffraction) deviates red most, because sin θ = mλ/d grows with λ. If a question shows a spectrum with red at the far edge, it is a grating, not a prism.

§ 06

Thin lenses and principal-ray diagrams

The ideaA lens is two curved glass surfaces that between them take a spreading fan of light and either pull it back together or push it further apart. Where the picture forms comes out of exactly the same equation you have just used for curved mirrors, so there is no new physics here, only a new object. Better still, you can often skip the arithmetic and draw the answer: three particular paths through a lens behave in ways you can predict on sight, and wherever those three cross is where the picture is.

Definition & formula

Thin lens
A refracting element whose thickness is negligible compared with do, di and R, so that both refracting surfaces can be treated as acting at one plane.
1/do + 1/di = 1/f thin lens, paraxial rays, monochromatic light, lens in air
Full definition, symbols and units
Focal point
The axial point to which paraxial rays parallel to the axis converge (converging lens) or from which they appear to diverge (diverging lens). A thin lens has one on each side, equidistant, even when the two faces differ.

The thin-lens equation is identical in form to the mirror equation, which is not a coincidence: both come from the paraxial approximation applied to a surface that turns a diverging spherical wave into a converging one. Learn one equation, then learn the single sign table in §3 — its mirror rows and its lens rows.

The three principal rays

Any two of these locate the image; draw the third as a check. All three must meet at one point — if they do not, your focal marks are wrong.

  1. Parallel ray. Leaves the object tip parallel to the axis, and after the lens passes through the far focal point F′ (converging) or appears to come from the near focal point (diverging).
  2. Central ray. Passes through the centre of the lens undeviated — the two faces are parallel there, so it behaves like a thin sheet of glass.
  3. Focal ray. Passes through the near focal point F on the way in, and emerges parallel to the axis.

Trap · "a converging lens makes a real image"

Only when the object is outside the focal point. Inside it, a converging lens gives a virtual, upright, magnified image — the entire basis of the magnifying glass. Conversely a diverging lens with a real object never makes a real image, so "diverging lens, real image" is always a wrong option unless the incoming light is already converging.

§ 07

The lensmaker's equation and power in dioptres

The ideaThis section runs the previous one backwards. Instead of asking what a given lens does, it asks what you would have to grind in a workshop to get a lens that does what you want. Only two things are yours to choose — what the lens is made of, which fixes how much light slows down inside it, and how strongly you curve the two faces — and between them those two settle the lens completely. Opticians then quote lens strength in a unit picked for one thoroughly practical reason: put two lenses together and their strengths simply add up, which focal lengths stubbornly refuse to do.

Definition & formula

Two things determine a lens: how much slower light is inside it (the factor n − 1) and how strongly the surfaces are curved (the bracket). Both appear as products, so doubling the curvature or doubling n − 1 doubles the power. Note the immediate corollary of the third formula: a glass lens (n = 1.52) immersed in water (n = 1.33) has its power cut by a factor of (1.52/1.33 − 1)/(1.52 − 1) = 0.143/0.52 ≈ 0.27 — which is why you cannot see clearly underwater without a mask. The air gap in front of the cornea is what your eye's optics depend on.

Trap · the sign of R₂

For a biconvex lens, the second surface bulges towards the incoming light, so its centre of curvature is on the incoming side and R₂ is negative. The bracket then becomes 1/R₁ + 1/|R₂|, a sum, and the lens converges. Students who put both radii positive get a near-zero or negative power and conclude the formula is broken. Sanity check every time: a fatter middle must give a bigger positive P.

Dioptres are the clinician's unit for exactly one reason: they add. Two thin lenses in contact place the image of the first at the object position of the second with negligible separation, and the reciprocal-focal-length algebra collapses to a plain sum. A +2.00 D reading add on top of a −3.00 D distance correction gives −1.00 D through the lower segment of the bifocal. Focal lengths, by contrast, never add.

Worked example 4 · from glass shape to prescription

A biconvex lens is ground from crown glass (n = 1.50) with a front radius of 20 cm and a back radius of 30 cm. (a) Find f and P. (b) It is then cemented to a diverging lens of power −1.50 D. Find the combined focal length.

  1. Signs first. Light meets the front surface, whose centre of curvature is on the outgoing side: R₁ = +0.20 m. The back surface bulges towards the source, centre of curvature on the incoming side: R₂ = −0.30 m.
  2. (a) 1/f = (1.50 − 1)(1/0.20 − 1/(−0.30)) = 0.50 × (5.000 + 3.333) = 0.50 × 8.333 = 4.17 m⁻¹.
  3. So P = +4.17 D and f = 1/4.17 = 0.240 m = 24.0 cm. Positive, as a biconvex lens must be.
  4. (b) In contact, powers add: P = 4.17 + (−1.50) = +2.67 D.
  5. f = 1/2.67 = 0.375 m = 37.5 cm. The pair still converges, but more weakly — the focal length grew from 24 to 37.5 cm.
(a) P = +4.17 D, f = 24.0 cm · (b) P = +2.67 D, f = 37.5 cm

Exam point

Convert to metres before taking the reciprocal. "f = 25 cm" is +4.00 D, not +0.04 D. And powers add only for lenses in contact: for separated lenses you must trace the image through, or use P = P₁ + P₂ − d P₁P₂ with d the separation in metres.

§ 08

The eye — and the instruments built on it

The ideaYour eye is a lens system with a screen at the back of it, and seeing sharply means the picture lands exactly on that screen and not in front of or behind it. Most of the bending happens where air meets the wet front surface of the eye rather than at the lens inside — which is why everything goes soft underwater, and why surgery reshapes the front surface rather than the lens. Long sight and short sight are not faults of the screen; they are mismatches between how strongly the eye bends light and how far back the screen sits, and a spectacle lens simply supplies the missing amount. Everything after that — magnifier, microscope, telescope — is a way of making a small thing take up a bigger slice of what you can see.

Where the refraction actually happens

The relaxed adult eye has a total power of about +60 D. Because the image forms in vitreous of n ≈ 1.34, the image-side focal length is n/P ≈ 1.34/60 ≈ 22 mm, measured from the eye's principal plane — which itself sits about 2 mm behind the cornea, putting the retina ≈24 mm behind the front of the eye. That is the axial length of a normal eye. Differentiate the same relation and 1 mm of extra eyeball is worth about 3 D, which is why so small a mismatch makes you myopic. Of that +60 D, the cornea supplies about +43 D — roughly two-thirds — almost all of it at the air–cornea interface (its front surface alone is ≈+48 D, its back surface ≈−6 D), and the crystalline lens only about +17 to +20 D. The reason is the index step, not the shape: air (1.00) to cornea (1.376) is a jump of 0.38, whereas the lens (≈1.41) is bathed in aqueous and vitreous at 1.34, a jump of only ≈0.07. Refraction scales with Δn, so the biggest jump does the most work.

Two clinical consequences follow immediately. First, refractive surgery reshapes the cornea (LASIK, PRK) because that is where the power is. Second, submerging the eye in water abolishes the air–cornea step and destroys ≈2/3 of the eye's power, leaving you severely hyperopic — a goggle restores the air gap and with it your vision.

The lens matters for a different reason: it is the only part that can change. Ciliary muscle contraction slackens the zonules and lets the lens round up, adding up to ≈+14 D in a child. That is the amplitude of accommodation, and it decays with age as the lens hardens.

All three below solve the same problem: the retina resolves an angle, so to see fine detail you must make the object subtend a bigger angle. The benchmark is the largest angle the naked eye can manage, which is the object held at the near point, 25 cm.

One converging lens, object just inside F. With the virtual image at infinity (relaxed eye) M = 25/f; pulling the image in to the near point gives the slightly larger M = 1 + 25/f. A 5 cm lens gives ×5.

Compound microscope

Refracting telescope

§ 09

Interference: Huygens, coherence, two slits, thin films

The ideaSend two copies of the same light to the same spot by slightly different routes and they will either reinforce each other or cancel each other out, depending on whether one arrives a whole number of ripples behind the other or half a ripple out of step. That is why light passed through two narrow slits lands on a screen as a row of bright and dark bands instead of two blobs, and why a soap bubble and an oil slick show colours they contain no pigment for. The one condition is that the two copies must be carved from the same original beam: two separate lamps drift out of step millions of times a second, and their bands blur away before anything can see them.

Definition & formula

Huygens' construction is the bridge between the two halves of this page. Draw a plane wavefront reaching a boundary at an angle and let each point emit a wavelet that travels at the local speed; the envelope on the far side is tilted by exactly the angle Snell's law predicts, because in the same time the wavelets have radii in the ratio r₁/r₂ = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁. The same construction applied at the edge of an aperture shows wavelets leaking sideways — diffraction. Nothing new is postulated; the ray model simply stops being an adequate summary.

Coherence: why two lamps never interfere

An ordinary source emits in bursts of ~10⁻⁸ s as individual atoms de-excite, each burst with an unrelated phase. Two such sources have a phase difference that scrambles ~10⁸ times a second, so the fringes exist at every instant but wash out on any real detector. The classical fix is to split one wavefront — two slits illuminated by the same source (division of wavefront), or the two surfaces of a film (division of amplitude). A laser sidesteps the problem: stimulated emission makes all the photons share a phase, and coherence lengths run to metres.

Exam point · coherence in the clinic

Optical coherence tomography inverts the usual goal. It uses a source with a deliberately short coherence length (a few µm), so interference only occurs when the sample and reference path lengths match to within that distance. Scanning the reference mirror therefore reads out depth, giving micrometre-resolution cross-sections of the retina — an interference experiment used as a ruler.

Young's double slit

Two slits a distance d apart, illuminated coherently, send light to a point P at angle θ. The lower slit's path is longer by Δ = d sin θ — this is the entire physics. When Δ is a whole number of wavelengths the waves arrive in phase and add; when it is a half-integer they cancel. That the fringes are evenly spaced near the axis follows only from the small-angle approximation sin θ ≈ tan θ = y/L, giving ym = mλL/d and hence a constant Δy = λL/d.

Worked example 7 · fringe spacing

Two slits 0.25 mm apart are lit by 550 nm light; the screen is 2.0 m away. (a) Find the fringe spacing. (b) At what angle does the third-order bright fringe appear?

  1. (a) Δy = λL/d = (550×10⁻⁹ m × 2.0 m)/(0.25×10⁻³ m) = (1.100×10⁻⁶)/(2.5×10⁻⁴) = 4.4×10⁻³ m = 4.4 mm.
  2. Check the small-angle assumption: y₁/L = 4.4×10⁻³/2.0 = 2.2×10⁻³ rad ≈ 0.13°. Comfortably small ✓.
  3. (b) sin θ = mλ/d = 3 × (550×10⁻⁹)/(0.25×10⁻³) = (1.65×10⁻⁶)/(2.5×10⁻⁴) = 6.6×10⁻³.
  4. θ = arcsin(6.6×10⁻³) = 6.6×10⁻³ rad = 0.38°, i.e. 13.2 mm from the centre — exactly 3 Δy, as the even spacing requires.
  5. Note what the formula says about design: to spread the fringes you want a small d and a large L. Halving the slit separation doubles the spacing.

Worked example 8 · anti-reflective coating

Spectacle glass (n = 1.52) is coated with magnesium fluoride (n = 1.38). What is the thinnest coating that suppresses reflection at 550 nm, at normal incidence?

§ 10

Diffraction, the grating and the resolution limit

The ideaLight spreads sideways after it squeezes past an edge or through a narrow gap — and the narrower the gap, the more it spreads, which is the opposite of what squeezing something ought to do. One consequence runs through the whole of imaging: no instrument ever renders a point as a point, only as a small smudge, and two points closer together than that smudge merge into one. That puts a hard floor under the finest detail any eye, microscope or telescope can ever show, a floor set by the light itself rather than by the quality of the glass — and it is exactly why, to see smaller things, people had to stop using light.

Definition & formula

Diffraction
The spreading of a wave after it passes an aperture or edge — the interference of a wavefront with itself, once part of it has been removed.
a sin θ = m λ (dark, m = ±1, ±2 …) single slit of width a, far-field screen — note this gives the minima

Divide a slit of width a into two halves. Every ray in the top half has a partner in the bottom half a distance a/2 away; when those pairs are exactly out of phase, the whole slit cancels. That happens when (a/2) sin θ = λ/2, i.e. a sin θ = λ. Repeating with quarters, sixths and so on gives the general condition a sin θ = mλ for darkness. This is the one formula on the page whose integers label minima rather than maxima, and mixing it up with the double-slit condition is a reliable way to lose a mark.

Trap · a narrower slit spreads the light more

The first minimum sits at sin θ = λ/a, so shrinking a pushes it to a larger angle and the central band gets wider. Ray intuition says the opposite — squeeze the beam and it should get narrower — and that intuition is right only while a ≫ λ, where the geometric shadow dominates. When a → λ, θ → 90°: the light floods the entire forward hemisphere. This inverse relationship, obstacle size down ⇒ spreading up, is the signature of every wave phenomenon on this page.

From two slits to thousands

A grating obeys d sin θ = mλ — formally identical to Young's condition — but the maxima are dramatically sharper. With N slits, moving off a maximum by a phase of only 2π/N is enough for the N amplitudes to cancel around a closed polygon, so the peak width scales as 1/N. Ten thousand lines give needle-sharp orders, which is what turns a grating into a spectrometer: it separates wavelengths that a prism would smear together, with resolving power λ/Δλ = mN.

Worked example 9 · how many orders fit?

A grating is ruled with 600 lines per millimetre and illuminated normally with 550 nm light. Find the angles of the visible orders and the highest order that exists.

  1. d = 1/N = 1 mm / 600 = 1.667×10⁻⁶ m = 1667 nm.
  2. m = 1: sin θ = λ/d = 550/1667 = 0.330 → θ₁ = 19.3°.
  3. m = 2: sin θ = 2 × 0.330 = 0.660 → θ₂ = 41.3°.
  4. m = 3: sin θ = 0.990 → θ₃ = 81.9° — only just makes it, and grazing the surface.
  5. m = 4: sin θ = 1.320 > 1 → no solution. The highest order is therefore m = 3, and in general mmax = ⌊d/λ⌋.
  6. Physical reading: a grating can only diffract into real angles. Push d down towards λ and you are left with the zeroth order alone.

Why resolution has a floor — and why electron microscopes exist

Every real instrument looks at the world through a finite aperture, so every point source arrives as an Airy disc of angular radius 1.22λ/D rather than a point. Two sources closer than that merge. This is not a manufacturing limitation: you cannot resolve detail much finer than the wavelength you are using, however perfect the glass.

Worked example 10 · the eye, the microscope, and the reason for electrons

(a) With a 2.0 mm pupil and 550 nm light, what is the finest detail the eye can resolve at reading distance (25 cm)? (b) What is the floor for a light microscope with numerical aperture 1.4? (c) How does a 100 kV electron compare?

§ 11

Polarisation, Brewster's angle and Malus's law

The ideaLight wobbles from side to side as it travels, and it can wobble in any direction across its line of travel. Everyday light is a jumble of all those directions at once; a polarising filter lets through only the ones lined up with it and blocks the rest. That is the whole trick behind sunglasses that kill the glare off a wet road, because light bouncing off a flat surface comes away wobbling mostly flat along that surface. Once you can say how much light survives a filter turned to a given angle, the rest of the section is arithmetic.

Definition & formula

Polarisation
The orientation of the electric-field oscillation of a light wave. Only transverse waves can be polarised, so the existence of polarised light is direct proof that light is transverse.
I = I₀ cos²θ Malus's law — polarised light entering an analyser at angle θ
Full definition, symbols and units
Unpolarised light
Light whose field direction changes randomly and rapidly — an equal mixture of every transverse orientation, which is what ordinary thermal sources emit.

The angle of incidence at which the reflected beam is completely polarised parallel to the surface, because the reflected and refracted rays are then perpendicular.

Three routes to polarised light are worth knowing:

  1. Absorption. A Polaroid sheet contains aligned long-chain molecules; the field component along the chains drives conduction electrons and is absorbed, so the surviving field is perpendicular to the chains. The transmission axis is at right angles to the molecules, which surprises people.
  2. Reflection. Light reflected from any dielectric is partially polarised parallel to the surface, and at Brewster's angle it is completely so. Glare from water, wet roads and car bonnets is therefore largely horizontal — and polarising sunglasses have a vertical transmission axis to block it.
  3. Scattering. Air molecules re-radiate like tiny dipole antennas, which do not radiate along their own axis; light scattered at 90° from the sun is strongly polarised. Bees navigate by it.
Go deeper · why Brewster’s angle is arctan(n₂ / n₁)

Brewster's condition has a one-line explanation. At θB the reflected and refracted rays are exactly 90° apart. The refracted ray's oscillating dipoles point along its own field direction, and a dipole cannot radiate along its own axis — so the component polarised in the plane of incidence has no way to appear in the reflected beam, leaving only the perpendicular component. Putting θ₂ = 90° − θB into Snell's law and using sin(90° − θ) = cos θ gives tan θB = n₂/n₁ directly. For crown glass, θB = arctan 1.52 = 56.7°; for water, arctan 1.33 = 53.1°.

Worked example 11 · three polarisers, and why the middle one matters

Unpolarised light of intensity I₀ meets polariser P₁, then P₂ at 30° to P₁, then P₃ at 90° to P₁. Find the emerging intensity. Then remove P₂ and repeat.

  1. After P₁. Unpolarised light has every orientation equally, and ⟨cos²θ⟩ = ½ over all angles, so I₁ = 0.500 I₀ — and this does not depend on how P₁ is turned.
  2. After P₂. Now the light is polarised, so Malus applies with θ = 30°: I₂ = I₁ cos²30° = 0.500 I₀ × (0.866)² = 0.500 × 0.750 I₀ = 0.375 I₀.
  3. After P₃. P₃ is 90° − 30° = 60° from P₂: I₃ = I₂ cos²60° = 0.375 I₀ × (0.500)² = 0.375 × 0.250 I₀ = 0.094 I₀, i.e. about 9.4 % gets through.
  4. Remove P₂. P₁ and P₃ are crossed: I = 0.500 I₀ × cos²90° = 0.
  5. Inserting a polariser therefore increases the transmitted light from zero to 9.4 %. There is no paradox: P₂ does not filter the wave, it projects it onto a new axis, and the projected wave has a component along P₃ that the original had not.
With P₂: 0.094 I₀ (9.4 %) · Without P₂: exactly 0

Trap · cos²θ, and the first polariser

Two errors dominate. (1) Using cos θ instead of cos²θ — Malus's law is about intensity, which goes as amplitude squared. At 60°, cos 60° = 0.5 but cos²60° = 0.25. (2) Applying Malus's law at the first polariser. Unpolarised light has no defined θ, so the answer is always I₀/2 there, and Malus's law only starts at the second element.

Exam point · polarised light in the clinic

Compensated polarised-light microscopy of joint aspirate distinguishes the two crystal arthropathies, and it turns on birefringence — the splitting of light into two polarisations that travel at different speeds. Gout: monosodium urate, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent (yellow when the crystal's long axis is parallel to the compensator's slow axis). Pseudogout: calcium pyrophosphate, rhomboid, positively birefringent (blue when parallel). Same instrument, opposite colours, opposite diagnoses.

One line to carry into the exam

Unpolarised → halve it. Polarised → cos² it. Reflected at arctan(n₂/n₁) → it is already polarised, parallel to the surface.
