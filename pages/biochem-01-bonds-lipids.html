§ 01

One ruler for every bond

The ideaNo bond is strong or weak on its own — it is only strong or weak compared with the constant random jostling that warmth gives every molecule in your body. Line every kind of bond up against that jostling on a single scale and the whole subject sorts itself into two families: a small number of permanent joins that hold a molecule together for its lifetime, and a large number of flimsy attractions that are made and broken thousands of times a second. Everything a cell does that looks like recognising, responding or changing its mind happens in that flimsy lower band.

Set the reference point first: thermal energy at body temperature is RT ≈ 2.6 kJ/mol (R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, T = 310 K). Any interaction of that magnitude will be knocked apart by random thermal motion if it stands alone. To matter biologically, it has to come in numbers — which is exactly how noncovalent forces operate in living systems.

Click any row below to expand the notes for that interaction. The axis is logarithmic; note that covalent bonds and noncovalent interactions differ by roughly two orders of magnitude.

The big picture first

Covalent bonds sit at 200–900 kJ/mol. Every noncovalent interaction is squeezed into 0.4–40 kJ/mol. The consequence: covalent bonds do not break spontaneously under physiological conditions — they need an enzyme or harsh chemistry — whereas noncovalent interactions are forming and breaking constantly. Reversibility, recognition and regulation all live in that lower band.

Exam point

"A single van der Waals contact ≈ RT" is the comparison most often turned into a concept question. It also explains why the high affinity of an antibody–antigen or enzyme–substrate pair never comes from one strong interaction, but from the additive effect of dozens of weak ones (cooperativity / avidity) — and therefore why shape complementarity is the origin of specificity.

The whole ruler in four numbers

Four-tenths, four, forty, four hundred — each rung ten times the last

Four-tenths (0.4) is the floor of the entire noncovalent band — the weakest van der Waals contact. Four is the ceiling of van der Waals and simultaneously the floor of the hydrogen bond: the one rung where two families meet. Forty is the ceiling of the whole noncovalent band — the strongest salt bridge in water. Four hundred is an ordinary covalent bond: C–C 348, C–H 414 and O–H 463 all round to it. Each rung is ten times the one below, and typical values sit two rungs apart — a few kJ/mol against a few hundred — which is the two orders of magnitude this whole figure is built around. And RT ≈ 2.6 lands between the first two rungs, which is the entire point of the dashed line. My own hook, not a circulating one. It carries magnitudes, not the individual covalent values: hold S–S ≈ 214 and C=O ≈ 799 separately as the two ends of this ruler's covalent range — S–S is explicitly the weakest of the common covalent bonds (which is why a cell can use it as a redox switch) and C=O the highest value shown — and note that the ladder says nothing about the ≈3 kJ/mol won per –CH₂– buried, which is a per-unit figure rather than a bond strength.

Exam technique · the order-of-magnitude filter

  1. Put any number in the stem on the ladder before you read the options. Under 40 kJ/mol it is a noncovalent interaction; over 200 it is covalent. Nothing biological lives in the gap between, so a value in the hundreds attached to a hydrogen bond, or a value under 100 attached to a C–C bond, is a free elimination requiring no chemistry at all.
  2. Carry one number and derive the rest. RT = 8.314 × 310 = 2577 J/mol ≈ 2.6 kJ/mol at body temperature (≈2.5 at 25 °C — near enough that you never recompute it). Everything else on this page is quoted as a multiple of it: one van der Waals contact ≈ RT; the hydrogen bond between two water molecules ≈ 20, about eight times RT; the O–H covalent bond inside one water molecule 463, more than twenty times that again.
  3. Any option crediting "one strong interaction" is wrong. Affinity and specificity on this page never come from a single contact — antibody–antigen, enzyme–substrate, protein folding all answer many weak interactions plus shape complementarity. That single rule kills the "one high-affinity bond" option every time it appears, and it appears often.
  4. Two absolutes worth having pre-loaded. Covalent bonds do not break spontaneously at 37 °C — they need an enzyme or harsh chemistry; noncovalent interactions are forming and breaking constantly. Any option that reverses either is out on sight, and with −0.5 marking a single elimination already makes answering strictly better than a blank.
  5. Answer this section in ten seconds, not sixty. §01 is pure recognition — there is nothing here to compute. Bank the time for §06 and §09, the only two places on this page where a stem can genuinely make you calculate.
§ 02

Functional groups: the alphabet of biochemistry

The ideaMost of a biological molecule is a dull chain of carbon and hydrogen. The interesting behaviour comes from a small handful of atom clusters bolted onto that chain, and there are only about a dozen of them worth knowing. Each cluster answers three questions about its molecule: will it mix with water, does it carry an electrical charge inside the body, and what can it be joined to. Learn the clusters and you can predict how a molecule behaves without ever having seen it before.

Every biomolecule is a hydrocarbon skeleton decorated with a small set of functional groups. Those groups decide three things that this whole page depends on: (1) polarity — will it dissolve in water; (2) ionisation — does it carry charge at pH 7.4; and (3) reactivity — which condensation reaction it can undergo, and therefore which covalent bond it can form.

The rule that generates the whole table

Solubility in water tracks one thing: can the group participate in the water hydrogen-bond network, or does it interrupt it? That gives a strict ordering:

charged (ion–dipole) > polar, H-bond donor and acceptor (–OH, –NH) > polar, acceptor only (C=O, –O–) > weakly polar (–SH, aromatic π) > nonpolar (–CH₃, –CH₂–, phenyl)

Which two groups make which bond — and why that table is not really a table

Acyl lands on N, O or S → amide, ester, thioester

Three of the six rows are one rule, not three facts: the bond is named after the atom the acyl carbon ends up bonded to. Land on N (–COOH + –NH₂) and you get an amide — the peptide bond. Land on O (–COOH + –OH) and you get an ester — triacylglycerol, phospholipid, wax. Land on S (–COOH + –SH) and you get a thioester — acetyl-CoA, high energy, because sulfur's larger orbitals overlap the carbonyl poorly and so stabilise the ester resonance far less than oxygen does. The last one even labels itself: a thiol hands over its sulfur, so the product is a thioester. My own compression of a standard naming convention, not a circulating hook. It covers only the carboxyl rows; the other three each need one line of their own — glycosidic is anomeric –OH + –OH (the anomeric carbon is a masked aldehyde, which is exactly what makes it the reactive one); phosphodiester is one phosphate esterified twice, which is what the "di" is telling you; disulfide is –SH + –SH and is an oxidation, not a condensation — no water leaves, so it does not belong to this rule at all. The polarity ordering in the callout above needs no mnemonic and you should not build one. It is generated by a single question — can this group join water's hydrogen-bond network, and how fully? Full charge beats donor-and-acceptor beats acceptor-only beats weakly polar beats nonpolar, and any list you memorised would just be that answer written out longhand.

The classic trap

An amide (–CONH₂, and the peptide bond itself) is polar but never ionises at physiological pH — the lone pair on nitrogen is delocalised into the carbonyl. So Asn and Gln are polar-neutral, while Asp and Glu (carboxyl) are negatively charged. Questions love to put "amide" among carboxyl / phosphate / guanidinium and ask which one carries no charge.

Carbon oxidation state → energy content

Alkane (–CH₃) → alcohol (–OH) → aldehyde (–CHO) → carboxylic acid (–COOH) → CO₂. Moving right is oxidation. The more reduced a molecule is (more C–H bonds), the more energy its complete oxidation releases — which is why fat yields ≈ 9 kcal/g against ≈ 4 kcal/g for carbohydrate and protein.

Exam technique · "which group is charged (or uncharged) at pH 7.4"

  1. One comparison decides everything: pKa against 7.4. pKa below 7.4 → the group has given its proton away, so an acid is now negative. pKa above 7.4 → it has kept or taken a proton, so an amine is now positive. There is no third move on this question type.
  2. Run the five you will actually be offered. Carboxyl ≈2–5 → negative. Phosphate pK₁ ≈ 2, pK₂ ≈ 6.8 → one to two negatives. Amino ≈9–10.5 → positive. Guanidinium ≈12.5 → positive at any physiological pH, so Arg is effectively always charged. Imidazole ≈6.0 → the only group genuinely on the fence, which is why His is the answer to every "acts as acid and base at physiological pH" stem.
  3. "Polar but uncharged" is the amide, essentially always. It has no pKa in the table at all — the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl, so it is neither acid nor base. Asn and Gln neutral, Asp and Glu negative: same skeleton, opposite answer. That is the trap, and it is worth recognising in three seconds.
  4. Two-unit sanity check. One pH unit from the pKa is already 91 % converted; two units is 99 %. So any group whose pKa sits more than about two units from 7.4 is fully charged or fully neutral, and the only partial states you ever have to reason about are His and phosphate's second proton.
  5. Elimination without solving anything. In a five-option "which carries no charge" list, carboxyl, phosphate, amino and guanidinium all go at a glance. One elimination already flips the expected value positive under −0.5 marking; here you routinely get four, so this item should never be left blank.
  6. If the stem says "hydrogen bond" rather than "charge", switch tests. A donor needs an H sitting on N or O. –OH has an O–H and lone pairs → donor and acceptor; C=O and –O– have lone pairs but no O–H → acceptor only; –CH₃ does neither. Options on this topic are built from exactly that trio, so labelling each option with one of the three settles the question before you think about the molecule it came from.
§ 03

Covalent bonds: the scaffold

The ideaThese are the permanent joins — the ones that turn a pile of small units into a protein, a starch grain or a strand of DNA, and that stay put unless the cell deliberately dismantles them. Nearly all of them are made in the same way: two pieces are stitched together and a single molecule of water is squeezed out at the join. To take them apart the cell puts that water back, using a dedicated tool for each type of join. So the thing to learn is not the structures but the pairings: which two attachments meet, what join they make, and what undoes it.

There are only a handful of covalent bonds worth memorising in biochemistry, and most of them are made by condensation and broken by a hydrolase. Learn them as a triple — how it forms / where it appears / what cleaves it.

Go deeper · Peptide-bond geometry: partial double bond, planarity, trans preference

Three properties of the peptide bond you will be asked about

① Partial double-bond character

Resonance shortens C–N to ≈ 1.32 Å, between a single and a double bond — roughly 40 % double-bond character.

② No free rotation → planar

Six atoms lie in one plane. Only φ (N–Cα) and ψ (Cα–C) can rotate — which is precisely what the Ramachandran plot maps.

③ Almost always trans

The trans configuration avoids side-chain clash. The one common exception is the X–Pro bond, where cis becomes appreciable.

Easily confused

A disulfide is formed by oxidation, not condensation. That is why it appears in the oxidising environment of the ER lumen and the extracellular space; the cytosol is reducing (high glutathione), so stable cytosolic disulfides are rare.

§ 04

The four noncovalent interactions

The ideaThese are the temporary attractions. Any one of them is too feeble to survive the jostling of body heat, so none of them matters alone — they matter because there are hundreds of them at once, and two surfaces that fit each other closely can make far more of them than two surfaces that do not. What separates the four is reach and fussiness: how far away they can still pull, and whether they care about the angle they are approached from. Those two differences are what most questions are actually testing.

All four are weak and reversible, but each has a different distance dependence and a different sensitivity to its environment — and that is usually what the question is really testing.

① Ionic interaction / salt bridge

F ∝ q₁q₂ / (ε·r²) — the slowest fall-off with distance, so it is the longest-range of the four.

  • Dielectric constant ε: water ≈ 78.5, protein hydrophobic core ≈ 4 → a buried salt bridge is far stronger than a surface-exposed one.
  • Weakened by high salt (charge screening) and by pH change (side chains lose their charge).
  • Examples: the salt bridges stabilising the T state of haemoglobin; ion-exchange chromatography.

② Hydrogen bond

D–H···A, where D and A must be N, O or F — in biology, N and O. Length ≈ 2.6–3.5 Å.

  • Directional: strongest when D–H···A approaches 180°, which is what makes it a source of specificity.
  • One water molecule can take part in up to four H-bonds (2 donated, 2 accepted).
  • Examples: α-helix (i to i+4), β-sheet, base pairing (A=T two, G≡C three → higher GC% raises Tm).

③ Van der Waals forces

Three flavours: dipole–dipole (Keesom), dipole–induced dipole (Debye) and London dispersion (instantaneous dipoles — present between all atoms).

  • Attraction ∝ 1/r⁶, repulsion ∝ 1/r¹² (Lennard-Jones) → the steepest distance dependence, effective only at very close range.
  • Optimal separation = the van der Waals contact distance (sum of the two vdW radii); closer than that and it becomes repulsive.
  • One contact ≈ RT, so it wins by number — the physical basis of lock-and-key shape complementarity.

④ The hydrophobic effect

Strictly speaking not a force at all — it is the result of water squeezing nonpolar groups together so that it can keep its own hydrogen-bond network.

  • Driven by entropy, not enthalpy → see §06.
  • Together with van der Waals forces it is the dominant driver of protein folding.
  • Weakened by detergents (SDS), organic solvents, and — counter-intuitively — cooling.

The four, told apart by the one property each owns alone

RATE — Reach, Angle, Touch, Entropy

One word, four letters, in the order of the four cards above. Reach — the ionic interaction: F ∝ q₁q₂/(ε·r²) falls off most slowly of the four, so the salt bridge is the longest-reaching. Angle — the hydrogen bond is the only directional one, strongest as D–H···A approaches 180°, which is precisely why specificity is its job. Touch — van der Waals: 1/r⁶ attraction against 1/r¹² repulsion is the steepest law here, so it exists only at the contact distance and turns repulsive if the atoms are pushed any closer. Entropy — the hydrophobic effect is the only one driven by entropy: water regaining disorder, not any attraction between the greasy groups themselves. My own hook, not a circulating one. Read R·A·T·E as four discriminators — the one property each interaction owns that the other three do not — never as a ranking of strength; the hydrophobic effect has no simple distance law at all, which is why it gets a driver word rather than a reach word. The strength order needs no mnemonic either: it follows from how much charge is separated. Full charges (ionic 8–40) beat permanent partial charges with a hydrogen between them (H-bond 4–30) beat the fluctuating and induced dipoles of the van der Waals family (0.4–4) — which is why R, A and T happen to fall in descending strength — with the hydrophobic effect worth ≈3 kJ/mol per –CH₂– buried, a per-unit figure that does not sit on that scale at all. Derive it once and you never have to store it.

Exam technique · the FALSE-statement and matching items on noncovalent forces

  1. Read the cue word and jump straight to the interaction. 1/r² · longest-range · dielectric · ε · high salt · pH change → ionic. 180° · directional · specificity from geometry · urea · guanidinium HCl → hydrogen bond. 1/r⁶ · 1/r¹² · contact distance · repulsive if too close · shape complementarity · "wins by number" → van der Waals. Entropy · clathrate cage · released water · SDS · organic solvent · cold → hydrophobic effect. Most items on this topic are one cue and nothing else, which turns a 60-second question into a 15-second one.
  2. Know the two swaps the examiner actually uses. (i) Directionality moved off the hydrogen bond and onto van der Waals — van der Waals forces have no directionality, full stop. (ii) The hydrophobic effect relabelled as enthalpy-driven, or as "nonpolar molecules attracting one another". Either sentence appearing in a FALSE-statement item ends the question on the spot.
  3. The §10 table needs no mnemonic — it is one sentence applied four times. Every denaturant supplies exactly what its target is made of: urea and guanidinium HCl are rival hydrogen-bond partners; high salt is a charge screen and extreme pH removes the charge outright; SDS and organic solvents are a fake hydrophobic phase; DTT and β-mercaptoethanol are electrons. Ask what the interaction is made of and its breaker names itself. Mismatched pairs — "high salt breaks hydrogen bonds", "urea reduces disulfides" — are free eliminations.
  4. Recover the salt-bridge direction from the formula, not from memory. ε sits on the bottom of F ∝ q₁q₂/(ε·r²), and ε ≈ 4 in the protein core against ≈78.5 in water: a roughly twenty-fold smaller denominator means a roughly twenty-fold larger force, so buried beats surface-exposed. The arithmetic settles it faster and more reliably than recall does.
  5. Cold is the sting in this topic. Cooling weakens the hydrophobic effect, so cold denaturation is real. An option saying only heat denatures proteins, or that cooling always stabilises them, is wrong — and it reads exactly like textbook prose, which is why it survives a careless pass.

§ 05

Water and its hydrogen-bond network

The ideaWater is not a neutral backdrop that biology happens in front of; it is a participant with strong preferences of its own. Each water molecule can hold on to as many as four neighbours at once, so liquid water is a constantly rearranging web rather than a crowd of separate particles. Almost every odd thing about it — that it takes so much warming to heat up, that ice floats instead of sinking, that it pulls salts apart so easily — comes straight out of that one fact, and so does most of what the rest of this page describes.

Water is not the background, it is the protagonist. Every "anomalous" property of water follows from a single fact: one molecule can make up to four hydrogen bonds.

Everything that follows from four H-bonds

  • High boiling point, heat of vaporisation and specific heat — breaking the network costs energy, which is what buffers body temperature.
  • High surface tension — hence the need for pulmonary surfactant in the alveoli.
  • Ice floats — crystallising locks each molecule into an open tetrahedron of four H-bonds, lowering the density.
  • High dielectric constant (ε ≈ 78.5) — electrostatic attraction between ions is cut by almost 80-fold, so salts dissolve and salt bridges are weak in water.
  • Autoionisation — Kw = 10⁻¹⁴, so pH + pOH = 14.

One line to remember

An intramolecular hydrogen bond in water is close to a zero-sum trade — it must first compete with water. Hydrogen bonds mainly buy directionality and specificity; the bulk of the stability comes from the hydrophobic effect.

There is no hook for that list and you should not build one — a five-item hook here would be a second thing to remember that decays into noise, because every item is one step from the same sentence: one water molecule can make up to four hydrogen bonds. Run the chain instead, and it comes out in the order you need it. Four bonds per molecule → liquid water is a continuous network rather than a crowd of separate particles → tearing that network apart costs energy, which is high boiling point, high heat of vaporisation, high specific heat and high surface tension in one move → freezing locks each molecule into an open four-bond tetrahedron with space in the middle, so ice is less dense and floats → a dense network of strong dipoles screens charge, so ε ≈ 78.5, salts dissolve and salt bridges are weak in water → and the same O–H that donates a bond can also let its proton go, so Kw = 10⁻¹⁴ and pH + pOH = 14. The "four" itself is 2 + 2 and you can read it straight off the figure: two hydrogens to donate, two lone pairs to accept.

§ 06

The hydrophobic effect is entropy-driven

The ideaOil and water do not separate because the oil likes itself. They separate because the water is being forced to be tidy. Water cannot hold on to a greasy molecule, so it has to arrange itself into a neat cage around it, and neat arrangements are exactly what a liquid resists. Push the greasy pieces together and less cage is needed, so the trapped water is set free again — and it is that recovered freedom, not any pull between the greasy pieces, that drives the whole thing. This is the single most misunderstood idea on the page, and it is the reason proteins fold and membranes build themselves.

Drop a nonpolar molecule into water. Water cannot hydrogen-bond to it, so it arranges a highly ordered clathrate cage of water around it. The number of hydrogen bonds is not actually reduced — so ΔH can even be negative (exothermic!) — but the freedom of those water molecules collapses, so ΔS is negative.

Test it with ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Drag the temperature and watch the two terms compete:

Three things to read off this figure

  1. Dissolving methane or ethane in water is actually exothermic (ΔH < 0) and still does not happen — proof that entropy, not enthalpy, is in charge.
  2. Burying a nonpolar group is simply the reverse process: the ordered cage is released, ΔSwater rises sharply, ΔG goes negative, and it happens spontaneously.
  3. Lower the temperature and the −TΔS penalty shrinks → the hydrophobic effect weakens. This is the origin of cold denaturation.

The most common wrong statement

"Oil molecules attract each other strongly, so they clump together." No. Oil molecules only have weak van der Waals attraction for each other; the real driver is water recovering its own disorder. When a question asks what drives hydrophobic interaction, the answer is an increase in entropy.

What actually drives it, and which way every sign points

Water's freedom, not oil's friendship

Friendship is the wrong story: nonpolar groups have only weak van der Waals attraction for one another, and no question on this page is ever answered by it. Freedom is the right one: the water released from the cage gains entropy, and that is the whole driving force. Hang all three signs off that one line. Dissolving a nonpolar solute in water: ΔH ≈ 0 or even negative — methane and ethane genuinely are exothermic — while ΔS is strongly negative because of the clathrate cage, so −TΔS is strongly positive and ΔG > 0: it does not dissolve, in spite of the favourable enthalpy. Burying the same group is that identical process run backwards, so every sign flips — the cage is released, ΔSwater > 0, ΔG < 0, and it happens on its own with no energy supplied. Whenever a stem asks what drives hydrophobic interaction, the answer is an increase in entropy. My own phrasing, not a circulating hook; the content is this section's own "most common wrong statement" callout. It fixes the driver and the signs and deliberately nothing else — it does not rank the four interactions, and it must not be read as "heat is harmless". Warming actually strengthens the hydrophobic effect (−TΔS grows with T) while breaking hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts, which is why proteins can denature at both ends of the temperature range.

Exam technique · running ΔG = ΔH − TΔS here without a sign error

  1. Fix the direction before you touch a number. The tabulated ΔH and ΔS on this topic are for transfer into water, i.e. dissolving. Burying is the reverse reaction, so all three signs flip. Most of the marks lost on this section are lost in that single step, before any arithmetic happens at all.
  2. Units are both the trap and the check. ΔS is quoted in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ while ΔH and ΔG are in kJ·mol⁻¹, so TΔS must be divided by 1000. If your ΔG lands about a thousand times too large, you have located the slip in five seconds without re-deriving anything.
  3. The worked case, so you know what the right magnitude looks like. Methane at body temperature: ΔS = −87.9 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ → −TΔS = 310 × 87.9 / 1000 = +27.2 kJ/mol; with ΔH = −11.7 that gives ΔG = +15.5 kJ/mol, so it does not dissolve. Burying that same methane is −15.5 kJ/mol and is spontaneous. Tens of kJ/mol is the right order here; single digits or hundreds means you slipped a factor somewhere.
  4. Temperature direction, one line. −TΔS grows with T, so warming strengthens the hydrophobic effect and cooling weakens it. Any option that has the hydrophobic effect getting stronger in the cold is out, and so is any option claiming a protein is stable at all low temperatures — cold denaturation is exactly what the shrinking −TΔS term predicts.
  5. Never let a favourable ΔH decide spontaneity. Spontaneity is ΔG and only ΔG. "Dissolving methane in water is exothermic" is a true sentence and the wrong answer to "why does oil not mix with water" — the archetypal textbook-sounding distractor on this page. Check that the option answers the question actually asked.
  6. Worth the full 66 seconds. This and §09 are the only two places on the page where a stem can make you compute, and the computation is three keystrokes. Do it properly rather than guessing a sign from the shape of the options.
§ 07

Amphipathic molecules and their aggregates

The ideaSome molecules have a split personality: one end is content in water and the other end is not. Put enough of them into water and they sort themselves out with no help and no energy supplied — the water-avoiding ends bury themselves together in the middle, the water-loving ends face outwards. Which structure they settle into — a solid ball, a flat sheet, or a hollow bag with water on both sides — is decided by something as simple as whether the molecule is wider at the top or at the bottom. This is how every membrane in your body assembles itself.

Amphipathic (amphiphilic) means one molecule carries both a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail. Put it in water above its critical micelle concentration (CMC) and it aggregates spontaneously. Note that word — this is self-assembly; no energy input is required, because the driving force is the entropy gain of the hydrophobic effect.

Molecular shape dictates the geometry

This is the shortcut for the exam: compare the cross-sectional area of the head with that of the tails.

Inverted cone

Big head, small tail (single tail)
Lysophospholipids, fatty acid salts, SDS and other detergents
→ spherical micelle (positive curvature)

Cylinder

Exam point

Both are amphipathic, but single-tailed fatty acids and detergents form micelles while two-tailed phospholipids form bilayers. This is also why bile salts emulsify fat: they are facial amphipaths — hydrophilic on one face, hydrophobic on the other — and their job is to break large fat droplets into micelles, increasing the surface area available to pancreatic lipase.

Which aggregate a given amphipath forms

One tail balls up · two tails lie flat · fat tails fold back

One tail balls up — one tail means the head is wide relative to what hangs off it (an inverted cone), so the surface curves away from the tails and closes into a spherical micelle: SDS and other detergents, fatty acid salts, lysophospholipids, bile salts. Two tails lie flat — two tails make head and tails roughly equal in cross-section (a cylinder), which cannot pack into a sphere, so it lays down a planar bilayer: PC, PS, sphingomyelin. Fat tails fold back — when the tails outbulk the head (a cone), the sheet prefers to curve the other way: PE and cardiolipin, negative curvature, the HII phase, and hence their presence at sites of fusion and budding. My own line; the head-versus-tail area rule underneath it is the standard one and this section states it. Counting tails separates micelle from bilayer and that is all it does — it cannot separate PC from PE, since both carry two tails and only the head-to-tail area ratio decides between them, and cardiolipin has four tails yet is still a cone because its single head is small for that many. If the words "cone" and "inverted cone" ever confuse you, drop the names and compare the two cross-sections directly, exactly as this section instructs.

§ 08

The lipid map

The idea"Lipid" is not really a family of related structures, it is a shopping category: anything greasy enough to refuse to dissolve in water gets filed under the name. That is why cooking fat, the building blocks of membranes, cholesterol and a few vitamins all share one word while looking nothing like one another. Two questions cut the pile into usable groups: does the molecule contain the kind of join that soap-making can split, and does it carry a water-friendly end big enough to make it sit at a boundary rather than hide in the middle of a droplet?

The only thing all lipids share is insolubility in water and solubility in organic solvents — it is a classification by solubility, not by structure, which is why the structures underneath vary so wildly. Splitting them by saponifiable vs non-saponifiable is the cleanest cut.

Three membrane lipid families

Glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, cholesterol. Eukaryotic membranes carry all three; bacteria have no cholesterol (hopanoids play the equivalent role).

Membrane asymmetry

Outer leaflet: PC, sphingomyelin, glycolipids. Inner leaflet: PE, PS, PI. PS flipping outward is the "eat me" signal of apoptosis and the surface that initiates coagulation.

Which leaflet a lipid lives in — derived rather than listed

Choline and sugar face out; charge and ethanolamine face in

Choline out — the two phosphocholine-bearing lipids face out: PC and sphingomyelin, which is what they have in common despite one being a glycerophospholipid and the other a sphingolipid. Sugar out — sugars always face the outside of a cell, exactly as they do on a glycoprotein, so glycolipids are outer-leaflet; recognition has to happen where the cell can actually be recognised. Charge in — PS and PI carry net negative charge and face the cytosol. Ethanolamine in — PE is the ethanolamine one and goes inward with them. Then the payoff the examiners are after: PS appearing on the outer face is abnormal — it is the "eat me" signal of apoptosis and the surface on which coagulation is initiated. My own hook, built to make the list above derivable rather than memorised; it covers exactly the six lipids this section names and nothing beyond them. It answers only the leaflet question — for what each lipid actually does, and for anything about cholesterol, go back to the table.

Exam technique · identifying an unfamiliar lipid in four questions

  1. Backbone first: glycerol or sphingosine? Sphingosine makes it a sphingolipid, and its fatty acid is attached by an amide rather than an ester. That pairing — sphingosine + fatty acid = ceramide — is the core of every sphingolipid and a favourite one-line question in its own right.
  2. Then: phosphate? sugar? Phosphate → phospholipid. Sugar and no phosphate → glycolipid (cerebroside = one sugar; ganglioside = an oligosaccharide carrying sialic acid). Sphingosine and phosphate = sphingomyelin, the only molecule that is both a phospholipid and a sphingolipid — so it is the answer to any stem built around the word "both".
  3. No fatty acid on an ester or amide link → non-saponifiable. That is the entire cut, and it sorts the last three rows without memorising them: four fused rings → steroid; a 20-carbon fatty-acid derivative → eicosanoid; an isoprenoid → fat-soluble vitamin. The one list here genuinely worth holding is the standard A D E K, and it carries its own consequences — absorbed with bile salts and packed into chylomicrons, and able to accumulate to toxicity in a way the water-soluble vitamins cannot.
  4. Charge filter — one glance, no structure work. TAG and waxes have no polar head at all, so any option calling either amphipathic, membrane-forming or surface-active is out. Cholesterol has exactly one –OH, enough to orient it inside a bilayer and nowhere near enough to build one. Only the amphipaths make membranes.
  5. Check which job was asked about. Storage → TAG. Membrane structure → glycerophospholipids. Recognition → glycolipids, outer leaflet. Signalling → eicosanoids. The standard distractor here is a perfectly true textbook sentence about the wrong one of those four, so name the job before you pick the option.
  6. Ten seconds, then move on. This section is a catalogue, and a catalogue item you cannot do in ten seconds you will not do in sixty either. Eliminate whatever the filters above kill, answer from what survives — never blank once even one option is gone — flag it and come back.
§ 09

Saturation → melting point → membrane fluidity

The ideaThe greasy tails of a fat are long, floppy chains. Straight chains stack against one another like pencils in a box, and it takes a lot of warmth to shake them loose, so the fat is a solid at room temperature — butter. Put a permanent bend in the middle of the chain and the neat stacking fails, the chains slide past each other easily, and the very same kind of fat is a liquid — olive oil. A cell membrane is built from those same chains, so the same rule decides whether the membrane behaves more like oil or more like wax, and a membrane that is too stiff or too runny stops working.

A fatty acid has only two variables: chain length and number (and geometry) of double bonds. Through "how tightly can they stack", those two set the melting point — and the melting point sets membrane fluidity.

Melting points of common fatty acids (°C). The dashed line is body temperature, 37 °C — anything to its right is solid at body temperature, anything to its left is liquid.

Look at the 18-carbon series: 18:0 → 18:1 → 18:2 → 18:3. Every additional cis double bond drops the melting point by a large step, at constant chain length.

Why a cis double bond matters so much

Trans fatty acids (from partial hydrogenation) are geometrically almost straight, so they behave like saturated fat — high melting point, tight packing — while also raising LDL and lowering HDL, which is why they are judged more harshly than saturated fat.

Cholesterol: a bidirectional fluidity buffer

Its rigid ring system slots between the phospholipid tails with the 3-OH aligned to the head groups. The effect depends on which side of Tm you are on:

ConditionWhat cholesterol does
T > Tm (membrane fluid)Restrains acyl-chain motion → decreases fluidity
T < Tm (membrane gel-like)Wedges in and blocks ordered crystalline packing → increases fluidity

Net result: cholesterol keeps the membrane neither too fluid nor too rigid, and lowers permeability to small molecules and ions. With sphingomyelin it also concentrates into lipid rafts.

Membrane fluidity — everything in one place

  • ↑ fluidity: higher temperature, more cis unsaturation, shorter chains, cholesterol below Tm.
  • ↓ fluidity: lower temperature, more saturation, longer chains, cholesterol above Tm, high sphingomyelin content.
  • Lipids diffuse laterally very fast, but flip-flop is extremely slow and needs flippase / floppase / scramblase — usually at the cost of ATP.

The 18-carbon series: double bonds against ω number

Down the 18s: nine, six, three — one more double bond, three off ω

Count the double bonds up and the ω number down: 18:1 → ω-9 (oleic), 18:2 → ω-6 (linoleic), 18:3 → ω-3 (α-linolenic). One more double bond, three fewer in the ω number, every single time — and the two that are essential are the last two, because humans lack the Δ12 and Δ15 desaturases needed to put a double bond in beyond C9. Reading the chart above, those same three are 13 °C, −5 °C and −11 °C, so the ω number and the melting point fall together. My own hook; the numbers are this section's own. It holds only inside the 18-carbon series — arachidonic acid is 20:4 and ω-6, not the ω-0 a naive extension would predict, because the chain length changed. The deeper rule is the one actually worth keeping: elongation adds carbons at the carboxyl end and mammalian desaturases count from that end too, so the ω end is never touched and the ω number never changes for the life of a molecule. That is exactly why linoleic acid (ω-6) can become arachidonic acid (ω-6), and why no amount of metabolism turns an ω-6 into an ω-3.

Cholesterol's direction — the only part of the fluidity list you cannot derive

Brake above Tm, antifreeze below

Above Tm the membrane is already fluid, and cholesterol's rigid ring system restrains acyl-chain motion: it is a brake, and it decreases fluidity. Below Tm the membrane is trying to crystallise, and cholesterol wedges between the tails and blocks ordered packing: it is antifreeze, and it increases fluidity. Same molecule, opposite sign, decided entirely by which side of Tm the stem has put you on. My own hook. It carries the direction and nothing more — cholesterol also lowers permeability to small molecules and ions, and with sphingomyelin concentrates into lipid rafts, and neither of those is in the line. The rest of the fluidity list needs no mnemonic. Temperature, chain length, cis unsaturation and sphingomyelin content all read off a single question — can the tails stack? Anything that helps them stack lowers fluidity; anything that stops them raises it. Cholesterol is the sole entry that answers that question both ways, which is precisely why it is the one that needs a hook.

Exam technique · ranking fatty acids, then reading it straight into fluidity

  1. Count double bonds before you count carbons. In the range this paper uses, one cis double bond outweighs six carbons: 18:1 melts at 13 °C while 12:0 melts at 44 °C. So sort on unsaturation first, and use chain length only to break ties between equally unsaturated chains.
  2. Carry the step sizes, because they are wildly unequal. Each extra pair of carbons is worth roughly +7 to +10 °C (12:0 44 → 14:0 54 → 16:0 63 → 18:0 70), but the first cis double bond costs about −57 °C (18:0 70 → 18:1 13) and the second and third only about −18 and −6. So 18:0-versus-18:1 is a chasm while 18:2-versus-18:3 is a nudge — which tells you how much weight to put on a close-looking pair of options, and where a careless ranking will actually cost you.
  3. trans is the intended error. A trans double bond is geometrically almost straight, so it packs and melts like a saturated chain. Any option that ranks purely by "number of double bonds" without checking the geometry is the distractor — and conversely, a stem that troubles itself to write cis is telling you the geometry is the point.
  4. Read across; do not learn a second rule. Lower melting point → looser packing → more fluid membrane. The entire fluidity callout above is that one sentence repeated, with cholesterol as the single exception that needs its own direction.
  5. The 37 °C sanity check, free of charge. Anything melting above 37 °C is solid inside you: palmitic (63) and stearic (70) are, oleic (13) and everything below it is not. If your reasoning implies a membrane of pure 18:0 would work, it would not — it would be wax at body temperature, and that is exactly why real membranes are built with unsaturated tails.
  6. One cue, one whole question. "Grown at low temperature" → the organism raises cis unsaturation and shortens its chains, because it has to keep the membrane fluid. "Membrane too rigid" → add cis double bonds, shorten the chains, or raise the temperature. Both are the same packing rule read backwards, and recognising the direction answers the item in fifteen seconds without touching a single melting point.
Go deeper · Essential fatty acids: why ω-6 and ω-3 must come from the diet

Essential fatty acids

Humans lack Δ12 and Δ15 desaturases and cannot introduce double bonds beyond C9, so linoleic acid (18:2, ω-6) and α-linolenic acid (18:3, ω-3) must come from the diet. Arachidonic acid (20:4, ω-6) can be made from linoleic acid, so it is only conditionally essential.

§ 10

What breaks what

The ideaEvery attraction described on this page has one particular weakness, so a question about wrecking a protein is really a matching exercise: pair each attraction with the thing that undoes it. Warmth and rival molecules break up the flimsy attractions; making the surroundings more acidic or more alkaline switches off the electrically charged ones; soap-like molecules get in among the greasy core and prise it open. The permanent joins of the chain itself survive all of it — which is why a boiled egg is ruined but not chopped into pieces.

The standard way to answer a denaturation question — map each interaction directly onto the thing that destroys it. Denaturation destroys noncovalent interactions and disulfides; the peptide bond (primary structure) is untouched.

Tying it together: why SDS-PAGE needs two reagents

SDS destroys the hydrophobic core and coats the protein with negative charge in proportion to its length; β-ME or DTT reduces disulfides so that subunits separate. Leave either one out and the band you measure is not the true subunit mass. This question ties §03, §04 and §10 together and is a very typical integration item.
