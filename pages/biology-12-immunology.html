Biology · Note 12

Immunology

An immune system has to solve one problem that has no clean solution: it must destroy anything that should not be there, without knowing in advance what that will be, and without destroying you. Evolution's answer is two systems bolted together. The innate system is fast, is encoded in the genome you were born with, and recognises broad chemical signatures shared by whole classes of microbe. The adaptive system is slow the first time, builds its receptors by cutting and re-joining its own DNA, and therefore can recognise molecules that have never existed before — including ones that will not exist for another thousand years. Almost every fact on this page falls out of that division of labour, plus one further idea: the antigen never instructs anything. It selects from a repertoire that was already there.

§ 01

Two systems, one problem

The ideaYou are a warm, wet, nutrient-rich container that every microbe on Earth would like to live inside. Defending it means answering two demands that pull against each other: be quick, because bacteria double every twenty minutes, and be exact, because the thing you are attacking is made of the same chemistry you are. Nothing can be both at once, so the body runs two systems. One is ready on day one but only knows a short list of danger signatures. The other can learn anything, but takes a week to get going the first time — and it never forgets.

Definition & key facts

Immunity
The capacity to recognise material as foreign, damaged or altered and to remove it, while leaving healthy self tissue intact. The second half of that sentence is the hard half, and § 11 is what happens when it fails.
innate = fast · germline-encoded · pattern-based · no memory adaptive = slow first time · somatically generated · antigen-specific · memory
Full definition, symbols and units
Antigen
Any molecule that an antigen receptor — an antibody or a T-cell receptor — can bind. An immunogen is an antigen that also provokes a response; the two are not synonyms, and a hapten is the standard counterexample.
Epitope (antigenic determinant)
The small patch actually contacted by one receptor, typically 5–15 amino acids or a few sugar residues. One protein carries many epitopes, which is why even a "monospecific" antiserum is a mixture.
Lymphocyte
The cell type that carries a somatically rearranged antigen receptor: B cells (bone-marrow matured, become antibody factories) and T cells (thymus matured, help or kill). Natural killer cells look like lymphocytes but carry no rearranged receptor and belong to the innate system.
Cytokine
A small secreted protein by which one leukocyte changes another's behaviour — interleukins, interferons, tumour necrosis factors, chemokines. Short range, short half-life, very high potency; this is why immune signalling can be catastrophic when it becomes systemic.

The trade-off is worth stating precisely, because the exam tests it as a comparison rather than as two separate lists. The innate system's receptors are inherited: every human being has the same Toll-like receptor 4, tuned to the same bacterial lipopolysaccharide, and it works within minutes because nothing has to be built. The price is that the number of things it can recognise is fixed at birth and is small — a few dozen molecular patterns. The adaptive system's receptors are manufactured in each lymphocyte by cutting and re-joining its own DNA, so the number of things it can recognise is astronomically large. The price is that this happens before the antigen is known, so almost every lymphocyte is useless for any particular infection, and finding the rare useful one and growing it into an army takes about a week.

The two are not parallel alternatives; they are wired in series. Innate recognition is what licenses the adaptive response. A dendritic cell that has engaged a Toll-like receptor matures, migrates to a lymph node, and switches on the co-stimulatory molecules a T cell needs (§ 08). Without that innate signal the same antigen is presented in silence and the T cell is switched off rather than on. This is exactly why a purified protein injected alone is a poor immunogen and the same protein plus an adjuvant — which does nothing except supply synthetic danger — is a good one.

Two textbook sentences that are not quite true

"Innate immunity is non-specific." It is specific — just not clonally specific. TLR5 responds to flagellin and to nothing else; TLR4 will not look at flagellin. The honest phrasing is that innate receptors are specific for shared patterns rather than for individual epitopes, and that the specificity is not adjustable. If an option says innate immunity "responds identically to all foreign material", reject it.

"Innate immunity has no memory." This is the standard answer and it is what an exam wants, but it is now known to be incomplete. Trained immunity — epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming of monocytes and NK cells after BCG vaccination or exposure to fungal β-glucan — gives weeks-to-months of heightened, non-specific responsiveness. It is not clonal and not antigen-specific, so it is not adaptive memory; but "none at all" is too strong.

Where things happen

Primary lymphoid organs are where lymphocytes are born and vetted — bone marrow and thymus — and no antigen from outside is supposed to be there. Secondary lymphoid organs are where lymphocytes and antigen are deliberately introduced to each other — lymph nodes for tissue lymph, spleen for blood, MALT for mucosal surfaces. A swollen lymph node is a clone being grown, not an infection of the node.

§ 02

Innate I: barriers, pattern recognition and the cells that eat

The ideaMost defence is not immunology at all — it is plumbing and chemistry. Skin is a dry, salty, acidic, dead wall; mucus is flypaper on an escalator; tears carry an enzyme that dissolves bacterial cell walls; the stomach is a vat of acid. Only when something gets past all of that does a cell have to make a decision, and the decision is made with inherited receptors that read chemical signatures no animal cell ever displays. What follows is either eating the intruder or killing the cell that is harbouring it.

Definition & key facts

Pattern-recognition receptor (PRR)
A germline-encoded receptor that binds a PAMP — a molecule that is essential to a class of microbe and absent from the host. Toll-like receptors are the best-known family; there are also cytosolic NOD-like and RIG-I-like receptors and the soluble mannose-binding lectin.
the innate question is not "what is this?" but "is this pattern one of ours?" recognition of the microbial, not of the individual microbe
Full definition, symbols and units
Phagocytosis
Engulfment into a phagosome, which fuses with a lysosome to give a phagolysosome. Killing uses the respiratory burst (NADPH oxidase → superoxide → H₂O₂ → hypochlorite via myeloperoxidase), nitric oxide, low pH, lysozyme and defensins.
Opsonisation
Coating a particle with a molecule a phagocyte has a receptor for — C3b (receptor CR1) or IgG (receptor FcγR). It does not kill anything; it converts a slippery target into a grippable one, raising uptake by orders of magnitude.
Natural killer (NK) cell
A large granular lymphocyte with no rearranged antigen receptor. Its inhibitory receptors read self MHC class I; where that signal is weak or absent, activating receptors win and the cell kills by perforin and granzyme.
Lysozyme
An enzyme in tears, saliva, mucus and egg white that hydrolyses the β(1→4) bond between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetylglucosamine in peptidoglycan — so it is far more effective against Gram-positive bacteria, whose peptidoglycan is exposed.

Why the nucleic-acid sensors sit inside endosomes

TLR3, 7, 8 and 9 recognise RNA and DNA — molecules you are also made of. Putting them on the cell surface would be suicidal, because host DNA released by ordinary cell death would set them off. Instead they are placed on the inside of endosomes, where the only nucleic acid that arrives is nucleic acid that was swallowed. Location does the discriminating that chemistry cannot. This is a general principle worth carrying: much of self/non-self discrimination in the innate system is really compartment discrimination. It also explains the disease that follows when it breaks — in systemic lupus erythematosus, immune complexes ferry self DNA into endosomes and switch on TLR9 as though it were bacterial.

Which Toll-like receptor reads which pattern

DEF from 3 — dsRNA, endotoxin, flagellin · then 9 is a g: CpG DNA

Ours rather than a standard hook, but it self-checks: three consecutive letters for three consecutive receptors, so a dropped item leaves an audible gap. Start the run at TLR3 and walk D → E → F up 3 → 4 → 5. D = double-stranded RNA at TLR3 — read the D as ds, not DNA; the DNA sensor is the 9. E = endotoxin at TLR4, which is the LPS of the Gram-negative outer membrane. F = flagellin at TLR5 — five and flagellin share their F. Then the run breaks and you jump to TLR9 → unmethylated CpG DNA, held by the shape of the digit: a printed 9 is a lower-case g, and G is the letter that names the ligand. Four receptors, four ligands — one alphabetical run and one outlier. It deliberately leaves out TLR2 (lipoteichoic acid and peptidoglycan) and TLR7/8 (ssRNA), so do not treat it as the whole family. And it says nothing about where each receptor sits — that part needs no hook at all, because the paragraph above already gives you the rule: anything that reads nucleic acid has to be hidden inside an endosome or your own DNA would set it off. Hence 3, 7, 8 and 9 endosomal, 2, 4 and 5 on the surface, rebuildable from the principle every time.

Natural killer cells and the logic of "missing self"

Cytotoxic T cells have an elegant weakness. They can only kill a cell whose MHC class I molecules are presenting the right peptide — so a virus that simply removes MHC class I from the infected cell's surface becomes invisible to them. Many viruses do exactly this (cytomegalovirus, adenovirus, HIV), and so do many tumours. NK cells exist to close that hole. Their inhibitory receptors (the KIR family) bind self MHC class I; as long as that signal arrives, the killing programme is vetoed. Take MHC class I away and the veto is lifted, so the cell that has hidden from the T cell has thereby exposed itself to the NK cell.

Two things follow that examiners like. First, NK killing uses the same machinery as a cytotoxic T cell — perforin pores and granzyme proteases driving apoptosis — so the weapon is not what distinguishes them; the trigger is. Second, an NK cell can also be aimed by antibody: antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity works because CD16 on the NK cell grips the Fc of IgG already stuck to a target. That is an innate cell being pointed by an adaptive molecule, and it is the mechanism through which several therapeutic monoclonal antibodies actually work.

Killing by apoptosis, not by bursting

Both NK cells and cytotoxic T cells force the target into apoptosis: granzyme B enters through perforin pores and activates the caspase cascade; the Fas ligand pathway does the same thing by receptor engagement. This matters biologically, not just as a label. Apoptosis packages the cell's contents — including the viral genome, and the host DNA already chopped up by caspase-activated DNase — into membrane-bound bodies that macrophages swallow whole. Necrotic bursting would release intact infectious virions into the tissue. If an option says a cytotoxic T cell "lyses the target osmotically like complement", it has confused the two mechanisms; complement's membrane attack complex does lyse osmotically, but a CTL does not.

Exam technique · innate-cell stems, decided from one cue

  1. "The virus down-regulates or removes MHC class I" → NK cell. This single cue is worth recognising on sight, because it converts a reasoning question into a reflex. The same cue also kills two options for you: a cytotoxic T cell needs class I (less of it is strictly worse for it), and a helper T cell is irrelevant because an infected fibroblast or epithelial cell has no class II at all.
  2. "Helminth", "too large to be engulfed", "major basic protein" → eosinophil. Any option that has a phagocyte swallowing a multicellular parasite is wrong on size alone — the eosinophil degranulates onto it from outside.
  3. "Primes a naive T cell" → dendritic cell, and nothing else. Macrophages and B cells present to T cells that are already primed; only the dendritic cell starts one from naive. If a stem says naive and an option says macrophage, that option is dead.
  4. Use the differential as a two-way switch. Neutrophilia → bacterial (they are 60–70 % of leukocytes and die within hours); lymphocytosis → viral. Eosinophilia → parasites or allergy. That is three stem-to-answer links for the price of one table.
  5. Kill anything that makes a killer lymphocyte lyse its target osmotically. NK cells and cytotoxic T cells both use perforin plus granzyme to drive apoptosis; osmotic lysis belongs to complement's membrane attack complex (§ 03). This is the classic pair of distractors in this section, and the phrasing is often lifted straight from textbook prose about the MAC and attached to the wrong cell — it will sound familiar, and that is exactly the trap.
§ 03

Innate II: complement, inflammation and fever

The ideaBlood carries a set of about thirty proteins that do nothing at all until one of them is cut. Cutting the first one lets it cut the next, and so on, so a single event on a bacterial surface becomes thousands of molecules deposited within seconds. That amplifying cascade does three jobs — it paints the microbe so phagocytes can grip it, it screams for help, and if the target is thin-walled enough it drills a hole in it. Inflammation and fever are the same idea scaled up to a tissue and to a whole body: change the local conditions so that defence works better and the invader works worse.

Definition & key facts

Complement
A cascade of ~30 plasma proteins, made mainly by the liver, circulating as inactive zymogens. Three activation routes converge on one enzyme, C3 convertase, and everything downstream is shared. It is an innate system even though one of its three triggers is an antibody.
classical · lectin · alternative → C3 convertase → C3a + C3b → C5 → MAC three ways in, one hub, three ways out: inflammation, opsonisation, lysis
Full definition, symbols and units
Classical pathway
Triggered when C1q binds the Fc of antibody already bound to antigen. One IgM pentamer suffices; IgG needs two molecules landing close enough together for C1q's six arms to bridge them — which is why IgM is by far the more efficient complement fixer per molecule. C1r and C1s then cleave C4 and C2 to build C4b2a.
Lectin pathway
Mannose-binding lectin recognises the terminal mannose patterns of microbial glycans (mammalian glycans are capped with sialic acid instead) and its associated proteases, MASP-1/2, cleave C4 and C2 exactly as C1s does. No antibody required.
Alternative pathway
C3 hydrolyses spontaneously at a low rate ("tickover"). On a host cell the resulting C3b is instantly inactivated by regulators; on a microbial surface, which has none, it is stabilised, recruits factors B and D and builds C3bBb. This is a surface-discrimination mechanism: the pathway is always running, and self is defined as whatever can switch it off.
Membrane attack complex (MAC)
C5b recruits C6, C7 and C8, and C8 polymerises 10–18 molecules of C9 into a transmembrane pore roughly 10 nm across. Water and ions enter, the cell lyses osmotically.

The classical pathway's numbering is famously out of order — activation runs C1 → C4 → C2 → C3 — because the components were numbered as they were purified, decades before the sequence was known. Do not try to derive the order from the numbers; learn the picture instead.

The classical order, without storing four loose digits

the convertase spells it — C4b2a: 4 before 2

Our own phrasing, and it is really a derivation rather than a hook, which is why it will not decay. You have to know the name of the classical C3 convertase anyway, and the name carries the sequence inside it: C4b2a is assembled from C4 first and C2 second. Put C1 in front of it — C1q is the trigger, C1r and C1s are the knife — and C3 after it, since C3 is what the convertase exists to cut, and the full activation order C1 → C4 → C2 → C3 falls out with nothing arbitrary left to remember. It covers the classical and lectin entrances only. The alternative pathway is not an exception so much as the same trick again: its convertase is called C3bBb, which tells you C3b lands first and factor B is recruited onto it.

Which fragment does which job, from the letter alone

small a = anaphylatoxin, floats away · big b = binds, stays stuck

This is the standard letter rule, and it is worth using rather than inventing anything because it generalises across the cascade instead of being one fact about C3. Every cleavage throws off a small a fragment and leaves a large b fragment. C3a and C5a diffuse away and inflame — mast-cell degranulation, vasodilation, raised permeability, and for C5a the strongest neutrophil chemoattractant known. C3b and C4b stay on the surface where they were generated: C3b is the major opsonin that CR1 grips, and both are structural parts of the next enzyme in the chain. So "opsonin" always means a b, and "chemotaxis, shock, wheal" always means an a. One place not to lean on it: C2's fragments are conventionally named the other way round, so in C4b2a the enzymatic piece is 2a even though it is the large one. For C3, C4 and C5 — which is where the questions are — the rule is safe.

"The classical pathway needs antibody, so complement is adaptive"

No. The complement proteins are germline-encoded, identical in every person, present before any infection, and incapable of changing their specificity or remembering anything — every criterion for innate. What the classical pathway shows is that complement is a shared effector arm that either system can call. The adaptive system does not build a new weapon for each antigen; it builds a new targeting device (the antibody) and then hands the target to innate machinery — complement, phagocytes, NK cells. Hold on to that: most of what an antibody achieves, it achieves through its Fc, by recruiting something innate.

Inflammation: four signs, one purpose

Damaged tissue and activated mast cells release histamine, and C3a and C5a amplify it. Histamine does two things to local vessels: it dilates arterioles and it opens the junctions between capillary endothelial cells. Every classical sign follows mechanically from those two changes. More blood flow gives rubor (redness) and calor (heat). Fluid leaking into the interstitium gives tumor (swelling), and the pressure of that fluid together with bradykinin and prostaglandins sensitising nociceptors gives dolor (pain). Galen's fifth sign, functio laesa, loss of function, is the consequence.

The point of the leak is delivery. Plasma proteins — complement, antibody, clotting factors, acute-phase proteins — cannot reach the tissue through an intact capillary wall, and neutrophils cannot cross one either. So the sequence at the vessel wall is: margination (cells drift to the wall as flow slows), rolling on selectins induced on the endothelium, firm adhesion through integrins binding ICAM-1, diapedesis between the endothelial cells, and finally chemotaxis up the C5a and chemokine gradient. Knock out the integrin subunit CD18 and you get leukocyte adhesion deficiency: neutrophils are made and circulate in large numbers but cannot leave the blood, so wounds do not form pus and do not heal.

Fever is a raised set point, not a broken thermostat

This is the single most misread item in the section. Bacterial LPS and other exogenous pyrogens do not heat you. They cause macrophages to release the endogenous pyrogens IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α, which act on the preoptic area of the anterior hypothalamus and induce prostaglandin E₂. PGE₂ raises the hypothalamic set point — say from 37.0 °C to 39.0 °C. Everything else is normal thermoregulation doing its job against the new target: you feel cold, you shiver, you vasoconstrict, you seek blankets, until the body reaches the new set point. When the pyrogen clears, the set point drops, you are now too hot by your own reckoning, and you sweat and flush. Aspirin and other NSAIDs work by inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase, so less PGE₂ is made and the set point falls back — they do not cool you directly.

Contrast hyperthermia (heat stroke, malignant hyperthermia), where the set point is normal and heat production or environmental load simply overwhelms dissipation. There, antipyretics are useless and physical cooling is the treatment. A moderate fever is useful: it slows the growth of many pathogens whose optimum is 37 °C, drives hepatic sequestration of iron and zinc away from bacteria, and speeds phagocytosis and lymphocyte proliferation. Above about 40.6 °C the cost exceeds the benefit — seizures in small children, protein denaturation beyond roughly 41–42 °C.

Interferons: the neighbours, not the patient

A virus-infected cell secretes type I interferon (IFN-α/β). It does not save that cell — the cell is usually already doomed — it acts on surrounding uninfected cells, which respond by making PKR, oligoadenylate synthetase and Mx proteins and thereby enter an antiviral state in which translation of viral messages is blocked. So interferon builds a firebreak. Two further points get examined. Interferon is not virus-specific — it is induced by one virus and protects against many — but it is largely species-specific, which is why animal interferon is useless in humans. And type II interferon, IFN-γ, is a different molecule with a different job: made by NK cells and TH1 cells, its main role is to activate macrophages and raise MHC expression, making it a bridge into the adaptive response rather than a direct antiviral.

Go deeper · why complement does not dissolve you, and what happens when the brakes fail

The alternative pathway is always ticking over, depositing C3b indiscriminately on any surface it touches, including yours. Self-protection is therefore not a matter of avoiding deposition but of actively removing it, and host cells carry a set of regulators that microbes lack:

  • Factor H — binds sialic acid and heparan sulfate, which are abundant on host cells and rare on bacteria, then accelerates decay of C3bBb and helps factor I degrade C3b. Mutations cause atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome and are a major risk factor for age-related macular degeneration.
  • CD55 (decay-accelerating factor) — dismantles the C3 convertase on host membranes.
  • CD59 (protectin) — blocks C9 polymerisation, so no MAC forms.
  • C1 inhibitor — restrains C1r/C1s and, importantly, also restrains the contact (kallikrein–bradykinin) system.

Each brake has a disease attached, and they are the neatest way to remember what each protein does. Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria is a somatic mutation in PIGA that removes the GPI anchor used by both CD55 and CD59, so red cells lose their MAC protection and are lysed by ordinary tickover — treated with eculizumab, an anti-C5 monoclonal that prevents the MAC from ever forming. Hereditary angioedema is C1-inhibitor deficiency; the swelling is driven mostly by unrestrained bradykinin rather than by histamine, which is why it does not respond to antihistamines or adrenaline and why it is dangerous to treat it as anaphylaxis.

Deficiencies of the terminal components C5–C9 are strikingly narrow in their consequences: recurrent Neisseria infections and almost nothing else. That single observation tells you what the MAC is actually for. Most bacteria are killed by phagocytosis after opsonisation; the MAC matters only for organisms with thin, exposed outer membranes, and Neisseria is the clinically important example.

§ 04

What makes adaptive immunity adaptive

The ideaHere is the fact that organises everything from here to the end of the page: your body makes its enormous collection of different antigen receptors before it has any idea what it will need to fight. Each lymphocyte gets one receptor, at random, and then waits. When a pathogen arrives it does not teach anything; it simply happens to fit a handful of the millions of waiting cells, and those cells — and only those — are told to divide. Specificity, diversity, memory and even self-tolerance all fall out of that one design choice.

Definition & key facts

Clonal selection
Burnet's principle: a huge repertoire of lymphocytes, each with a single, randomly generated specificity, exists before antigen. Antigen selects the few that fit and drives their proliferation into effector and memory clones. The antigen is a filter, never a template.

Penicillin allergy (the β-lactam ring haptenises serum proteins) and poison-ivy dermatitis (urushiol haptenises skin proteins) are the standard examples.
Central tolerance
Deletion of strongly self-reactive lymphocytes during development, in the bone marrow (B cells) and the thymus (T cells). Thymic medullary epithelium expresses the transcriptional regulator AIRE, which forces ectopic expression of tissue-restricted proteins — insulin, thyroglobulin — so that T cells reactive to them can be deleted before they ever leave.

Everything that catches what central tolerance missed: anergy (antigen without co-stimulation switches the cell off, § 08), suppression by regulatory T cells, and deletion of chronically stimulated cells.

Why "the antigen instructs the antibody" is the wrong answer, and why it once looked right

Before 1957 the leading model was instructional: an antibody was thought to be a generic polypeptide that folded around the antigen, using it as a template, the way a glove takes the shape of a hand. It explained specificity beautifully and it was completely wrong. Three observations killed it. Denatured and refolded antibody regains its original specificity with no antigen present, so the shape is encoded in the sequence. Sequencing showed that antibodies of different specificity differ in amino-acid sequence, which no folding model can produce. And memory makes no sense under instruction — if the antigen shapes the protein, why would a second exposure be faster?

This matters because instructional language survives in casual writing and therefore in distractors. Sentences like "the antigen causes the B cell to produce a matching antibody", "the lymphocyte adapts its receptor to fit the pathogen" or "exposure to a new antigen creates a new specificity" all sound like textbook prose and are all instructional. The selectionist replacements are: the antigen finds a cell that already fits; the receptor was fixed before the encounter and never changes its specificity thereafter; new specificities arise only by generating new cells.

One qualification you should be able to make

There is exactly one process in which an antigen-receptor sequence changes after the encounter: somatic hypermutation in the germinal centre (§ 07). But look at what it is: random point mutation followed by selection for the cells that now bind better. It is Darwinian, not instructional — the antigen still does not write the sequence; it only decides which accidents survive. If a question asks whether antibody specificity can improve during a response, the answer is yes, by mutation and selection; if it asks whether the antigen directs the change, the answer is no.

Exam technique · the one-word filter for any clonal-selection stem

  1. Find the verb, then ask who is acting. If an option makes the antigen the actor — it "instructs", "causes the B cell to produce a matching antibody", "makes the lymphocyte adapt its receptor", "creates a new specificity" — it is instructional and it is wrong, whatever else it says. Run this filter before you assess the biology; it usually kills two options in under ten seconds.
  2. Check that the surviving option puts the repertoire first. The correct shape is always antigen finds / selects / binds a cell that already fitted, and that cell then divides. Repertoire → selection → expansion → effector plus memory; any option that reverses an arrow in that chain is a distractor built on purpose.
  3. Hold the single legal exception. Somatic hypermutation does change a receptor sequence after the encounter — but by random mutation followed by selection, so the antigen still writes nothing. If the stem asks whether affinity can improve during a response, the answer is yes; if it asks whether the antigen directs the change, the answer is no. Read which of the two is being asked.
  4. Expect the wording to sound like a textbook. Instructional phrasing survives in casual writing, so these distractors have been lifted from prose that reads perfectly well. Familiarity is not evidence here — the test is only ever "who is the subject of the sentence".
  5. Budget ten seconds, not sixty. Clonal-selection items are pure recognition once the filter is automatic. Bank the time for § 06 and § 09, where there is arithmetic to do.

Two receptors, two entirely different ways of seeing

This distinction is worth more marks than almost anything else on the page. A B-cell receptor (and the antibody it later secretes) binds antigen as it is: free in solution, on a cell surface, in whatever three-dimensional shape it happens to have. It therefore recognises conformational epitopes — residues that are far apart in the sequence but adjacent in the folded protein — and it is not restricted to proteins at all. Polysaccharide capsules, lipids and nucleic acids are all perfectly good B-cell antigens.

A T-cell receptor can do none of this. It binds only a short linear peptide that has been chopped out of a protein and loaded into the groove of an MHC molecule on another cell's surface, and it contacts the MHC molecule as much as the peptide. Three consequences follow immediately, and all three are examined. T cells cannot respond to pure polysaccharides or lipids, because those cannot be presented in an MHC groove — which is why capsule vaccines need conjugation (§ 07). T cells cannot act on a free-floating toxin; they act only on cells. And T cells are MHC-restricted: the same peptide presented by a different person's MHC is, to that T cell, a different ligand entirely (§ 08).

Antibody sees the sculpture; the T cell sees the rubble

An antibody looks at an intact object from outside. A T cell is shown fragments of what a cell has been building or eating, held up in a standard frame. So anything that changes an antigen's shape (heat, denaturation) destroys most B-cell epitopes but leaves T-cell epitopes untouched — the peptides are chopped out anyway.

Tolerance is a subtraction, and it is expensive

If receptors are generated at random, a large fraction of them will fit something you are made of. The system deals with this by deleting the offenders before they are released. In the thymus a developing T cell must pass two opposite tests: positive selection, in which it must bind self MHC at least weakly or it dies by neglect (this is where MHC restriction is imposed), and negative selection, in which it must not bind self peptide–MHC too strongly or it is deleted. Only about 2 % of thymocytes satisfy both, which tells you how blunt the generating mechanism is. B cells face an analogous screen in the marrow, with the additional option of receptor editing — a self-reactive B cell may re-rearrange its light chain and try again.

Tolerance is therefore learned, not inherited, and it is learned against whatever is present at the time. That has a clean experimental consequence, and it makes a good stem: a foreign antigen introduced during fetal or neonatal life is treated as self for the rest of the animal's life (Medawar's neonatal tolerance experiments). It also explains a whole category of autoimmunity — tissues the thymus never sampled, such as the lens of the eye, sperm produced only after puberty, or myelin basic protein behind the blood–brain barrier, remain sequestered antigens against which no tolerance was ever established.

§ 05

The antibody molecule and its five classes

The ideaAn antibody is one molecule doing two unrelated jobs at the two ends of a Y. The tips are variable — that is where the astronomical diversity lives, and it is the only part that touches the antigen. The stem is constant within a class, and it is the part that other cells and molecules read: it says what should now be done about whatever the tips have caught. Learn the molecule as tips and stem, and the five classes stop being a list to memorise — they become five different instructions attached to the same grabber.

The five classes, derived rather than listed

Ask what each Fc binds and the biology writes itself. IgM's Fc, presented five times over on a pentamer, is a superb C1q platform — so IgM is the complement fixer. And IgD's Fc binds essentially nothing in serum — so IgD is a membrane molecule, not a soluble effector.

"IgM is too big to cross the placenta"

Very common, and it gets the right answer for the wrong reason — which means it fails the moment the question is rephrased. Placental transfer is not filtration by size; it is active, receptor-mediated transcytosis by FcRn, which binds the Fc of IgG and of nothing else. IgA is smaller than IgM and does not cross either; a monomeric IgM would not cross; and IgG crosses against its concentration gradient, which no size-based mechanism could achieve. The reason this matters clinically: because only IgG crosses, IgM found in a newborn's blood must have been made by the fetus, and is therefore evidence of intrauterine infection. And the same FcRn explains IgG's 21-day half-life — it rescues IgG from endosomes and returns it to the surface instead of letting it be degraded.

§ 06

Building the repertoire: V(D)J recombination

The ideaThere is not enough DNA in the human genome to store a separate gene for every antibody you can make — not close. The solution is to store the pieces instead. Each developing lymphocyte physically cuts its own chromosome and stitches one randomly chosen piece from each of several drawers into a working gene, throwing the unused pieces away. Because the cutting is deliberately sloppy at the joins, even two cells that picked the same pieces end up with different genes. This is the only place in the body where the genome is rearranged on purpose.

Definition & key facts

V(D)J recombination
Somatic, irreversible rearrangement of the immunoglobulin and T-cell-receptor loci, carried out by the RAG1/RAG2 recombinase, which cuts at recombination signal sequences and lets the general non-homologous end-joining machinery re-seal the DNA. The heavy chain uses V, D and J segments; the light chain uses V and J only.

The joins are not made cleanly. Hairpin intermediates are opened off-centre, generating palindromic P nucleotides; terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) adds template-independent N nucleotides; and exonucleases chew back a few bases. This happens exactly at the CDR3 loop — the centre of the binding site — so it contributes far more diversity than the combinatorial choice does.

Once one allele produces a functional chain, rearrangement of the other is shut down. Consequence: one lymphocyte, one specificity. Without it clonal selection would be meaningless, because selecting a cell would select two receptors.

Notice the economy. The heavy-chain locus in a human occupies a few million base pairs and holds perhaps seventy gene segments; from that it generates thousands of distinct heavy-chain variable exons. Pair those with independently generated light chains and you multiply again, because the binding site is built from both — six CDRs, three contributed by each chain. Then add junctional diversity at CDR3, which is not a multiplication over drawers at all but over individual nucleotides, and the number leaves the realm of anything you could store.

Worked example 1 — how big is the repertoire, and what does the arithmetic leave out?

Take the standard textbook counts: the human heavy-chain locus has 40 functional V, 23 D and 6 J segments; the κ light-chain locus has 40 V and 5 J; the λ locus has 30 V and 4 J. (a) How many distinct antibodies can combinatorial joining plus heavy–light pairing produce? (b) What is still missing?

  1. Heavy-chain variable exons. Three independent choices, so multiply: 40 × 23 × 6 = 5 520.
  2. κ light chains. 40 × 5 = 200.
  3. Pairing, κ only. Any heavy chain can meet any light chain: 5 520 × 200 = 1 104 000 ≈ 1.1 × 10⁶. This is the figure Campbell quotes as "more than a million".
  4. Include λ. 30 × 4 = 120 λ chains, so 200 + 120 = 320 light chains in total and 5 520 × 320 = 1 766 400 ≈ 1.8 × 10⁶. Note the light chains are added (κ or λ, alternatives) while V, D and J are multiplied (each is chosen). Getting that distinction right is most of the skill in this calculation.
  5. (b) What is missing — and it is the larger part. Junctional diversity at the two heavy joins and the one light join adds several bases of free choice at CDR3; a conservative extra factor of ~10⁵ takes the pre-immune repertoire past 1.1 × 10⁶ × 10⁵ ≈ 10¹¹. Somatic hypermutation adds more again, but after antigen — so it belongs to the response, not to the starting repertoire.
  6. Sanity check. A human body holds on the order of 10¹² lymphocytes in total. So the genetics can specify far more receptors than you have cells to carry them: your actual repertoire is a sample of the potential one, capped by cell number. That is why a naive clone for any given epitope is only about 10–100 cells — and why the primary response needs a week to grow it (§ 09).
(a) 5 520 heavy × 200 κ = 1.104 × 10⁶ (1.77 × 10⁶ including λ) · (b) junctional diversity (≈10¹¹ potential) and, post-antigen, somatic hypermutation

Three claims about diversity that examiners like to mix up

(1) The DNA really is cut and rejoined, and the unused segments are physically deleted from that cell's chromosome — this is not alternative splicing, and it is not reversible. Every daughter of that B cell inherits the same rearrangement, which is why a clone is monospecific. (2) Somatic hypermutation is not how the repertoire is built. It refines an existing specificity after antigen has selected it; a stem that offers hypermutation as the source of the naive repertoire is wrong. (3) The same machinery builds T-cell receptors — RAG, RSSs, the 12/23 rule, junctional diversity, allelic exclusion. What T cells lack is somatic hypermutation and class switching, and the reason is worth knowing: a TCR that mutated after leaving the thymus would never be re-screened for self-reactivity.

Exam technique · any "how big is the repertoire" calculation

  1. Write the two skeletons before you look at the options. heavy = V × D × J and light = V × J. Only the heavy chain has a D — remember it as heavy Duty — and writing both lines first stops you pairing the wrong counts when the stem lists five or seven numbers in prose.
  2. Multiply choices, add alternatives. Every segment is a separate choice, so V, D and J multiply; κ and λ are two ways of doing the same job, so they add. Then the heavy pool multiplies by the light pool, because any heavy chain can meet any light chain. That one distinction is most of the marks.
  3. Run the standard numbers once so you recognise them cold. 40 × 23 × 6 = 5 520 heavy; 40 × 5 = 200 κ; 5 520 × 200 = 1.104 × 10⁶. With λ: 30 × 4 = 120, so 200 + 120 = 320 light chains and 5 520 × 320 = 1.766 × 10⁶. If an option quotes 1.1 × 10⁶ and another quotes 1.8 × 10⁶, the question is asking whether you included λ — go back and check the stem's wording.
  4. Three distractors are pre-built; name them and move on. 5 720 is addition where multiplication was needed. 5 520 alone has dropped the light chain, which is wrong because three of the six CDRs come from VL. Anything around 10¹¹ labelled as combinatorial has smuggled in junctional diversity.
  5. Sanity-check by order of magnitude, not by recomputing. Pure combinatorics lands near 10⁶. Getting past 10¹¹ requires P and N nucleotides at CDR3. Anything invoking somatic hypermutation is post-antigen and cannot belong to the naive repertoire at all — so an option that offers hypermutation as the source of the starting repertoire is wrong before you check its number.
Go deeper · the cutting machinery, the 12/23 rule, and what breaks when RAG fails

Each V, D and J segment is flanked by a recombination signal sequence: a conserved heptamer, a non-conserved spacer of either 12 or 23 base pairs, and a conserved nonamer. RAG1/RAG2 will only bring together one 12-spacer RSS and one 23-spacer RSS — the 12/23 rule. On the heavy-chain locus the V and J segments carry 23-spacers while D carries 12-spacers on both sides, which mechanically forbids a direct V-to-J join and forces every heavy chain to include a D segment. The rule is not a preference; it is geometry, imposed by how the RAG complex holds the two signals.

RAG makes a clean double-strand break at the heptamer and leaves the coding end as a hairpin. Artemis, activated by DNA-PKcs, nicks the hairpin open — and if it nicks off-centre, the strand that unfolds carries a short palindrome copied from the other strand: the P nucleotides. TdT then adds up to about twenty untemplated N nucleotides, exonucleases trim, and non-homologous end joining seals the result. Two-thirds of joins are out of frame and the cell dies; the survivors are the repertoire. That is the deep reason the process is so wasteful, and why so many developing lymphocytes never leave the marrow or the thymus.

The disease consequences map onto the machinery precisely. Loss of RAG1 or RAG2 gives severe combined immunodeficiency with neither T nor B cells — the receptor cannot be built at all; hypomorphic mutations give Omenn syndrome, in which a very few clones do form and expand pathologically. Loss of Artemis gives SCID with radiosensitivity, because the same enzyme is needed for repairing radiation-induced breaks. And because RAG creates double-strand breaks next to highly transcribed loci, mistakes translocate oncogenes into immunoglobulin enhancers — MYC into IGH in Burkitt lymphoma, BCL2 into IGH in follicular lymphoma. The mechanism that makes adaptive immunity possible is also a standing cancer risk.

§ 07

The humoral arm: B cells, plasma cells and memory

The ideaA B cell is a cell that has one question and one product. The question is asked by the antibody stuck in its membrane: does this fit? If it does, the cell usually still cannot act — it must first show a piece of what it caught to a helper T cell and be given permission. Once permitted, it does two things at once: it becomes a factory that pumps out the same antibody in soluble form, and it leaves behind a small, quiet population that will remember. Almost every property of the secondary response (§ 09) is decided in this section.

Definition & key facts

B-cell activation (T-dependent)
The BCR binds antigen and internalises it; the B cell digests it and presents peptides on MHC class II; a helper T cell whose TCR fits that peptide–MHC delivers CD40L and cytokines. Plasma cell
The terminally differentiated effector: enormous rough ER, no surface immunoglobulin left to speak of, no MHC II, no further division. Secretes on the order of 2 000 antibody molecules per second. Memory B cell
Long-lived, quiescent, class-switched and affinity-matured, carrying the improved receptor in its membrane. It secretes nothing until re-challenged. Its value is that it starts the next response from a clone that is already large, already IgG and already high-affinity.
Class switch recombination (CSR)
AID-driven recombination between switch regions that deletes the intervening constant-region DNA and places the same V(D)J exon next to Cγ, Cα or Cε. Why help must be antigen-linked

A helper T cell does not hand out help to whoever asks. The B cell must present, on its own MHC class II, a peptide from the same physical antigen the T cell was primed against. This is called linked recognition, and the two cells need not see the same epitope — indeed they usually cannot, because the B cell sees a surface feature and the T cell sees an internal peptide. What links them is that both epitopes were on one molecule or one particle.

The safety logic is elegant. Suppose a B cell has drifted into weak self-reactivity through somatic hypermutation. It will bind a self protein, internalise it, and present self peptides — for which, thanks to thymic negative selection, essentially no helper T cell exists. No help, no response. Linked recognition means that tolerance in the T-cell compartment is automatically enforced in the B-cell compartment as well, without the B cells having to be perfectly screened themselves. It is one control point doing two jobs.

The conjugate vaccine: a mechanism, not a trick

Encapsulated bacteria — Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae type b — are dangerous precisely to the age group that cannot mount a T-independent response, and their protective antigen is a capsular polysaccharide. A plain polysaccharide vaccine therefore fails in children under two: IgM only, no memory, no boosting. The fix is to covalently link the polysaccharide to a carrier protein (tetanus toxoid or the diphtheria mutant CRM197). Now a B cell whose receptor binds the sugar internalises the whole conjugate, digests it, and presents carrier peptides on MHC II — for which primed helper T cells exist. Linked recognition is satisfied, the germinal centre runs, and the anti-sugar response acquires class switching, affinity maturation and memory. This is one of the clearest cases in the whole subject where a purely mechanistic insight produced a vaccine that eliminated a disease; Hib meningitis in vaccinated countries has fallen by well over 95 %.

Plasma cell versus memory cell: the same clone, opposite settings

Both descend from the same activated B cell, and the exam tests whether you can keep them apart. The plasma cell is maximally productive and terminal: it has rebuilt itself around secretion, loses its surface immunoglobulin and MHC class II, and cannot divide or be re-stimulated. The memory cell is maximally durable and silent: it keeps its receptor in the membrane, keeps its MHC II, does not secrete, and can wait decades. The trap is the sentence "memory cells continue to secrete small amounts of antibody to maintain immunity". They do not — the residual titre comes from long-lived plasma cells sitting in bone-marrow niches. Memory cells contribute nothing until the antigen comes back, at which point they are what makes the response fast.

Class switching changes the verb, not the noun

Switch recombination joins a switch region upstream of Cμ to one upstream of Cγ, Cα or Cε and deletes everything in between. Three consequences. The V(D)J exon is untouched, so the antibody still binds exactly the same epitope — the class change alters what happens after binding, not what is bound. The deletion is physical, so the switch is irreversible: a cell that has switched to IgG can never make IgM again, which is why memory responses are IgG. And it requires CD40L, so it fails in X-linked hyper-IgM syndrome, where patients make plenty of IgM and almost no IgG, IgA or IgE. Note the one apparent exception that is not one: a naive B cell displays IgM and IgD simultaneously, but that is alternative RNA splicing of one long transcript, not class switching, and no DNA is lost.

Which cytokine switches the cell to which class

IFN-γ → IgG · TGF-β → IgA at the barrier · IL-4 → IgE, the allergy pair

Ours, but the first third is not really an invention: γ is the Greek letter that is G, and IFN-γ drives the opsonising IgG subclasses, so that link stores itself. Hang the other two on themes you already have. TGF-β is the barrier and mucosal cytokine, and IgA is the barrier and mucosal antibody — the two belong to the same surface. IL-4 is the TH2 cytokine and IgE is the allergy antibody, and § 11 will use exactly that pair again to explain sensitisation. It omits that IL-4 also drives IgG1, so do not use it to exclude an IgG1 option. And it says nothing about IgM or IgD, which is correct — neither requires switching. IgM is the default because Cμ is the constant region nearest the rearranged V(D)J exon (§ 05), and naive IgD comes from alternative splicing of the same transcript, not from a switch.

Where each source of diversity acts

Before antigen, in the marrow: V(D)J recombination and junctional diversity — they build the repertoire. After antigen, in the germinal centre: somatic hypermutation (improves affinity) and class switching (changes function). If a question puts hypermutation in the bone marrow or V(D)J in the germinal centre, it is wrong.

§ 08

The cell-mediated arm: T cells, MHC and antigen presentation

The ideaAntibodies are useless against a pathogen that is already inside a cell — there is nothing for them to reach. So the body runs a second system in which every cell continuously publishes a sample of the proteins it is currently making, held up in a standard clamp on its surface. T cells patrol, reading those samples. If a cell is displaying something that should not be there, it is killed. A second clamp, carried only by specialised cells, publishes samples of what has been swallowed from outside, and that one summons help rather than execution. Two clamps, two audiences, two outcomes.

Definition & key facts

MHC (major histocompatibility complex)
A family of cell-surface glycoproteins whose sole job is to hold a short peptide in a groove where a T-cell receptor can read it. In humans the genes are the HLA cluster on chromosome 6, expressed codominantly and the most polymorphic genes known — which is why unrelated people reject each other's tissue and why HLA type is associated with dozens of diseases.
CD8 × MHC class I = 8 · CD4 × MHC class II = 8 the "rule of 8" — the single most efficient thing to memorise on this page
Full definition, symbols and units

One polymorphic α chain (three domains) plus invariant β₂-microglobulin. Genes: HLA-A, -B, -C. On every nucleated cell. Groove closed at both ends → peptides of 8–10 residues. Loaded with cytosolic peptides.
MHC class II
Two membrane-anchored chains, α and β, both contributing to the groove. Genes: HLA-DP, -DQ, -DR. Only on professional antigen-presenting cells — dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells (plus thymic epithelium). Groove open at both ends → peptides of 13–25 residues, which can hang out at either end. Loaded with endosomal peptides.

A T cell recognises peptide only when presented by a self MHC molecule, because its receptor contacts the MHC helices as well as the peptide. Imposed during positive selection in the thymus. Deriving the rules instead of memorising them

Every fact in the table below follows from asking what each pathway is for.

Why is class I on every nucleated cell? Because any cell can be infected by a virus, and the only way a killer can tell is if that cell reports its own cytosolic contents. Universality is not generosity; it is the necessary condition for surveillance. Mature red blood cells are the informative exception — no nucleus, no synthesis to report, no MHC — which is why transfusion is matched on ABO and Rh rather than on HLA (§ 10).

Why is class II restricted to professional APCs? Because its job is to activate helper T cells, and activation is dangerous. You want that decision made only by cells that have properly sampled the environment and that also carry B7 co-stimulation, so that the two-signal rule can be applied. Letting an ordinary epithelial cell activate helper T cells would remove the safety interlock.

Why are class I peptides shorter? The class I groove is built from the α1 and α2 domains of a single chain and is closed at both ends, so the peptide must fit exactly — 8 to 10 residues. The class II groove is built from α1 of one chain and β1 of another and is open at both ends, so a longer peptide can thread through and overhang. A structural fact explains a numerical one.

Why does the killer need CD8 and the helper CD4? The co-receptors bind the non-polymorphic parts of their MHC class — CD8 to the α3 domain of class I, CD4 to the β2 domain of class II. They stabilise a very weak TCR interaction and recruit the kinase Lck. Because each co-receptor grips only one class, the pairing of cell type to MHC class is enforced physically, not by convention.

The rule of 8 fixes the pairing (CD8 × class I, CD4 × class II); this adds the two things it leaves out. The class I genes are HLA-A, -B, -C, the opening of the alphabet for the opening class; every class II gene begins with D — HLA-DP, -DQ, -DR — so a gene name starting with D is class II and there is nothing further to decide. The helper T cell is the hub, and the subsets are its settings

A CD4 cell kills nothing and secretes no antibody. What it does is decide which kind of response is mounted, by choosing which cytokines to release — and that choice is itself made for it by the cytokines the innate system produced when it first met the pathogen. IL-12 from an activated macrophage pushes towards TH1; IL-4 pushes towards TH2. So the innate system reports what sort of enemy it has found, and the adaptive system selects the matching weapon.

MHC restriction, and how to reason about it under time pressure

A TCR does not bind a peptide floating free; it binds a composite surface made of the peptide plus the two α-helices flanking the groove. Change the MHC allotype and you have changed a large part of the ligand, so the same peptide is no longer recognised. Three standard consequences: a cytotoxic T cell from person A cannot kill person B's infected cells even if the virus is identical; a vaccine peptide that works in one HLA type may not work in another; and a bone-marrow graft must be HLA matched or the transferred T cells will be functionally blind (and, worse, will attack the host, § 11). The restriction is learned, not encoded: in the thymic cortex a developing T cell must bind self MHC weakly to survive positive selection, so the surviving repertoire is by construction restricted to whichever MHC molecules that individual happens to have.

"MHC molecules present foreign antigens"

Overwhelmingly, they do not. At any moment a healthy cell's class I molecules are loaded with self peptides — thousands of different ones, from the ordinary turnover of its own proteins. Presentation is continuous and indiscriminate. What makes the system work is not selective presentation but selective recognition: the T-cell repertoire has already had its strongly self-reactive members deleted in the thymus, so the display is ignored until one unfamiliar peptide appears among the thousands. Getting this the right way round matters, because it explains three things at once — why negative selection has to be so thorough, why a T cell must be able to detect a handful of foreign complexes against a background of tens of thousands of self ones, and why the whole scheme fails towards autoimmunity rather than towards blindness when tolerance breaks.

Exam technique · routing any antigen down class I or class II in fifteen seconds

  1. Ask one question: was this protein made inside the cell that is displaying it? Made inside (viral, tumour, self, or translated from an injected mRNA) → proteasome → TAP → class I → CD8 → the verdict is kill. Taken in from outside → endosome → class II → CD4 → the verdict is help. Every other row of the table is downstream of that one answer, so do not try to recall the table; recall the question.
  2. Read the rest off the rule of 8. Once you know the class, the co-receptor, the T-cell type and the outcome are fixed — CD8 × I, CD4 × II. There is nothing left to remember and nothing to get the wrong way round.
  3. Eliminate on pairing before you read the biology. Any option pairing CD8 with class II, or CD4 with class I, is dead on sight. In a five-option item that is routinely two options gone, which by itself makes answering strictly better than leaving it blank.
  4. Then eliminate on the cell. If the presenting cell is not a dendritic cell, macrophage or B cell (thymic epithelium aside, which no stem about infection will mean), class II is impossible — so an option about an infected fibroblast, hepatocyte or epithelial cell presenting on class II is wrong. If the cell is a mature red cell, both classes are impossible, which is the same fact that makes HLA matching irrelevant to a red-cell transfusion (§ 10).
  5. Keep exactly one exception loaded. Cross-presentation, dendritic cells only, exogenous antigen into class I. If a stem describes a dendritic cell that ate a dying infected cell and then primed a CD8 response, that is the exception being tested and not a contradiction. Anything else claiming exogenous antigen on class I is a distractor.
Go deeper · cross-presentation, checkpoints, superantigens and why cancer immunotherapy works

Cross-presentation. The tidy rule — exogenous to class II, endogenous to class I — has one deliberate exception. Dendritic cells can divert material from phagosomes into the class I pathway. Without it, a cytotoxic response could only ever be primed against viruses that happen to infect dendritic cells; with it, a dendritic cell that has eaten a dying infected cell from any tissue can prime CD8 T cells against its contents. This is also how most cancer vaccines and most tumour-antigen priming is thought to work.

Checkpoints. Co-stimulation has a matching set of brakes. CTLA-4 appears on activated T cells and binds B7 with much higher affinity than CD28 does, so it wins the competition and shuts the response down — a brake at the priming stage, in the lymph node. PD-1, engaged by PD-L1, acts later and in the tissue. Tumours exploit both, typically by expressing PD-L1 and looking, to a T cell, like healthy tissue. Antibodies that block CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) or PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) release the brake rather than pressing the accelerator — which is exactly why their side effects are autoimmune. The 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Allison and Honjo for this.

Superantigens. Staphylococcal toxic-shock toxin TSST-1 and streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins bypass the groove entirely. They clamp the outside of an MHC class II molecule to the outside of a TCR β chain, so activation happens without any peptide being read. Instead of the normal one in 10⁵ T cells, up to 20 % of the repertoire is activated at once, and the resulting cytokine storm — not the bacterium — causes the shock. It is the clearest demonstration available that the specificity of the response, not its magnitude, is what makes it useful.

CAR-T cells. A synthetic receptor that fuses an antibody's single-chain variable fragment to the T cell's own signalling tails gives a T cell an antibody's eyes. The clinical point is mechanistic: because the recognition element is an antibody, a CAR-T cell binds intact surface antigen without MHC — so it works even on tumours that have escaped normal T cells by shedding class I. The commercial anti-CD19 products for B-cell leukaemia are the proof.

§ 09

Memory in action: response curves, active and passive immunity, and vaccines

The ideaThe first time you meet a pathogen, almost nothing happens for a week — not because the machinery is slow, but because the right cell has to be found and grown into an army from a starting population of perhaps fifty cells. The second time, that army already exists, it is already making the better class of antibody, and its receptors have already been improved. That is all immunological memory is, and vaccination is simply the act of paying the first cost against a harmless imitation.

Definition & key facts

Primary and secondary response
Primary: a lag of 5–10 days, IgM first and then some IgG, a modest peak, moderate affinity, and a titre that decays. Secondary: a lag of 1–3 days, almost entirely IgG, a peak roughly 100-fold higher, much higher affinity, and a much slower decay.
active = you make it, slow, lasting · passive = you are given it, instant, temporary and the cross-cut: natural = by living · artificial = by medicine
Full definition, symbols and units
Active immunity
The recipient's own lymphocytes engage the antigen, so clonal expansion, class switching, affinity maturation and memory all occur. Takes days to weeks to develop; lasts years to life.
Passive immunity
Pre-formed antibody is transferred. Protection is immediate and lasts only as long as the transferred molecules survive — with IgG that is a half-life of about 21 days. No memory whatsoever, because the recipient's own B cells never engage the antigen through their receptors.
Adjuvant
A substance (alum, oil emulsions, TLR agonists) added to a vaccine to supply the innate danger signals a purified antigen lacks. It provides no antigen of its own; it supplies signal 2.
Herd immunity threshold
The immune fraction at which each case produces on average fewer than one further case, so a chain of transmission dies out. The unvaccinated are protected because the pathogen cannot find a route to them.

Worked example 2 — why does a primary response take a week when a plasma cell works in seconds?

A naive B cell specific for one epitope is activated. Once activated it divides every 8 hours. A plasma cell secretes about 2 000 antibody molecules per second. (a) How many descendants after 5 days? (b) How much antibody would one plasma cell make in a day? (c) Which step actually sets the lag, and what does that predict about the secondary response?

  1. (a) Doublings. 5 d × 24 h/d = 120 h, and 120 / 8 = 15 doublings, so 2¹⁵ = 32 768 descendants from one cell.
  2. Realistic starting clone. Only about 10–100 naive cells in the whole body carry any given specificity (§ 06), so a realistic 5-day output is 100 × 32 768 ≈ 3.3 × 10⁶ cells.
  3. (b) Secretion. 2 000 × 86 400 = 1.73 × 10⁸ molecules per plasma cell per day — about 4.3 × 10⁻¹¹ g of IgG per cell per day (150 kDa ÷ NA), which is a substantial fraction of the cell's own total protein mass — the plasma cell really is rebuilt around secretion.
  4. (c) Where the time goes. The chemistry is not limiting: a single plasma cell reaches 10⁸ molecules a day, so even a thousand of them would give a measurable titre within hours. What takes days is everything before that — antigen has to be carried to a lymph node, a dendritic cell has to prime a helper T cell, the rare B cell has to find both the antigen and that T cell, and then the clone has to grow through fifteen doublings. The lag is a search-and-expansion problem.
  5. Prediction. If the lag is clone size, then anything that starts you with a bigger, better clone must shorten it. That is exactly what memory is: after a primary response the precursor pool for that epitope is 100–1 000× larger, already class-switched to IgG (so no time is spent switching) and already affinity-matured (so less time is spent selecting). Hence a 1–3 day lag and a ~100-fold higher peak — the same arithmetic, started from a better place.

This is the single most common wrong explanation, and it is testable rather than merely inelegant. Residual antibody, if anything, slows a second response — it mops up the antigen before B cells can see it, which is precisely the mechanism used deliberately in anti-D prophylaxis (§ 10) and is why maternal antibody blocks live vaccines given too early. The secondary response is faster because of cells: a memory pool that is larger, pre-switched and pre-matured. A neat way to see this is that immunological memory transfers with lymphocytes and not with serum.

Any option that gives a passive scenario memory, lasting immunity or a delayed onset is dead without further thought — and that combination is the single commonest distractor in this section.
  3. If the stem gives a half-life, count halvings — never exponentiate.   4. Herd immunity is two formulas and one classic trap. Hc = 1 − 1/R₀ and Reff = R₀(1 − p). For R₀ = 12: 1 − 1/12 = 91.7 %, and at 85 % coverage 12 × 0.15 = 1.8 > 1, so outbreaks still grow. A second one quotes R₀ itself as a percentage; R₀ is dimensionless.
  5. Sanity-check the direction before you commit. A higher R₀ must demand more coverage, never less, and Hc can never exceed 1. If your number moves the wrong way when you imagine R₀ doubling, you have inverted the fraction.
Worked example 3 — maternal antibody, vaccine timing and the herd-immunity threshold

(a) A newborn is born with maternal IgG at a titre of 1 024 against measles. IgG half-life is 21 days. When does the titre fall below 8, and what does that imply about when to give the vaccine? (b) Measles has R0 ≈ 12. What coverage is needed for herd immunity, and what happens at 85 % coverage?

  1. (a) Dilutions. From 1 024 down to 8 is log₂(1024/8) = log₂(128) = 7 halvings, so 7 × 21 = 147 days ≈ 5 months.
  2. Check the standard milestones. After 63 days (3 half-lives) only (½)³ = 1/8 = 12.5 % remains; after 180 days, 180/21 = 8.57 half-lives leave 0.26 %. Maternal protection is therefore essentially gone by about six months — which matches the clinical observation that infants with X-linked agammaglobulinaemia stay well until roughly that age and then begin to have bacterial infections (§ 11).
  3. Why this dictates the schedule. Maternal IgG neutralises a live attenuated vaccine virus just as effectively as it neutralises wild measles, so a dose given at two months would simply be destroyed. Wait too long and the child is unprotected in the window after maternal antibody has waned. The compromise — the first MMR at 12 months in most schedules, earlier in outbreaks — is the resolution of exactly this tension, and it is a passive-versus-active question in clinical clothing.
  4. (b) Threshold. Hc = 1 − 1/12 = 0.917, i.e. 91.7 %. Measles is at the hard end precisely because R₀ is so high; polio (R₀ ≈ 5–7) needs only 80–86 %.
  5. At 85 % coverage. Reff = 12 × (1 − 0.85) = 1.8. Greater than 1, so each case still produces nearly two more and outbreaks grow — slower than in a naive population, but they do not die out. This is why measles returns first when coverage slips: the threshold is a cliff, not a slope.

How an mRNA vaccine works, mechanism by mechanism

A lipid nanoparticle carries the mRNA across the plasma membrane of local muscle and dendritic cells — naked mRNA would be destroyed by extracellular RNases within minutes and could not cross the membrane at all. The message is translated by ordinary host ribosomes in the cytosol, producing the antigen — for the COVID-19 vaccines, the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein locked by two proline substitutions in its prefusion conformation, which is the shape that raises the best neutralising antibodies.

Because the protein is made inside the cell, some of it is degraded by the proteasome and presented on MHC class I to CD8 cells; secreted and surface-displayed protein is also taken up by dendritic cells and presented on MHC class II, and intact protein is seen directly by B cells. Both arms are engaged, which is why the platform behaves more like a live vaccine than like a subunit one.

Two design details are worth knowing because they are genuinely mechanistic. Uridine is replaced by N1-methylpseudouridine, which dampens recognition by the endosomal RNA sensors TLR7/8 — otherwise the innate response would shut down translation before enough antigen was made. And the mRNA never enters the nucleus, where the genome is; the formulation supplies neither reverse transcriptase nor integrase, and translation in the cytosol leaves no DNA copy behind, so there is no route to the genome. (Strictly, the caveat is that human cells do carry LINE-1 retrotransposon reverse transcriptase; but that is a nuclear-localised, tightly repressed activity, and no integration of vaccine sequence has ever been demonstrated — the exam answer remains "no integration".) The mRNA itself is degraded within days. If an option claims genomic integration or nuclear entry, reject it.

Go deeper · monoclonal antibodies, hybridomas and how a therapeutic antibody gets its name

An antiserum is polyclonal: dozens of clones responded, each to a different epitope, so the mixture is irreproducible and finite. Köhler and Milstein's 1975 solution was to make an individual B cell immortal. Immunise a mouse, harvest its spleen B cells, and fuse them with a myeloma line that has been selected to lack the enzyme HGPRT. Grow the fusion in HAT medium: aminopterin blocks de-novo nucleotide synthesis, so cells must use the salvage pathway. Unfused myeloma cells die (no HGPRT); unfused B cells die of old age within days; only the hybridoma — immortal from the myeloma, HGPRT-competent from the B cell — survives. Clone it out by limiting dilution and you have an unlimited supply of one antibody against one epitope.

Mouse antibodies provoke a human anti-mouse response, so therapeutic antibodies are progressively humanised, and the suffix records how far: -omab mouse, -ximab chimeric (mouse V regions on human constant regions), -zumab humanised (only the CDRs are murine), -umab fully human. Reading the name tells you the construction — rituximab is chimeric, trastuzumab is humanised, adalimumab is fully human.

Their mechanisms are exactly the effector functions of § 05, chosen deliberately. Neutralisation: omalizumab mops up free IgE; palivizumab blocks RSV entry. Blockade of a signal: infliximab sequesters TNF-α; trastuzumab blocks HER2. ADCC and complement: rituximab's Fc recruits NK cells and complement onto CD20⁺ B cells. Releasing a brake: pembrolizumab blocks PD-1. And the diagnostic uses are the same molecule in a different job — the pregnancy test and the lateral-flow antigen test are both sandwich immunoassays built on two monoclonals against two different epitopes of one target.

§ 10

Blood groups, Rh and haemolytic disease of the newborn

The ideaRed cells wear sugars and proteins on their surface, and which ones you wear is inherited. Two systems matter clinically, and they behave in opposite ways. For ABO you already carry antibodies against the sugars you lack — you were never transfused, you simply grew up with gut bacteria wearing similar sugars. For Rh you carry nothing until you are exposed, but once you are, the antibody you make is the class that crosses the placenta. That single difference in antibody class is why one system causes immediate transfusion reactions and the other causes disease in a later pregnancy.

Why an ABO mismatch kills within minutes

Transfuse group A blood into a group B recipient and the recipient's pre-existing anti-A IgM coats the donor cells immediately. IgM is the best complement fixer there is (§ 05), so the classical pathway runs at full speed: massive intravascular haemolysis, free haemoglobin in plasma and urine, C3a and C5a driving shock and fever, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. This is a type II hypersensitivity reaction (§ 11) with the antibody already in place — which is why there is no lag at all. Note also what is not involved: red cells carry no MHC, so no T cell is engaged and HLA matching is irrelevant to a simple red-cell transfusion.

"Group O is the universal donor" — of what, exactly?

Of red cells, and only after they have been separated from the plasma. Whole group O blood carries anti-A and anti-B, and in a large-volume transfusion those antibodies attack the recipient's own cells; that is why modern practice transfuses packed red cells with the plasma removed. For plasma products the ranking inverts completely — AB is the universal plasma donor. If a question specifies plasma, platelets or whole blood rather than red cells, the tidy O-and-AB answer changes, and that is precisely the point at which it is being tested.

Exam technique · transfusion, cross and haemolytic-disease stems

  1. Find the noun before you find the answer. Red cells, or plasma? O and AB swap places between the two — O is the universal donor of red cells, AB of plasma — so the first thing to underline in the stem is which product is being transfused. If it says plasma, platelets or whole blood, the tidy O-and-AB answer is the trap rather than the answer.
  2. Kill any option that wants HLA matching for red cells. Mature red cells have no nucleus and therefore no MHC at all; they are matched on ABO and Rh. HLA matters for platelets, granulocytes and solid organs.
  3. Run a cross as two independent crosses, then multiply. ABO on chromosome 9, Rh on chromosome 1, so work each Punnett square separately and multiply the two probabilities at the end (½ × ½ = ¼ in the standard O/dd × AB/Dd item). Watch for the codominance twist: O × AB gives only A and B children — neither parental phenotype appears.
  4. If the question turns on timing, it is asking about antibody class, not about the antigen. Already present and IgM (anti-A, anti-B) → no lag at all, hence an immediate transfusion reaction, and hence ABO haemolytic disease can touch a first baby. Must be provoked and is IgG (anti-D) → nothing in the first pregnancy, trouble in the next. Whenever a stem stresses "first" versus "second", answer from class.
  5. Have the anti-D numbers ready rather than reasoning them out. 28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery; the mechanism is passive antibody clearing the fetal D-positive cells before the mother's B cells can be activated by them. An option that explains anti-D as "vaccinating the mother against D" has it exactly backwards — it prevents an active response rather than causing one.

Rh disease: the reasoning chain that explains every feature

Follow the logic and you never have to memorise the clinical picture. An Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus. During pregnancy she is largely separated from fetal blood, but at delivery a few millilitres of fetal cells enter her circulation — a fetomaternal haemorrhage. Those cells carry the D antigen, a protein, so the response is T-dependent (§ 07): it goes through a germinal centre, it class-switches to IgG, and it leaves memory. The first child is therefore almost always unaffected; the sensitisation happens on the way out.

In a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy the memory response is fast and IgG, and IgG is the one class that crosses the placenta by FcRn. Fetal red cells are opsonised and destroyed extravascularly in the fetal spleen. The fetus becomes anaemic, ramps up extramedullary haematopoiesis and releases immature nucleated erythroblasts — hence the older name erythroblastosis fetalis — and in severe cases develops high-output failure and generalised oedema (hydrops fetalis). After birth, bilirubin from the haemolysis can no longer be cleared by the placenta, so it accumulates and, being lipid-soluble, can cross into the basal ganglia and cause kernicterus.

Prevention is an exercise in applying passive immunity against the mother's own immune system. Give the Rh-negative mother anti-D immunoglobulin at 28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery. The injected IgG binds the fetal D-positive cells and they are cleared before her B cells can be activated by them — the same "antibody feedback" phenomenon that makes maternal antibody block a live vaccine (§ 09), used deliberately. This is artificial passive immunity given to prevent an active response, and it is the cleanest illustration of the passive/active distinction anywhere in medicine.

Worked example 4 — an ABO × Rh cross, and how often Rh disease can even arise in Taiwan

(a) A woman of blood group O, Rh-negative, has children with a man of group AB whose Rh genotype is Dd. For any one child, what is the probability of group B and at risk of Rh haemolytic disease? What groups are possible at all? (b) Rh-negative frequency is about 15 % in Europeans and about 0.3 % in Taiwanese. How much rarer is the at-risk pairing in Taiwan?

  1. Genotypes. Mother: group O = ii; Rh-negative = dd. Father: group AB = IAIB; Rh-positive heterozygote = Dd.
  2. ABO. Mother contributes i always; father contributes IA or IB with equal probability. Children are ½ IAi (group A) and ½ IBi (group B) — never O and never AB. That is worth noticing: an O × AB cross produces neither parent's phenotype, which is the classic codominance question.
  3. Rh. dd × Dd → ½ Dd (Rh-positive) and ½ dd (Rh-negative). Only the Rh-positive children are at risk.
  4. Combine. The two loci are on different chromosomes, so they assort independently: P = ½ × ½ = ¼.
  5. The clinical qualifier. "At risk" is not the same as "affected". The first Rh-positive fetus usually escapes, because sensitisation occurs at its own delivery; and with anti-D prophylaxis the risk is largely abolished. A complete answer says 1/4, and mostly from the second such pregnancy onwards.
  6. (b) Population arithmetic. The dangerous pairing is an Rh-negative mother with an Rh-positive father. Europe: 0.15 × 0.85 = 0.1275 = 12.75 % of couples. Taiwan: 0.003 × 0.997 = 0.00299 = 0.30 %. The ratio is 0.1275 / 0.00299 = 42.6 — the at-risk pairing is about 43 times rarer in Taiwan. Rh disease is genuinely a European clinical preoccupation; in East Asian obstetrics, ABO incompatibility is the commoner cause of neonatal jaundice.

Why ABO incompatibility and Rh incompatibility behave so differently

Set them side by side and every difference traces to antibody class, which traces to antigen chemistry. The D antigen is a protein, so anti-D is T-dependent, class-switched IgG, made only after exposure, memory-bearing — hence: first pregnancy spared, later ones severe, and preventable by anti-D. The A and B antigens are carbohydrates, so anti-A and anti-B are T-independent, unswitched IgM, present from infancy without exposure, without memory — hence: ABO HDN can affect a first pregnancy (the antibody is already there), but it is mild, because IgM does not cross the placenta at all and fetal A and B antigens are only weakly expressed. The caveat worth carrying is that group O mothers, and only they, also make a minor IgG anti-A,B component; that small fraction is what crosses, and it is why ABO HDN is essentially confined to group O mothers with a group A or B baby. There is even a bonus consequence examiners like: if a mother is group O and the fetus is group A, her anti-A destroys any fetal cells that leak into her circulation before their D antigen can sensitise her — so ABO incompatibility partially protects against Rh sensitisation.

The one-line version

ABO: antibody first, disease immediate, IgM. Rh: exposure first, disease later, IgG. If a question turns on timing — why the first baby is fine, why a transfusion reaction has no lag — it is asking about class, not about the antigen.

§ 11

When immunity goes wrong: allergy, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, transplantation

The ideaThere are only four ways a defence system this powerful can fail, and they are the four corners of one square: it can respond too hard to something harmless, it can respond to you, it can fail to respond at all, or it can respond correctly to tissue you actually wanted. Nothing here is a new mechanism — every disease in this section is one of the mechanisms from §§ 02–09 pointed at the wrong target, which is why you can usually derive the clinical picture from the immunology rather than learning it separately.

Use the clock, then the mediator

Nearly every hypersensitivity question can be solved from the timing, because the timing is mechanistic. Minutes means preformed mediators already sitting in granules — type I. Hours means antibody that is already circulating and has to find and coat its target — types II and III. Two to three days means cells have to be recruited from the blood, re-activated, and then recruit and differentiate monocytes — type IV, and nothing else takes that long. Then distinguish II from III by asking whether the antigen is fixed to a cell (II) or soluble (III): the damage in type III occurs wherever the complex deposits, which is why it is systemic and multi-organ while type II hits one tissue.

Anaphylaxis, and why the second exposure is the dangerous one

Sensitisation is silent. On first exposure the allergen is presented, TH2 cells drive IgE class switching with IL-4, and the IgE distributes itself onto FcεRI on mast cells throughout the tissues, where it sits for weeks. Nothing is felt. On re-exposure the allergen — which must be at least bivalent — bridges two adjacent IgE molecules, and it is that cross-linking, not the binding itself, that triggers degranulation. Systemically, the released histamine causes vasodilation and capillary leak (hypotension and shock), bronchoconstriction and laryngeal oedema (airway obstruction), and urticaria.

The treatment reveals the mechanism. Adrenaline is first-line, intramuscular, and it works physiologically rather than immunologically: α1 vasoconstriction restores blood pressure and reduces oedema, β2 agonism opens the airways, β1 supports the heart, and it also stabilises mast cells. Antihistamines cannot rescue anaphylaxis, and knowing why is the point: histamine is only one of many mediators, the leukotrienes are made after the trigger and are far more potent bronchoconstrictors, and by the time symptoms appear the release has already happened. Blocking one receptor downstream of an event that has already occurred is not enough.

Autoimmunity: three routes worth being able to name

Molecular mimicry is the most examinable. Antibodies raised against the M protein of Streptococcus pyogenes cross-react with cardiac myosin and valve tissue, producing rheumatic fever weeks after a sore throat; antibodies against Campylobacter jejuni lipo-oligosaccharide cross-react with peripheral-nerve gangliosides in Guillain–Barré syndrome. The response was correct; the target resembled the enemy. Sequestered antigen explains sympathetic ophthalmia, in which penetrating injury to one eye exposes lens and uveal proteins the thymus never sampled, and the immune system then attacks both eyes. Regulatory failure is the third: loss of FoxP3 gives IPEX, loss of AIRE gives autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1, and both show that tolerance is maintained actively and continuously, not established once.

Two patterns are worth carrying. Autoimmune disease is markedly commoner in women — roughly 2:1 overall, and about 9:1 for SLE. And HLA type is the strongest genetic risk factor, which follows directly from § 08: your HLA molecules determine which self peptides can be presented at all. HLA-B27 and ankylosing spondylitis is the classic association; HLA-DQ2/DQ8 is effectively necessary for coeliac disease, and DR3/DR4 for type 1 diabetes.

HIV: why losing one cell type collapses both arms

HIV is an enveloped retrovirus whose surface glycoprotein gp120 binds CD4 — which makes the CD4⁺ helper T lymphocyte its principal target — and then requires a co-receptor, CCR5 for the macrophage-tropic strains that transmit infection and CXCR4 for the T-tropic strains that emerge later. Macrophages, dendritic cells and microglia also carry CD4 and serve as long-lived reservoirs, which is one reason the infection cannot be cleared. People homozygous for the CCR5-Δ32 deletion are largely resistant, which is the cleanest possible demonstration that two receptors are genuinely required.

Now apply § 07 and § 08. The CD4 helper cell is the hub of the entire adaptive response: it licenses B cells to class-switch, hypermutate and form memory; it activates macrophages to kill what they have engulfed; it supports the expansion of CD8 cytotoxic cells. Destroying it therefore cripples humoral immunity as well as cell-mediated immunity, even though B cells are never infected. That reasoning step is the one most worth having ready: a question that asks why an HIV patient responds poorly to a new vaccine is asking whether you know that antibody quality depends on T-cell help. Untreated, CD4 counts fall by roughly 80 cells·µL⁻¹ per year from a normal 500–1 500, so it takes about a decade to reach the AIDS threshold of 200 cells·µL⁻¹, below which the opportunistic infections appear — Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, oesophageal candidiasis, cytomegalovirus retinitis, disseminated tuberculosis — together with Kaposi sarcoma, itself driven by a second virus, HHV-8.

Transplantation: the same T cells, an innocent target

Rejection is not a special mechanism; it is § 08 working perfectly on tissue you wanted to keep. What makes it so vigorous is a quirk called direct allorecognition: recipient T cells can bind donor MHC molecules on donor cells directly, without any processing, and because MHC is what the whole repertoire was positively selected on, an unusually large fraction of T cells — on the order of 1–10 %, against roughly 1 in 10⁵ for an ordinary antigen — reacts to any given foreign MHC. That single number explains why an unmatched allograft is destroyed so much faster than a first infection is cleared.

Three transplant statements that sound right and are not

"A blood transfusion needs HLA matching." No — mature red cells have no nucleus and therefore no MHC at all; red cells are matched on ABO and Rh. HLA matching matters for platelets, granulocytes and solid organs. "An identical twin's kidney will still be rejected without immunosuppression." No — an isograft is genetically identical, so there is nothing foreign to recognise. "Corneal grafts need matching like any other transplant." No — the cornea is an immune-privileged site, avascular and without lymphatic drainage, so antigen never reaches a lymph node and most corneal grafts succeed unmatched. The brain, testis and anterior chamber of the eye share that privilege, and it is the same property that lets sequestered antigens there provoke autoimmunity when a trauma finally exposes them.

Go deeper · the HIV replication cycle, where the drugs act, and why the tests changed

The cycle, step by step, because each step is a drug target. Attachment and fusion: gp120 binds CD4, changes conformation, engages CCR5 or CXCR4, and gp41 drives membrane fusion — blocked by maraviroc (a CCR5 antagonist) and enfuvirtide (a fusion inhibitor). Reverse transcription: the two copies of positive-sense single-stranded RNA are copied into double-stranded DNA by a reverse transcriptase with no proofreading, an error rate of about 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁵ per base per cycle — which is the direct source of the drug resistance and of the antigenic variability that has defeated every vaccine attempt. Blocked by nucleoside analogues (zidovudine, tenofovir) and non-nucleoside inhibitors. Integration into host chromatin by integrase, creating the latent reservoir that no current therapy can clear — blocked by dolutegravir and raltegravir. Transcription, assembly and maturation: a polyprotein is cut by the viral protease into functional units, blocked by the -navir drugs. Combination therapy is used because single-agent therapy selects resistance within weeks; three drugs against two or three targets makes simultaneous escape mutations vanishingly unlikely.

The diagnostics track the immunology exactly. Antibody appears only after the primary response has run (§ 09), so the classical antibody test has a window period of roughly three to twelve weeks during which an infected person tests negative while being highly infectious — viral load peaks in that same window. Fourth-generation assays close much of the gap by also detecting the viral capsid protein p24, which appears within about two weeks, and nucleic-acid testing closes more. Confirmation was historically by Western blot, now generally by an antibody differentiation assay. Note the trap this generates: a negative antibody test in the first weeks after exposure means very little.

One more inference worth drawing. Because HIV integrates, mutates rapidly, hides in long-lived reservoirs, and destroys the very cell type needed to coordinate a response against it, it is a fair summary that HIV is difficult precisely because it attacks the mechanism that would otherwise defeat it. Antiretroviral therapy does not eliminate the virus; it suppresses replication so completely that the CD4 count recovers and the person becomes non-infectious — undetectable equals untransmittable — but stopping treatment allows rebound from the reservoir within weeks.
