Electric Charge, Field and Potential

Electrostatics has exactly one law — Coulomb's inverse square — and everything else is bookkeeping built on top of it. The bookkeeping matters because it is what makes problems solvable: divide the force by the test charge and you get a vector field; divide the energy by the test charge and you get a scalar potential. The single most valuable habit in this chapter is knowing which of those two to reach for, because scalars add with a pen and vectors need a protractor.

§ 01

Charge: quantised, conserved, two-signed

Definition & formula

Electric charge
An intrinsic property of matter — like mass — that determines how strongly a particle sources and responds to the electromagnetic interaction. Unlike mass it comes in two signs.
Quantisation
Every free object's net charge is an integer multiple of the elementary charge e. There is no such thing as half a unit of free charge.
Conservation
The algebraic sum of the charge in an isolated system never changes. Charging is always transfer, never creation.
q = n e with n = 0, ±1, ±2, … any isolated body
e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C ≈ 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ Cproton +e, electron −e
Σ q(isolated system) = constantexact, no known exception
qnet charge (C, coulomb)
nan integer (dimensionless)
eelementary charge, 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C

Two facts fix the whole subject. First, charge is quantised: Millikan's oil-drop experiment found that drops only ever carried 1e, 2e, 3e… and never anything in between. Quarks carry ±⅓e and ±⅔e, but they are permanently confined inside hadrons, so every charge you can actually isolate and measure is a whole multiple of e. Second, charge is conserved: rub a rod on fur and the rod does not manufacture charge — electrons move from one to the other, and the pair together stays neutral.

Note what quantisation does not mean. The step size 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C is so small compared with laboratory charges that charge behaves as a continuous variable for every purpose in this chapter. One coulomb is 1/(1.60×10⁻¹⁹) = 6.25×10¹⁸ elementary charges. When we say a capacitor holds 2.7 nC we are talking about roughly 1.7×10¹⁰ electrons — a number large enough that the graininess is invisible.

Exam point · is this charge possible?

A stock item asks which of several values could be a body's net charge. Divide each by 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C and keep the one that gives a whole number. 6.4×10⁻¹⁹ C = 4e ✓; 2.4×10⁻¹⁹ C = 1.5e ✗; 8.0×10⁻²⁰ C = 0.5e ✗.

Trap · "neutral" ≠ "no charge"

A neutral object is full of charge; the positives and negatives simply balance. That is why a neutral object can still be attracted to a charged rod (§2) and why a neutral conductor can still carry a huge field on its surface. Neutral means net zero, nothing more.

§ 02

Conductors, insulators, semiconductors — and three ways to charge them

Definition & formula

Conductor
A material containing charge carriers that are free to move macroscopically — delocalised electrons in a metal, dissolved ions in an electrolyte.
Insulator (dielectric)
A material in which every charge is bound to its own molecule; charge deposited on it stays where you put it.
Semiconductor
A material whose carrier population is small but thermally and chemically tunable, so its conductivity can be engineered by doping.
Polarisation
The separation of bound charge inside a neutral object placed in a field: no net charge appears, but the near face becomes oppositely charged to the source.
ρ(conductor) ~ 10⁻⁸ Ω·m ≪ ρ(semiconductor) ~ 10⁻⁵–10³ ≪ ρ(insulator) ≥ 10¹⁰ room temperature, order of magnitude
p(induced) = α Elinear response of a neutral molecule
n(free e⁻ in Cu) ≈ 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³≈ one per atom
ρresistivity (Ω·m)
ncarrier number density (m⁻³)
pinduced dipole moment (C·m)
αpolarisability (C·m²·V⁻¹)
Eapplied field (V·m⁻¹ = N·C⁻¹)

The classification is about mobility, not about how much charge a material contains. Copper and glass hold comparable numbers of electrons per cubic metre; in copper about one electron per atom is delocalised across the whole lattice, in glass every electron is locked to a bond. The practical consequence is that excess charge placed on a conductor redistributes itself over the surface within nanoseconds, whereas excess charge on an insulator sits in a patch until something carries it away.

The temperature behaviour is the discriminator examiners like: in a metal the carrier count is fixed and heating only makes the lattice vibrate more, so resistivity rises; in a semiconductor heating promotes many more electrons across the gap, so resistivity falls. Physiologically, remember that the body is a conductor by virtue of its ions — that is precisely why a defibrillator current crosses the chest (§11) and why the lipid bilayer, an excellent insulator only 2–5 nm thick, is what stands between the intracellular and extracellular conductors.

The three charging mechanisms

Induction, step by step. Bring a positive rod near an isolated metal sphere: the sphere's free electrons are attracted to the near side, so the near side goes negative and the far side positive — but the sphere is still neutral overall. Now touch a wire from the far side to earth. Electrons flow up from the earth to neutralise that positive far side. Break the earth connection first, then remove the rod, and the surplus electrons spread out: the sphere is left negative. Reverse the order — remove the rod first — and the electrons simply drain back to earth and you get nothing.

Trap · why a charged comb picks up neutral paper

Polarisation is not charging. The paper stays neutral, but the induced negative face is closer to the positive comb than the induced positive face is. Because Coulomb's law falls as 1/r², the nearer attraction beats the farther repulsion and there is a net attraction. This is why attraction proves nothing about an object's charge — only repulsion is a definitive test that two objects carry the same sign.

Memory hook

Touch it and it copies you; hover-and-earth it and it opposes you. Conduction = same sign, induction = opposite sign.

§ 03

Coulomb's law and the scale of the electric force

Definition & formula

Coulomb's law
The electrostatic force between two point charges acts along the line joining them, is proportional to the product of the charges, and falls as the inverse square of their separation.
Principle of superposition
The force on a charge from many others is the vector sum of the individual pairwise forces, each computed as if the others were absent.
F = k |q₁ q₂| / r² = (1 / 4πε₀) · |q₁ q₂| / r² point charges (or spheres, using centre-to-centre r), at rest, in vacuum
k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²·C⁻²the Coulomb constant
ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²·N⁻¹·m⁻² = 8.85×10⁻¹² F·m⁻¹permittivity of free space
F(net) = F₁ + F₂ + F₃ + …vector sum — resolve into components
Fforce magnitude (N)
q₁, q₂the two charges (C)
rseparation, centre to centre (m)
kCoulomb constant (N·m²·C⁻²)
ε₀permittivity of free space (F·m⁻¹)

Use the formula with magnitudes only and put the direction in by hand: like charges repel along the line joining them, unlike charges attract along it. Feeding signed charges into the formula and then also arguing about direction is how sign errors are made.

Why is k written as 1/(4πε₀)? Because a point charge spreads its influence over spheres, and the surface area of a sphere is 4πr². Writing the constant this way puts the geometric 4π where it belongs and leaves ε₀ as the pure "how easily does a field establish itself in this medium" constant. That pays off immediately in Gauss's law (§5) and in capacitance (§9), where the 4π disappears entirely. In a medium of dielectric constant κ, replace ε₀ by κε₀ — the force weakens, which is why ionic bonds are ~80× weaker in water (κ ≈ 80) than in vacuum.

Worked example 1 · superposition along a line

Three point charges on the x-axis: q₁ = +6.0 μC at x = 0, q₂ = −4.0 μC at x = 0.30 m, q₃ = +2.0 μC at x = 0.50 m. Find the net force on q₃.

  1. Force from q₁ on q₃. r = 0.50 m. F₁₃ = (8.99×10⁹)(6.0×10⁻⁶)(2.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.50)² = 0.1079/0.25 = 0.432 N. Both positive → repulsion → points in +x.
  2. Force from q₂ on q₃. r = 0.20 m. F₂₃ = (8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁶)(2.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.20)² = 0.0719/0.040 = 1.798 N. Opposite signs → attraction → points toward q₂, i.e. −x.
  3. Add as vectors. Both lie on the x-axis, so this is simple addition with signs: F = +0.432 − 1.798 = −1.366 N.
  4. Sanity check. q₂ is smaller in magnitude but 2.5× closer; 1/r² gives it a factor 6.25 advantage, which comfortably beats the factor 1.5 in charge. The nearer charge should dominate — it does.
F(net) ≈ 1.37 N, directed toward q₂ (the −x direction)

Worked example 2 · two protons: electric versus gravitational

Compare the electric repulsion and the gravitational attraction between two protons a distance r = 1.0×10⁻¹⁵ m apart (mp = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg, G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻²).

  1. Electric. FE = k e²/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(1.60×10⁻¹⁹)²/(1.0×10⁻¹⁵)² = 2.30×10⁻²⁸ / 1.0×10⁻³⁰ = 2.3×10² N. That is roughly the weight of a 23 kg child — acting on a single proton.
  2. Gravitational. FG = G mp²/r² = (6.67×10⁻¹¹)(1.67×10⁻²⁷)²/(1.0×10⁻³⁰) = 1.86×10⁻⁶⁴ / 1.0×10⁻³⁰ = 1.9×10⁻³⁴ N.
  3. Ratio. Both laws go as 1/r², so r cancels and the ratio is a pure number: FE/FG = k e² / (G mp²) = 2.30×10⁻²⁸ / 1.86×10⁻⁶⁴.
F(E) / F(G) ≈ 1.2×10³⁶ — independent of separation

Two consequences worth carrying. (i) Gravity is utterly negligible in any problem involving charged particles — never include weight unless the question is explicitly about a charged oil drop or a suspended pith ball. (ii) Since 230 N of repulsion does not blow nuclei apart, there must be a stronger, shorter-ranged interaction: the strong nuclear force.

Exam point · scaling questions

Most Coulomb questions never need a calculator. "One charge is tripled and the separation doubled" → F → F × 3 / 2² = 0.75 F. Read the ratio straight off the exponents: charge is first power, distance is inverse square.

§ 04

The electric field and its lines

Definition & formula

Electric field
The force per unit positive charge that a source distribution would exert at a point — a vector defined at every point in space, whether or not anything is there to feel it.
Test charge
A charge q₀ small enough that placing it does not disturb the sources it is measuring; the definition is strictly a limit as q₀ → 0.
Electric dipole
Two equal and opposite charges ±q separated by a small distance d, characterised by the dipole moment p = qd pointing from − to +.
E = F / q₀ ⟹ F = qE definition; the force on a negative q reverses
E = kQ / r² = Q / (4πε₀r²)point charge, or outside any spherical charge
E(net) = Σ Eᵢvector superposition — components, then Pythagoras
E(axis) = 2kp / r³ · E(bisector) = kp / r³dipole, far field r ≫ d
τ = pE sin θ · U = −pE cos θdipole in a uniform external field
Eelectric field (N·C⁻¹ = V·m⁻¹)
Fforce on the test charge (N)
q₀test charge (C)
Qsource charge (C)
rdistance from the source (m)
pdipole moment, p = qd (C·m)
τtorque on the dipole (N·m)
θangle between p and E (rad)

Coulomb's law describes an interaction between two objects; the field concept splits it into two independent halves. The source sets up a field; the field then acts on whatever is placed in it. The point of dividing by q₀ is that E is a property of the sources alone — change the probe and F changes, but F/q₀ does not. The limit q₀ → 0 is in the definition for a real reason: a finite probe would repel the source charges on a conductor and alter the very field it is measuring.

The units are worth pausing on. N·C⁻¹ and V·m⁻¹ are the same unit: V·m⁻¹ = (J·C⁻¹)/m = (N·m·C⁻¹)/m = N·C⁻¹. Use N·C⁻¹ when you are thinking about force, V·m⁻¹ when you are thinking about potential gradients (§8). For reference, dry air breaks down at about 3×10⁶ V·m⁻¹, and a resting cell membrane sustains about 1.4×10⁷ V·m⁻¹ (§11).

Worked example 3 · vector superposition of two fields

Two charges of q = +4.0 nC each sit at (−3.0 cm, 0) and (+3.0 cm, 0). Find the field at the point P = (0, 4.0 cm).

  1. Distance from each charge to P. r = √(0.030² + 0.040²) = 0.050 m (a 3-4-5 triangle), so r² = 2.5×10⁻³ m².
  2. Magnitude of each field. E₁ = E₂ = kq/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁹)/(2.5×10⁻³) = 35.96/2.5×10⁻³ = 1.44×10⁴ N·C⁻¹, each pointing away from its own positive charge.
  3. Resolve. By symmetry the x-components are equal and opposite → they cancel. Each y-component is E × (4.0/5.0) = 1.4384×10⁴ × 0.80 = 1.15×10⁴ N·C⁻¹.
  4. Add. Ey = 2 × 1.1507×10⁴ = 2.30×10⁴ N·C⁻¹; Ex = 0.
E = 2.30×10⁴ N·C⁻¹, straight up the +y axis

Note how much machinery that took: two magnitudes, two direction cosines, a cancellation argument. Hold on to this example — in §7 we compute the potential at exactly the same point in one line.

The dipole is the charge distribution biology runs on: a water molecule has p ≈ 6.2×10⁻³⁰ C·m, and every peptide bond, membrane lipid head and transmembrane helix carries one. Far from a dipole the field falls as 1/r³, not 1/r², because the two charges nearly cancel and only their separation survives. On the axis it is twice as strong as at the same distance on the perpendicular bisector, where it also points antiparallel to p. In a uniform field a dipole feels zero net force but a non-zero torque τ = pE sin θ, which is why polar molecules rotate to align with a field rather than being dragged along it.

Trap · the field does not care about the test charge

If a test charge of +2 nC feels 6×10⁻⁵ N at a point, the field there is 3×10⁴ N·C⁻¹. Replace it with −6 nC and the force becomes 1.8×10⁻⁴ N in the opposite direction — but the field is still 3×10⁴ N·C⁻¹ in the original direction. E belongs to the sources; F belongs to the pairing.

§ 05

Flux and Gauss's law

Definition & formula

Electric flux
A measure of how much field passes through a surface — literally the number of field lines crossing it, counting outward as positive.
Gauss's law
The net flux out of any closed surface equals the charge enclosed by that surface divided by ε₀. Charge outside the surface contributes exactly zero net flux.
Φ(E) = ∮ E·dA = Q(enclosed) / ε₀ any closed surface, always true
Φ = E A cos θuniform field, flat area, θ measured from the normal
E · 4πr² = Q/ε₀ ⟹ E = kQ/r²Gauss ⟹ Coulomb, for a sphere
Φ(E)electric flux (N·m²·C⁻¹ = V·m)
Efield magnitude (N·C⁻¹)
Aarea of the surface (m²)
θangle between E and the outward normal (rad)
Q(enc)net charge inside the surface (C)
σ, λsurface (C·m⁻²) and line (C·m⁻¹) charge density

Gauss's law is Coulomb's law rewritten so that geometry does the work. Because the field of a point charge falls exactly as 1/r² while the area of a sphere grows exactly as r², the product E×A is independent of r — the flux is a conserved count of lines. A charge outside your surface sends every line that enters straight back out again, so it contributes nothing. Only enclosed charge counts, and only the net enclosed charge at that.

Crucially, Gauss's law is always true but only useful when the symmetry lets you pull E outside the integral: spherical, cylindrical or planar. Choose a Gaussian surface on which E is either constant and perpendicular, or parallel to the surface (contributing zero flux).

The two "constant" rows are the ones students get wrong. A thin isolated sheet radiates from both faces, so the pillbox catches 2EA of flux and E = σ/(2ε₀). Same algebra, factor of two apart, because of where the field is allowed to exist.

Worked example 4 · a charged conducting sphere, inside and out

A solid metal sphere of radius R = 0.10 m carries Q = +5.0 nC. Find E at r = 0.050 m, at the surface, and at r = 0.30 m.

  1. r = 0.050 m (inside the metal). All the excess charge sits on the surface, so the Gaussian sphere encloses nothing: Q(enc) = 0 ⟹ E = 0.
  2. r = R = 0.10 m (at the surface). E = kQ/R² = (8.99×10⁹)(5.0×10⁻⁹)/(0.010) = 44.95/0.010 = 4.5×10³ N·C⁻¹.
  3. Cross-check with σ/ε₀. σ = Q/(4πR²) = 5.0×10⁻⁹/(4π × 0.010) = 3.98×10⁻⁸ C·m⁻²; σ/ε₀ = 3.98×10⁻⁸/8.85×10⁻¹² = 4.5×10³ N·C⁻¹ ✓ — the two routes agree, as they must.
  4. r = 0.30 m (outside). E = kQ/r² = 44.95/0.090 = 5.0×10² N·C⁻¹, exactly what a point charge of +5.0 nC at the centre would give.
E(0.050 m) = 0 · E(R) = 4.5×10³ N·C⁻¹ · E(0.30 m) = 5.0×10² N·C⁻¹

Trap · zero flux does not mean zero field

Put a Gaussian sphere between the two charges of a dipole so that it encloses no charge: the net flux is zero, yet the field there is large. Flux is a signed sum — as much goes in as comes out. Similarly, E on the Gaussian surface is produced by all the charges in the universe, not just the enclosed ones; it is only the net flux that depends on Q(enc) alone.

§ 06

Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium

Definition & formula

Electrostatic equilibrium
The state a conductor reaches — within nanoseconds — when no charge inside it is moving any more. Every property below follows from that one sentence.
E = 0 everywhere inside the conducting material otherwise free charges would still be accelerating
All excess charge resides on the surfaceGauss: any interior surface encloses zero net charge
E ⟂ surface, E = σ/ε₀ just outsidea tangential component would drive a surface current
V = constant throughout the conductorthe whole body is one equipotential volume
σ ∝ 1/R ⟹ E(surface) ∝ 1/Rconnected conductors of local radius R
Eelectric field (N·C⁻¹ = V·m⁻¹)
σsurface charge density (C·m⁻²)
Velectric potential (V)
Rlocal radius of curvature (m)
ε₀permittivity of free space (F·m⁻¹)

Everything in the box above is one idea repeated. If there were any field left inside the metal, the free electrons would feel a force and move; if they are moving, you are not at equilibrium yet. So at equilibrium the interior field must be exactly zero. Then Gauss's law applied to any closed surface drawn just inside the metal gives Q(enc) = ε₀Φ = 0 — so any excess charge has nowhere to be except the outer surface. And a tangential field component at the surface would push surface charge sideways, so the field must meet the surface at a right angle.

Exam point · charge on a shell with a charge inside it

Place +Q at the centre of an isolated neutral conducting shell. Draw a Gaussian surface inside the metal: E = 0 there, so Q(enc) = 0, so the inner surface must carry −Q. The shell as a whole is neutral, so the outer surface carries +Q. Outside, the field is exactly kQ/r², identical to no shell at all. Shielding works one way: a conductor protects its cavity from the outside world, but a charge inside the cavity still announces itself to the outside — unless you earth the shell, which drains the +Q from the outer surface.

Why charge concentrates at points. Imagine two conducting spheres of radii R₁ and R₂ joined by a long thin wire. They are one conductor, so they share one potential: kQ₁/R₁ = kQ₂/R₂, which means Q ∝ R. But the surface density is σ = Q/(4πR²) ∝ R/R² = 1/R, and the surface field is E = σ/ε₀ ∝ 1/R too. The smaller radius — a spike, an edge, a needle tip — carries less total charge but a far higher density and field. Push it past 3×10⁶ V·m⁻¹ and the air ionises: corona discharge, St Elmo's fire, and the entire operating principle of the lightning rod, which bleeds charge away quietly rather than waiting for a strike. The same effect is why sharp electrode tips are used deliberately in electrosurgery and why they are avoided in high-voltage insulation.

Memory hook

Inside metal: no field, no charge, one potential. At the surface: right angles. At a point: everything concentrates.

§ 07

Potential energy, potential and the volt

Definition & formula

Electric potential energy
The work an external agent must do to assemble a configuration of charges from infinite separation, quasi-statically. A scalar, measured in joules.
Electric potential
Potential energy per unit charge at a point: V = U/q₀. Like the field, it is a property of the sources alone, but it is a scalar.
The volt
One volt is one joule per coulomb. A "12 V battery" means each coulomb pushed round the circuit is given 12 J.
Electron-volt
The energy gained by one elementary charge falling through one volt: 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J. An energy unit, never a voltage.
V = U / q₀ = kQ / r (V → 0 as r → ∞) point charge; carry the sign of Q
U(pair) = k q₁ q₂ / rnote: 1/r, not 1/r² — and signed
V(net) = Σ kQᵢ / rᵢalgebraic sum — no components, ever
ΔU = q ΔV = q(V(b) − V(a))moving charge q from a to b
W(by field) = −ΔU = −q ΔVpath-independent: the force is conservative
1 eV = (1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C)(1 V) = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ Jenergy unit for particles

Velectric potential (V = J·C⁻¹)

This is the section that saves you time in every other section. The electric force is conservative — the work it does moving a charge between two points is independent of the path — so a potential energy exists, and dividing by the test charge gives a scalar field V that encodes everything about the sources. Two consequences you should exploit relentlessly:

  • Potentials add like numbers. To find V from ten charges, compute ten values of kQ/r and add them with their signs. No angles, no components, no diagram.
  • Only differences matter. V = 0 at infinity is a convention (in circuits we instead set earth = 0). Every physical statement involves ΔV.

Worked example 5 · the same geometry, done with potential

(a) Two charges of +4.0 nC at (−3.0 cm, 0) and (+3.0 cm, 0): find V at P = (0, 4.0 cm). (b) Now flip the right-hand charge to −4.0 nC and find both V and E at P.

  1. (a) Each charge is r = 0.050 m from P. V = kq/r + kq/r = 2 × (8.99×10⁹)(4.0×10⁻⁹)/0.050 = 2 × 719.2 = 1.44×10³ V. That is the entire calculation — one line, versus the four-step vector job in Worked example 3.
  2. (b) Potential. V = +719.2 + (−719.2) = exactly 0.
  3. (b) Field. The magnitudes are unchanged at 1.4384×10⁴ N·C⁻¹ each. Now the y-components cancel and the x-components add: Ex = 2 × 1.4384×10⁴ × (3.0/5.0) = 1.73×10⁴ N·C⁻¹ in the +x direction.
(a) V = 1.44×10³ V · (b) V = 0 but E = 1.73×10⁴ N·C⁻¹

Part (b) is the classic trap in one picture: V = 0 does not mean E = 0. The converse case is the midpoint of the pair in part (a): there the two field vectors are equal and opposite so E = 0, while V = 2kq/(0.030 m) = 2.4×10³ V. E = 0 does not mean V = 0 either. They are independent statements about a point.

Signs and work. Positive charges accelerate from high V toward low V; negative charges accelerate from low V toward high V. Both are moving toward lower potential energy — which is the statement to trust, because U = qV flips sign with q while V does not. Formally, W(field) = −ΔU = −qΔV, so if a proton falls through ΔV = −100 V it gains U → K of +1.6×10⁻¹⁷ J.

The electron-volt exists to make those numbers readable. An electron accelerated from rest through 100 V arrives with 100 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁷ J, giving v = √(2K/m) = √(2 × 1.60×10⁻¹⁷ / 9.11×10⁻³¹) = 5.9×10⁶ m·s⁻¹, about 2% of c — safely non-relativistic. Push the tube to 100 kV, as in a diagnostic X-ray unit, and the electrons carry 100 keV; there the non-relativistic formula is already ~14% wrong, and the electrons produce bremsstrahlung X-rays with a sharp maximum photon energy of exactly 100 keV. The kV setting on an X-ray or CT machine is literally choosing the top of the photon energy spectrum.

Trap · 1/r for potential, 1/r² for field

Potential of a point charge is kQ/r; the field is kQ/r². Likewise the interaction energy of a pair is kq₁q₂/r while the force is kq₁q₂/r². The energy expression also keeps the signs: two like charges have U > 0 (you had to push them together), an unlike pair has U < 0 (bound).

§ 08

Equipotentials and E = −dV/dx

Definition & formula

Equipotential surface
The locus of all points at the same potential. Moving a charge anywhere along it costs zero work.
Field as a gradient
The electric field is the negative rate of change of potential with position: it points "downhill", in the direction in which V falls most steeply.

E and V are not two independent things; V is the potential-energy landscape per unit charge, and E is its slope. The minus sign encodes the physics: a positive charge is pushed down the potential hill, exactly as a ball rolls down a gravitational one. Two operational consequences: where the equipotentials are closely spaced, the field is strong (steep hill); where V is flat, E = 0.

Why equipotentials are always perpendicular to field lines. Suppose E had a component along an equipotential surface. Then moving a test charge a small distance along that surface would do work W = qE∥Δl ≠ 0, so ΔV ≠ 0 — contradicting the assumption that the surface is an equipotential. The only escape is E∥ = 0, i.e. E ⟂ surface. This is the same argument that made the field meet a conductor at right angles in §6, since the surface of a conductor is an equipotential.

Exam point · reading E off a V–x graph

Given a graph of V against x, the field is minus the gradient. A flat region (V constant) ⟹ Ex = 0. A steeply falling region ⟹ large positive Ex. A rising region ⟹ Ex is negative, i.e. the field points in −x. Students routinely forget the minus sign and get the direction backwards; the check is that E must always point from high V to low V.

Trap · "no work" does not mean "no force"

Sliding a charge around an equipotential takes zero work, but the field is still pushing on it the whole time — the force is simply perpendicular to the motion, exactly like the tension in a conical pendulum or the normal force on a level floor. Zero work, non-zero force.

§ 09

Capacitance, parallel plates and dielectrics

Definition & formula

Capacitance
The charge a conductor pair stores per volt of potential difference between them. A property of the geometry and the filling material alone — it does not depend on Q or V.
The farad
One farad is one coulomb per volt. It is an enormous unit; real capacitors run from pF (10⁻¹²) to mF (10⁻³).
Dielectric constant κ
The factor by which an insulating filler multiplies capacitance, equal to the factor by which the material's polarisation reduces the field inside it for a given free charge.
C = Q / V [F = C·V⁻¹] definition; Q is the charge on one plate
C = κ ε₀ A / dparallel plates, d ≪ plate dimensions (no fringing)

Ccapacitance (F, farad)

κdielectric constant (dimensionless)

A capacitor is any pair of conductors carrying equal and opposite charge. Its capacitance is fixed once you fix the geometry: charge the thing more heavily and V rises in exact proportion, leaving Q/V unchanged. For parallel plates the derivation is three lines of Gauss's law: E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A) between the plates, V = Ed = Qd/(ε₀A), so C = Q/V = ε₀A/d. Everything the formula says is intuitive — bigger plates hold more charge at the same voltage, and a smaller gap makes the plates pull harder on each other's charge.

Insert a dielectric and its molecules polarise: permanent dipoles rotate to align with the field, and even non-polar molecules acquire an induced moment p = αE. The layer of bound charge that appears on each face of the slab is opposite to the free charge on the adjacent plate, so it partly cancels it. The field in the gap falls from E₀ to E₀/κ, the voltage falls with it, and — since C = Q/V — the capacitance rises by κ. A dielectric also does a second, practical job: its breakdown field is far higher than air's, so the capacitor survives more volts before it arcs.

Worked example 6 · a real parallel-plate capacitor

Two square plates 5.0 cm × 5.0 cm are separated by d = 0.10 mm of air and connected to a 12 V battery. Find C, Q, the field in the gap and the surface charge density.

  1. Geometry. A = (0.050)² = 2.5×10⁻³ m²; d = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m.
  2. Capacitance (κ ≈ 1 for air). C = ε₀A/d = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(2.5×10⁻³)/(1.0×10⁻⁴) = 2.21×10⁻¹⁰ F = 221 pF.
  3. Charge. Q = CV = (2.2125×10⁻¹⁰)(12) = 2.66×10⁻⁹ C = 2.7 nC — about 1.7×10¹⁰ electrons moved from one plate to the other.
  4. Field. E = V/d = 12 / 1.0×10⁻⁴ = 1.2×10⁵ V·m⁻¹, comfortably below the 3×10⁶ V·m⁻¹ breakdown field of air.
  5. Surface charge density. σ = ε₀E = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(1.2×10⁵) = 1.06×10⁻⁶ C·m⁻²; cross-check Q/A = 2.655×10⁻⁹/2.5×10⁻³ = 1.06×10⁻⁶ C·m⁻² ✓.
C = 221 pF · Q = 2.7 nC · E = 1.2×10⁵ V·m⁻¹ · σ = 1.06 μC·m⁻²

Sliding in a dielectric: battery connected or disconnected?

This is the single most examined capacitor question, and the whole answer is deciding which quantity is held fixed. If the battery stays connected, V is clamped. If the battery is removed first, Q is stranded on the plates and cannot change. Everything else follows from C → κC.

Trap · "the energy went up, so where did it come from?"

With the battery connected, U increases by a factor κ. That energy is not free: the battery pumps an extra charge (κ−1)Q through a p.d. V, doing work (κ−1)QV, of which exactly half ends up as extra field energy and half is delivered as mechanical work on the slab plus resistive loss. With the battery disconnected, U falls by κ — the field itself does the work of pulling the slab in. Same slab, same κ, opposite sign of ΔU. Always identify the clamped variable first.

§ 10

Energy in a capacitor, energy in the field

Definition & formula

Stored energy
The work done separating the charge, recoverable when the capacitor discharges. The factor ½ appears because the voltage rises linearly from 0 to V as you charge it, so the average p.d. against which charge is moved is V/2.
Energy density
Energy per unit volume stored in the electric field itself — a statement that the field, not the plates, is where the energy lives.
U = ½ Q V = ½ C V² = Q² / (2C) three forms of one quantity; pick the one whose variables are fixed

The three expressions are algebraically identical — substitute C = Q/V and they collapse into each other — but they are not equally convenient. Use ½CV² when the capacitor is across a battery (V fixed); use Q²/2C when it has been disconnected (Q fixed). Choosing the wrong one is how students conclude that inserting a dielectric both raises and lowers the energy.

The factor of ½ has a physical reading. Moving the very first electron across the empty gap costs nothing, because V = 0; moving the last one costs the full qV. Since V climbs linearly with Q, the total work is the area under a straight line from 0 to V — a triangle, hence ½QV. Compare this with W = qV for a charge crossing a battery, where the p.d. is held constant and there is no ½.

Where the energy actually is. Take the parallel-plate case and substitute: U = ½CV² = ½(ε₀A/d)(Ed)² = ½ε₀E² × (Ad). The bracket is the volume between the plates, so the energy per unit volume is u = ½ε₀E². This is far more than a trick: it holds for any electric field, and it is the reason electromagnetic waves can carry energy across empty space with no charges present at all.

Worked example 7 · two routes to the same energy

For the 221 pF capacitor of Worked example 6 (A = 2.5×10⁻³ m², d = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m, V = 12 V, E = 1.2×10⁵ V·m⁻¹), find the stored energy twice — from the circuit variables and from the field.

  1. Route 1 — circuit. U = ½CV² = ½(2.2125×10⁻¹⁰)(12)² = ½ × 2.2125×10⁻¹⁰ × 144 = 1.59×10⁻⁸ J.
  2. Route 2 — field energy density. u = ½ε₀E² = ½(8.85×10⁻¹²)(1.2×10⁵)² = ½(8.85×10⁻¹²)(1.44×10¹⁰) = 0.0637 J·m⁻³.
  3. Multiply by the field volume. Ad = (2.5×10⁻³)(1.0×10⁻⁴) = 2.5×10⁻⁷ m³, so U = 0.06372 × 2.5×10⁻⁷ = 1.59×10⁻⁸ J ✓.
  4. Reality check. 16 nJ is tiny — enough to lift a 1 mg grain of sand about 1.6 mm. To store useful energy you need microfarads and kilovolts, which is exactly what a defibrillator does (§11).
U = 1.59×10⁻⁸ J by both routes · u = 6.4×10⁻² J·m⁻³

Exam point · U scales as the square

Double the voltage on a fixed capacitor and the stored energy quadruples (U ∝ V²). Double the charge on a disconnected capacitor and the energy also quadruples (U ∝ Q²). But double the capacitance and the answer depends on what is held fixed: at fixed V the energy doubles, at fixed Q it halves.

Both plate pairs share the same two nodes, so they share the same V and their charges add — equivalent to one capacitor with the combined plate area.

Why reversed Because C = Q/V is a "how much per volt" quantity — it plays the role of conductance 1/R, not resistance. Geometrically: putting capacitors in parallel is like widening the plates (C ∝ A, so add), and putting them in series is like thickening the gap (C ∝ 1/d, so add reciprocals).

A quick example to fix it: 3.0 μF and 6.0 μF in parallel give 9.0 μF; the same pair in series give 1/C = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 = 1/2.0, so C = 2.0 μF — smaller than either. In series the charge on each is identical, because the isolated conductor between the two capacitors must stay neutral: whatever +Q appears on one side, −Q is induced on the other.

Memory hook

Capacitors are upside-down resistors. Series capacitors behave like parallel resistors, and vice versa — because capacitance measures charge per volt, the way conductance measures current per volt.

The cell membrane is a leaky parallel-plate capacitor

A lipid bilayer is a ~5 nm sheet of hydrocarbon — an excellent insulator — with a conducting electrolyte on both sides. That is, geometrically and electrically, a parallel-plate capacitor with conducting "plates" made of ionic solution. Its measured specific capacitance is close to 1 μF·cm⁻² in essentially every cell examined, which is a strong clue that the dielectric thickness is the same everywhere: the hydrocarbon core of the bilayer. In parallel with that capacitance sit the ion channels, which act as conductances; the product of membrane resistance and capacitance gives the membrane time constant τ = RmCm (typically 1–20 ms), which is what sets how quickly a neurone's potential can follow its synaptic input. Every equation in cable theory and every voltage-clamp record rests on this one identification.

Worked example 8 · what the membrane's capacitance tells you

Take cm = 1.0 μF·cm⁻² and a resting potential of 70 mV. (a) If the bilayer's hydrocarbon core has κ ≈ 2.2, how thick is it? (b) How many monovalent ions per μm² of membrane are actually separated? (c) For a spherical cell of radius 10 μm, what fraction of the intracellular K⁺ is that?

  1. Convert the units. 1.0 μF·cm⁻² = 1.0×10⁻⁶ F / 1.0×10⁻⁴ m² = 1.0×10⁻² F·m⁻².
  2. (a) Thickness. From cm = κε₀/d, d = κε₀/cm = (2.2)(8.85×10⁻¹²)/(1.0×10⁻²) = 1.9×10⁻⁹ m ≈ 2 nm — which is exactly the hydrophobic core, not the full ~5 nm bilayer. The headgroups are hydrated and conduct; only the acyl-chain region is the dielectric.
  3. (b) Charge separated. σ = cmV = (1.0×10⁻²)(0.070) = 7.0×10⁻⁴ C·m⁻². Divide by e: 7.0×10⁻⁴/1.60×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.4×10¹⁵ ions·m⁻² = ≈ 4.4×10³ ions per μm².
  4. (c) Fraction of the cell's K⁺. Area = 4πr² = 1.26×10⁻⁹ m², so C = 1.26×10⁻¹¹ F ≈ 13 pF and Q = CV = 8.8×10⁻¹³ C ≈ 5.5×10⁶ ions. The cell volume is 4.19×10⁻¹⁵ m³ = 4.19×10⁻¹² L, holding 0.140 M × 4.19×10⁻¹² L × 6.02×10²³ = 3.5×10¹¹ K⁺ ions. Ratio = 5.5×10⁶/3.5×10¹¹ ≈ 1.6×10⁻⁵.
d ≈ 2 nm · ≈4.4×10³ ions·μm⁻² · only ~0.002% of intracellular K⁺ is separated

Part (c) is why an action potential does not measurably change ion concentrations: the membrane potential is a capacitive phenomenon involving a vanishing fraction of the cell's ions. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is restoring gradients over minutes, not rescuing each spike.

Exam point · the field across a membrane is enormous

E = V/d = 0.070 V / 5.0×10⁻⁹ m ≈ 1.4×10⁷ V·m⁻¹ — four to five times the breakdown field of air, sustained continuously by a 5 nm film. Two consequences worth knowing: voltage-gated channels have charged S4 helices that can sense this field directly, and a modest external pulse (a few hundred mV across the membrane) pushes it past dielectric breakdown, which is the mechanism of electroporation. Using the ~2 nm dielectric core instead gives ~3.6×10⁷ V·m⁻¹; either figure makes the point.

The defibrillator

Worked example 9 · defibrillator energy, charge and power

A defibrillator stores charge on a C = 100 μF capacitor charged to 2.5 kV, then discharges it through the chest in about 5.0 ms. Find the stored energy, the charge, the average power and the average current.

  1. Energy. U = ½CV² = ½(1.00×10⁻⁴)(2.5×10³)² = ½(1.00×10⁻⁴)(6.25×10⁶) = 3.1×10² J — in the clinical range of 150–360 J for a monophasic shock.
  2. Charge. Q = CV = (1.00×10⁻⁴)(2.5×10³) = 0.25 C.
  3. Average power. P = U/t = 312.5 / 5.0×10⁻³ = 6.3×10⁴ W. The energy is modest — that of a 1 kg book falling 32 m — but delivering it in milliseconds means tens of kilowatts.
  4. Average current. I = Q/t = 0.25 / 5.0×10⁻³ = 50 A — but that number assumes the whole 0.25 C really leaves in 5.0 ms, which needs τ = RC ≲ 2 ms, i.e. a load of roughly 20 Ω. A real chest is 50–100 Ω, so τ = RC ≈ 100 μF × 75 Ω ≈ 7.5 ms, the peak current is only I₀ = V/R = 2.5×10³/75 ≈ 33 A, and barely half the charge has flowed by 5.0 ms. That is precisely why clinical units truncate the waveform and quote delivered energy, which is always less than the stored ½CV².
U ≈ 310 J stored · Q = 0.25 C · P ≈ 63 kW and I ≈ 50 A if the discharge completes in 5.0 ms

The physics content of defibrillation is exactly the capacitor content of this chapter: a capacitor is the only practical way to store a few hundred joules and then release it in milliseconds, because the discharge rate is set by RC and not by the source. The physiology is worth stating correctly, because it is commonly misdescribed. A defibrillator does not restart a stopped heart. It depolarises a critical mass of myocardium simultaneously, ending the chaotic re-entrant wavefronts of ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia and giving the sinoatrial node a chance to resume an organised rhythm. Asystole and pulseless electrical activity are not shockable — there is no fibrillation to stop. Modern biphasic units reverse the current partway through the pulse, which achieves the same defibrillation at lower delivered energy (typically 120–200 J) and less myocardial injury.

Trap · joules are not volts

The dial on a defibrillator reads joules, an energy, set by ½CV². Doubling the selected energy does not double the voltage — it multiplies it by √2. And a charged defibrillator capacitor remains lethal after the machine is switched off; a capacitor is a store of energy, not a store of current, and it holds that store until something discharges it.
