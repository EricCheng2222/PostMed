Physics · Note 15

Modern Physics: Quanta, Atoms and Nuclei

Classical physics assumed that energy is continuous — that you can always take a little less. Every experiment in this chapter says the opposite: energy, angular momentum and even identity come in indivisible lumps, and a lump is either absorbed whole or not at all. Once you accept that single idea, the ultraviolet catastrophe, the threshold frequency, the line spectrum of hydrogen, the sharp cut-off of an X-ray tube and the exponential decay of a radionuclide stop being separate facts and become the same fact seen from five angles. Nearly every imaging machine in a hospital is an application of this chapter.

§ 01

Where classical physics broke: the blackbody

The ideaA warm object glows, and the mix of colours in the glow is decided by its temperature and by nothing else about it. When physicists first worked out how much of each colour should come off, the sums said that any warm thing ought to pour out limitless amounts of the shortest-wave light — which radiators, embers and people plainly do not do. The escape was to suppose that the tiny vibrating pieces of matter doing the emitting cannot hand over any amount of energy they please: they can only pay in whole packets, and the packet for short-wave light is so expensive that most of the time nothing can afford one. That single supposition is where the whole of this note begins.

A hot object glows, and the colour of the glow depends only on how hot it is — dull red at 900 K, orange at 1500 K, white at 6000 K. Classical physics could reproduce the long-wavelength end of that curve and nothing else. The Rayleigh–Jeans calculation counts the standing-wave modes inside a cavity and gives each one the equipartition share kBT of energy. The number of modes grows as 1/λ⁴, so the predicted intensity grows without limit as the wavelength shortens.

That is the ultraviolet catastrophe: the classical theory says a warm object should pour out infinite power in the ultraviolet and beyond. It does not, and the failure is not a small numerical discrepancy — it is a divergence. Something in the classical assumptions is structurally wrong.

Planck's fix was to forbid an oscillator from holding any energy it liked. If a wall oscillator of frequency f can only hold 0, hf, 2hf, …, then a high-frequency mode needs a whole quantum hf before it can be excited at all. When hf ≫ kBT the thermal bath simply cannot afford the entry price, so the short-wavelength modes stay empty. The mode counting is untouched — it is still 1/λ⁴, and multiplying it by the quantum energy hc/λ is what produces the 1/λ⁵ prefactor of Planck's law; what is new is the exponential factor 1/(ehc/λkBT − 1), which shuts it down. The divergence disappears, and out of the same formula fall both Wien's law and Stefan's law.

Worked example 1 · Wien's law and thermal imaging

At what wavelength does human skin at 33 °C radiate most strongly, and where does the Sun (surface ≈ 5800 K) peak?

  1. Convert to kelvin — Wien's law takes absolute temperature only: T = 33 + 273 = 306 K.
  2. Skin: λmax = b/T = 2.90×10⁻³ / 306 = 9.48×10⁻⁶ m ≈ 9.5 μm.
  3. Sun: λmax = 2.90×10⁻³ / 5800 = 5.00×10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm.
  4. Interpretation: skin peaks in the mid-infrared, roughly twenty times longer than visible light — which is why a thermal camera for fever screening is built around an 8–14 μm detector and sees nothing in the visible. The Sun peaks in the green, near the middle of the range our eyes evolved to use.
skin λmax ≈ 9.5 μm (infrared) · Sun λmax ≈ 500 nm (visible)

Trap · Wien's law needs kelvin, and it is inverse

Putting 33 into b/T instead of 306 gives 88 μm — an order of magnitude wrong. And the relation is inverse: hotter means shorter λmax. Students routinely write "the hotter body radiates at longer wavelength" because the total power goes up; the total power (T⁴) and the peak position (1/T) move in opposite senses along the λ axis.

§ 02

The photoelectric effect

The ideaShine light on a clean piece of metal and electrons are knocked out of it. What decides whether any come out at all is not how bright the light is but its colour: past a certain colour nothing happens, however bright the lamp and however long you wait, and on the other side of it electrons appear the instant you switch on, however feeble the source. That only makes sense if light arrives in separate packets and one electron takes one packet — turning the lamp up sends more packets, not bigger ones. Each metal charges its own fixed price to release an electron, and whatever the packet has left after paying it becomes the electron's speed.

Worked example 2 · Sodium under violet light

Sodium has φ = 2.28 eV. Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on it. Find the photon energy, Kmax, the stopping potential, and the threshold wavelength and frequency.

  1. Photon energy with the shortcut hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm: E = 1240/400 = 3.10 eV.
  2. Kmax = hf − φ = 3.10 − 2.28 = 0.82 eV.
  3. Stopping potential: Kmax = eVs, and because Kmax is already in electronvolts, Vs = 0.82 V numerically. (In joules: 0.82 × 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.31×10⁻¹⁹ J, divided by e gives the same 0.82 V.)
  4. Threshold: λ₀ = hc/φ = 1240/2.28 = 544 nm — green light. Anything redder than 544 nm produces no emission at all.
  5. f₀ = c/λ₀ = 3.00×10⁸ / 5.44×10⁻⁷ = 5.5×10¹⁴ Hz (equivalently φ/h = 2.28×1.60×10⁻¹⁹ / 6.63×10⁻³⁴ = 5.5×10¹⁴ Hz).
E = 3.10 eV · Kmax = 0.82 eV · Vs = 0.82 V · λ₀ = 544 nm · f₀ = 5.5×10¹⁴ Hz

Exam point · the three things a Kmax-vs-f graph gives you

Gradient = h (universal). x-intercept = f₀ (threshold). y-intercept = −φ (negative). Examiners love asking you to determine h from two data points: take h = (K₂ − K₁)/(f₂ − f₁) and convert eV to joules before dividing, or your answer will be out by a factor of 1.6×10⁻¹⁹.

Trap · "Kmax", not "K"

Only electrons released from the very surface with no collision losses emerge with hf − φ. Electrons from deeper down lose energy on the way out, so the emitted electrons have a spread of energies from 0 up to Kmax. That is why the photocurrent falls off gradually as the retarding voltage is raised and only vanishes at Vs. Also: φ is subtracted, never added — Kmax = hf + φ is the single most common sign error on this topic.

Why a doctor cares. Every scintillation detector — the gamma camera, the PET ring, the dosimeter badge reader — ends in a photomultiplier or photodiode whose first step is exactly this effect: a light photon liberates one electron, which is then multiplied into a measurable pulse. The threshold behaviour is also why UV-B (280–315 nm, i.e. 3.9–4.4 eV per photon) damages DNA while intense infrared merely warms you: the damage depends on the energy per photon, not the energy per second.

§ 03

The photon: energy, momentum and the Compton effect

The ideaA packet of light behaves like a particle in a fuller sense than merely carrying a fixed amount of energy: it also carries a shove. Fire one at a loosely held electron and the two rebound like billiard balls — the electron is knocked away, and the light that leaves is stretched to a slightly longer wave than the light that arrived, because it gave some of its energy up. How much it is stretched depends only on the angle it was deflected through, not on the kind of light and not on the material it struck. This section is also where you meet the most useful shortcut in the note: a one-line way of swapping between the colour of light and the energy of a single packet of it.

Momentum: the harder half of the photon

A photon has no rest mass, so E = pc exactly, giving p = E/c = h/λ. This is not a formality. In 1923 Compton fired X-rays at graphite and found that the scattered beam contained a component of longer wavelength, with the shift depending on angle and on nothing else — not on the incident wavelength, not on the target material. A wave scattering off a charge re-radiates at the same frequency, so a wave theory forbids the shift entirely.

Treat it instead as a billiard-ball collision between a photon and a free electron, conserving relativistic energy and momentum, and out drops Δλ = (h/mec)(1 − cos θ). The photon gives kinetic energy to the recoiling electron, so it must lose energy, so its wavelength must grow. The shift runs from 0 at θ = 0° (straight ahead, undeflected, so nothing is transferred) through 2.43 pm at 90° to a maximum of 4.85 pm at 180° (backscatter).

Trap · why you never see the Compton shift in visible light

Δλ is an absolute shift of a few picometres, so what matters is the fraction Δλ/λ. For 550 nm light at 90°, that is 2.43×10⁻³ nm / 550 nm ≈ 4×10⁻⁶ — undetectable. For a 0.05 nm X-ray it is 5%, easily measured. Compton needed X-rays not because the effect is different there but because the fraction is.

Exam point · Compton scattering in the clinic

Above about 30 keV, Compton scattering is the dominant photon interaction in soft tissue, and its probability depends on electron density, which barely differs between fat, muscle and water. That is precisely why it carries almost no diagnostic information — Compton-scattered photons arrive at the detector from the wrong direction and wash out contrast, which is why anti-scatter grids exist. Photoelectric absorption, whose probability scales roughly as Z³/E³, is what makes bone (Zeff ≈ 13) stand out against soft tissue (Zeff ≈ 7.4), and it is why kVp is kept low when contrast matters.

A PET photon of 511 keV Compton-scattered through 90° emerges at E′ = E / [1 + (E/mec²)(1 − cos θ)] = 511/(1 + 1) = 256 keV — which is why PET scanners set an energy window around 511 keV and discard everything below it.

§ 04

Wave–particle duality: de Broglie and uncertainty

The ideaIf something everyone had called a wave turns out to behave like a particle, the obvious next question is whether the things everyone calls particles behave like waves. They do: send electrons at a regular array of atoms and they spread and interfere exactly as light does through a fine grating. The heavier and faster the object, the shorter its wave, which is why an electron shows the effect and a thrown ball never could. And once a particle is also a wave, it can no longer have a sharp position and a sharp speed at the same moment — pin it into a small space and its speed becomes correspondingly vague. That is a fact about what those two words can mean together, not a complaint about the equipment.

De Broglie's argument in 1924 was one of symmetry. Light, long believed to be a wave, behaves as a particle when it exchanges energy. If nature is symmetric, then the electron, long believed to be a particle, should behave as a wave when it propagates. The relation λ = h/p is simply p = h/λ read backwards. Davisson and Germer confirmed it three years later by diffracting electrons off a nickel crystal and finding a Bragg pattern at exactly the predicted angles.

Worked example 3 · An electron and a baseball

Find the de Broglie wavelength of (a) an electron accelerated from rest through 100 V, and (b) a 0.145 kg baseball thrown at 40 m·s⁻¹. Comment.

  1. Electron. Work is done on it: K = eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 100 = 1.60×10⁻¹⁷ J. At 100 eV the electron is thoroughly non-relativistic (100 eV ≪ 511 keV), so p = √(2mK) = √(2 × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 1.60×10⁻¹⁷) = √(2.92×10⁻⁴⁷) = 5.40×10⁻²⁴ kg·m·s⁻¹.
  2. λ = h/p = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / 5.40×10⁻²⁴ = 1.23×10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.123 nm. That is the size of an atom and roughly the spacing of planes in a crystal — hence electron diffraction works.
  3. Baseball. p = mv = 0.145 × 40 = 5.8 kg·m·s⁻¹, so λ = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ / 5.8 = 1.1×10⁻³⁴ m.
  4. Compare: a nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁵ m across, so the baseball's wavelength is 10¹⁹ times smaller than a nucleus. Diffraction requires an aperture comparable to λ. No such aperture exists or could exist. The wave nature is not switched off for large objects — it is simply pushed below any conceivable measurement.
electron λ = 0.123 nm (atomic scale) · baseball λ ≈ 1×10⁻³⁴ m (unobservable)

Memory hook

λ = h/p, and h is 6.63×10⁻³⁴. To get a wavelength of ordinary size you need a momentum of extraordinary smallness. Only subatomic particles qualify.

Go deeper · the payoff — how a wavelength of a few picometres out-resolves a light microscope

The payoff: the electron microscope

Any microscope's resolution is limited to roughly half a wavelength of whatever it images with. Visible light at 550 nm caps a light microscope at about 200 nm — good enough for a cell, useless for a ribosome or a virion. Accelerate electrons through 100 kV and λ ≈ 1.23/√100000 nm = 3.9 pm non-relativistically (3.7 pm once the relativistic correction is applied at γ ≈ 1.2). That is 10⁵ times shorter than visible light. Lens aberrations, not diffraction, then become the limit, and practical instruments reach about 0.1 nm — enough to see individual atomic columns, protein complexes and virus capsids. The entire discipline of structural cell biology rests on λ = h/p.

Uncertainty

A wave with a perfectly sharp wavelength — and therefore a perfectly sharp momentum — is an infinite sinusoid, which is spread over all space. To localise a particle you must superpose many wavelengths, which smears its momentum. The trade-off is exact: Δx · Δpx ≥ ħ/2. It is a property of waves, not of microscopes; a perfect apparatus obeys it too.

Estimate. Confine an electron to an atom, Δx ≈ 1×10⁻¹⁰ m. Then Δp ≥ ħ/(2Δx) = 1.055×10⁻³⁴ / (2×10⁻¹⁰) = 5.3×10⁻²⁵ kg·m·s⁻¹, so Δv ≈ 5.3×10⁻²⁵/9.11×10⁻³¹ ≈ 5.8×10⁵ m·s⁻¹ — the same order as the 2.2×10⁶ m·s⁻¹ that the Bohr model assigns to the ground-state electron. The uncertainty principle alone tells you an atom cannot be smaller than it is: squeeze the electron in and its momentum spread, and therefore its kinetic energy, blows up. That is what stops the electron falling into the nucleus.

The energy–time form, ΔE·Δt ≥ ħ/2, says a state that lives only for a time Δt cannot have a perfectly sharp energy. An excited atomic state with a lifetime of 10⁻⁸ s has a natural linewidth of at least ΔE ≈ 1.055×10⁻³⁴/(2×10⁻⁸) = 5.3×10⁻²⁷ J ≈ 3×10⁻⁸ eV. Spectral lines are never infinitely thin; short-lived states give broad lines, which is how nuclear and particle physicists measure lifetimes they cannot time directly.

Trap · three ways students misuse λ = h/p

(1) Writing λ = h/(mc) for a photon. A photon has no mass; use p = E/c. (2) Using p = mv for a fast electron in a linac or an electron microscope above ~100 kV, where γ matters. (3) Using E = hc/λ for a matter wave. For a photon E = pc so E = hc/λ; for an electron E = p²/2m, so the kinetic energy goes as 1/λ², not 1/λ.

§ 05

Atomic models and the Bohr hydrogen atom

The ideaAn atom is almost entirely empty, with its positive charge and nearly all of its mass packed into a speck at the centre — which is why a few heavy particles fired at gold foil come straight back at you instead of sailing through. But a charged particle circling that speck ought, by the older rules, to radiate its energy away and fall in almost at once, so matter should not last a moment. The repair is that an electron in an atom cannot sit at any energy it likes: there is a fixed ladder of allowed energies, it radiates nothing while it stays on a rung, and light comes out only when it drops to a lower one. The size of the drop sets the colour, which is why each element gives out its own set of sharp colours instead of a smear.

Thomson (1904). Having discovered the electron, Thomson pictured the atom as a sphere of diffuse positive charge with electrons embedded in it — the "plum pudding". It explains why atoms are neutral and why they emit electrons, and nothing else.

Rutherford (1909–11). Geiger and Marsden fired α particles at a gold foil a few hundred atoms thick. Almost all passed through with a deflection under 1°, as the pudding model predicts. But about one in 8000 came back through more than 90°. A diffuse charge cannot do that: the Coulomb force from spread-out charge is far too weak, and a light scatterer would recoil rather than reflect. Rutherford's conclusion — "as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you" — was that the positive charge and essentially all the mass sit in a nucleus smaller than 10⁻¹⁴ m, about 10⁴ times smaller than the atom, i.e. 10⁻¹² of its volume. The atom is overwhelmingly empty.

The problem Rutherford created. An orbiting electron is an accelerating charge, and classical electrodynamics says an accelerating charge radiates. The electron should spiral into the nucleus in about 10⁻¹¹ s, emitting a continuous spectrum on the way. Matter should not exist, and the observed spectrum of hydrogen is not continuous but a set of razor-sharp lines.

Bohr (1913). Bohr fixed both problems with one postulate and one rule. Postulate: only orbits with L = nħ are allowed, and in them the electron does not radiate. Rule: radiation is emitted only when the electron jumps from one allowed orbit to a lower one, with the photon carrying exactly the energy difference. Setting the Coulomb force equal to the centripetal force and imposing L = nħ gives rn = n²a₀ and En = −13.6/n² eV, and the Rydberg formula that spectroscopists had fitted empirically for thirty years drops out with the right constant.

Emission versus absorption

An emission spectrum is bright lines on a dark background: excite the gas, and every downward jump that occurs contributes its own line. An absorption spectrum is dark lines on a continuous background: shine white light through cool gas and the atoms remove exactly those photons whose energy matches an upward jump. The lines sit at the same wavelengths — the level diagram is the same — but the population matters. Cool hydrogen has essentially all its atoms in n = 1, so it can only absorb Lyman transitions; the Balmer absorption lines seen in stellar spectra require the gas to be hot enough to keep a decent population in n = 2.

Trap · levels are negative, transitions are differences

En is negative because a bound electron has less energy than a free one at rest; E = 0 is the ionised state, not the ground state. Three errors follow. (1) Quoting the photon energy of a 3→2 jump as 1.51 eV (that is |E₃|, not the difference). (2) Forgetting that ionisation from n = 2 costs only 3.40 eV, not 13.6 eV. (3) Assuming an electron with 10 eV of energy can excite a ground-state hydrogen atom — it cannot, because the smallest available jump is 10.2 eV, so the collision is perfectly elastic. That last one is the Franck–Hertz experiment in miniature.

Exam point · the limits of Bohr

Bohr's model works quantitatively for one-electron systems only: H, He⁺, Li²⁺, with En = −13.6Z²/n² eV and rn = n²a₀/Z. It fails for helium onwards (electron–electron repulsion), predicts no fine structure, gives no line intensities, and assigns the ground state an angular momentum of ħ when the true value is 0. It survives in exams because its energy formula is right and its picture is teachable — not because it is correct.

§ 06

Beyond Bohr: the quantum atom in four numbers

The ideaThe tidy picture of an electron running round a circular track is wrong, even though the energies it predicts for hydrogen are right. The better picture treats the electron as a standing wave filling a cloud around the centre, so the allowed states are no longer assumed — they are whatever shapes fit, in the way the notes of a guitar string are whatever fits between its ends. Each state is then labelled by four numbers: which shell it belongs to, what shape it has, which way that shape points, and which way the electron's own built-in twist points. Add one rule — no two electrons in an atom may carry the same four labels — and you have the reason electrons stack up in shells rather than all crowding into the lowest one, which is to say the reason chemistry exists.

Exam point · nuclear spin is the same idea, and it is why MRI works

A proton also has spin ½, so in a magnetic field its two orientations have slightly different energies. MRI drives transitions between these two nuclear spin states — a direct, everyday application of space quantisation. The one-line summary: spin plus a field equals a two-level system, and a two-level system can be resonantly driven.

§ 07

X-rays: production, spectrum and attenuation

The ideaFire fast electrons into a heavy metal and they are dragged off course by the atoms they pass, shedding very energetic light as they slow. Some are slowed gently and some are stopped dead, so the light emerges at every energy up to a sharp ceiling — and that ceiling is set purely by the voltage used to accelerate the electrons, since nothing can give out more than it arrived with. Sitting on top of the smooth spread are a few very sharp spikes, made when an electron is knocked clean out of the innermost layer of a target atom; where they fall is a fingerprint of which metal the target is. Once the beam exists, what matters in a clinic is how it fades on the way through a body: every equal slab of tissue takes the same fraction of what reaches it, never the same amount, so the beam is always weakened and never entirely stopped.

An X-ray tube accelerates electrons from a hot filament through 20–150 kV onto a heavy metal target (tungsten for general radiography, molybdenum for mammography). Under 1% of the beam energy becomes X-rays; the rest becomes heat, which is why anodes rotate. Two production mechanisms operate at once and give two overlapping spectra.

The continuum. An electron passing a nucleus is deflected and decelerated, radiating a photon. The deflection can be anything from a graze to a head-on stop, so the emitted photon energy can be anything from nearly zero up to a maximum. The maximum is set by energy conservation: an electron that loses all its kinetic energy eV in a single event gives one photon of energy eV, and there is nothing more available. That defines the short-wavelength cut-off λmin = hc/eV. It is the crispest quantum signature in radiology: a purely classical theory would have no cut-off at all.

The lines. If the incident electron carries more than the K-shell binding energy of the target, it can eject a K electron; an L or M electron then drops into the hole and emits a photon of a fixed energy — Kα or Kβ. These sit at wavelengths fixed by the target element and are completely unaffected by the tube voltage, provided the voltage is above the K-edge at all. Tungsten's Kα is 59 keV; molybdenum's is 17.5 keV, which is deliberately chosen for mammography because breast tissue needs low-energy photons for soft-tissue contrast.

Attenuation and why an X-ray image has contrast

Photons are removed from a narrow beam one at a time, and the probability of removal in a thin slab is proportional to the slab's thickness. That is the definition of an exponential: dI = −μI dx ⇒ I = I₀e−μx. The coefficient μ bundles together photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and (above 1.02 MeV) pair production, and it depends strongly on both photon energy and atomic number. Because the law is exponential, the intensity never reaches zero — there is no thickness that stops all the radiation, only thicknesses that make what gets through negligible.

Worked example 5 · Radiographic contrast from the attenuation law

At about 60 keV, soft tissue has μ ≈ 0.20 cm⁻¹ and cortical bone μ ≈ 0.60 cm⁻¹ (approximate textbook values). A beam crosses 10 cm of a limb. Compare the transmitted intensity through pure soft tissue with that through a path of the same length made entirely of bone, and find both half-value layers.

  1. Soft tissue: I/I₀ = e−0.20×10 = e−2.0 = 0.135 — 13.5% gets through.
  2. Bone: I/I₀ = e−0.60×10 = e−6.0 = 0.00248 — 0.25% gets through.
  3. Contrast ratio: 0.135 / 0.00248 = 54. The bony path delivers 54 times fewer photons to the film, which is the entire reason a bone shows up white on a radiograph. Note how the exponential amplifies a threefold difference in μ into a 54-fold difference in signal.
  4. Half-value layers: HVL = ln2/μ gives 0.693/0.20 = 3.5 cm for soft tissue and 0.693/0.60 = 1.2 cm for bone.
  5. Sanity check in HVLs: 10 cm of soft tissue is 10/3.47 = 2.88 HVL, and (½)2.88 = 0.136. Consistent.

Trap · μ, HVL and units

(1) μ and x must use the same length unit; a μ in cm⁻¹ with an x in metres is off by 100. (2) "Two half-value layers" means intensity ×¼, not zero — halving twice never reaches zero. (3) Count half-value layers, not centimetres: 1 HVL of lead and 1 HVL of aluminium are wildly different physical thicknesses, but each halves the beam independently, so together they transmit ¼. Transmissions multiply; thicknesses in cm never add across materials. (4) Real tube output is polyenergetic, so the low-energy photons are removed first and the beam "hardens" as it goes — the first HVL is smaller than the second, and the strict exponential is only exact for a monoenergetic narrow beam.

Exam point · the two knobs on an X-ray tube

kV controls quality (photon energy, penetration, λmin); mA·s controls quantity (number of photons, and hence patient dose and image noise). Increasing kV moves λmin to shorter wavelength and raises total output steeply, but it also reduces subject contrast because photoelectric absorption falls as 1/E³. The characteristic line positions never move. Lead's HVL at 100 keV is only about 0.1 mm, which is why a 0.5 mm lead apron is worth wearing.

§ 08

Special relativity in brief

The ideaEveryone who measures how fast light travels gets the same answer, no matter how fast they themselves are moving. That sounds harmless and is not. A speed is a distance divided by a time, so if the answer is fixed for everybody, two people moving relative to each other must disagree about the distances and about the times. A clock moving past you runs slow, and a moving object is shortened along its direction of travel — both by the same factor, which is so close to one at everyday speeds that nobody notices and which grows without limit as the speed of light is approached. One further consequence matters more here than any other: mass and energy turn out to be the same thing counted in different units, and that is the accounting rule the nuclear sections are written in.

Worked example 6 · Cosmic-ray muons reaching sea level

Muons created about 10 km up in the atmosphere have a mean lifetime of 2.2 μs at rest and travel at 0.995c. Classically, how far should they get? What actually happens?

  1. Classical distance: d = vt = 0.995 × 3.00×10⁸ × 2.2×10⁻⁶ = 657 m. Essentially none should reach the ground — yet detectors at sea level see plenty.
  2. Lorentz factor: γ = 1/√(1 − 0.995²) = 1/√(1 − 0.990025) = 1/0.0999 = 10.0.
  3. In the Earth's frame the muon's internal clock is dilated: Δt = γΔt₀ = 10.0 × 2.2 μs = 22 μs, so the mean distance travelled becomes d = 0.995 × 3.00×10⁸ × 22×10⁻⁶ = 6.6 km. The 10 km descent is then only 10/6.6 = 1.5 dilated lifetimes, so a fraction e−1.5 ≈ 22% survive it — against the classical 10/0.657 = 15 lifetimes and e−15 ≈ 2×10⁻⁷. That is the difference between "none" and "plenty", and the observed sea-level flux matches.
  4. In the muon's own frame nothing dilates; instead the 10 km of atmosphere is contracted to L = L₀/γ = 10/10.0 = 1.0 km, which sweeps past in 1.0×10³ / (0.995 × 3.00×10⁸) = 3.4 μs — the same 1.5 lifetimes and the same 22% survival. Two descriptions, one prediction: relativity is consistent, not paradoxical.
classical 0.66 km · relativistic 6.6 km (γ = 10.0) — muons do reach sea level

Trap · which interval is Δt₀?

Δt₀ is the interval between two events measured by a clock that is present at both — here, the muon's own decay clock. Everyone else measures something longer. The reliable check: γ ≥ 1 always, so a dilated time is always bigger and a contracted length is always smaller. If your answer is a shorter time or a longer length, you have divided where you should have multiplied. Also: contraction acts only along the direction of motion; transverse dimensions are unchanged.

E = mc² and why it belongs in this chapter. The rest energy is not a form of energy the object "has as well" — it is the object, measured in energy units. Total energy is E = γmc²; subtract the rest energy and the remainder is kinetic. The electron's rest energy is mec² = 9.11×10⁻³¹ × (3.00×10⁸)² = 8.20×10⁻¹⁴ J = 0.512 MeV with these rounded constants, 0.511 MeV with exact ones — a number worth memorising because it sets the 511 keV photons of PET and the 1.022 MeV threshold for pair production.

The immediate consequence is that a system's mass changes when its internal energy does. Bind nucleons together and you release energy; the bound system must therefore weigh less than its parts. That missing mass is the mass defect of the next section — E = mc² is not an exotic aside to nuclear physics, it is the accounting identity nuclear physics is written in.

Clinical footnote. A radiotherapy linac accelerates electrons to 6 MeV, so γ = 1 + K/mec² = 1 + 6/0.511 = 12.7 and v = c√(1 − 1/γ²) = 0.997c. Treating those electrons with K = ½mv² would give a speed several times c — the machine in the basement of every cancer centre is a relativistic device.

§ 09

The nucleus: mass defect and binding energy

The ideaWeigh a nucleus and it comes out lighter than the pieces it is made of. The missing weight is energy that escaped when those pieces were pulled together, and it is exactly what you would have to put back to prise them apart again. How tightly each particle is held climbs steeply from the very lightest nuclei, peaks around iron, then falls slowly away again for the heavy ones. So joining light nuclei together and splitting heavy ones both shift matter towards that tightly held middle, and both therefore give energy out. That one curve is why the Sun shines, why a reactor works, and why two processes that sound like opposites both release energy.

§ 10

Radioactive decay and the decay law

The ideaAn unstable nucleus has a fixed chance of breaking up in the next second and no memory of how long it has already waited — it does not age, wear out, or become due. Across a large number of them that fixed chance means a fixed fraction goes in every equal stretch of time, so the population falls by half in some set period, then by half again over the next, and never quite reaches zero. The period belongs to that kind of nucleus alone: heating a sample, freezing it, or locking it into a different chemical compound changes nothing at all. What a detector responds to is how many break-ups happen per second, and that shrinks in step with the number still left.

Since only α changes A, and it changes it by 4, every member of a decay chain has the same value of A mod 4. There are exactly four natural decay series for that reason.

Worked example 8 · Technetium-99m: activity, timing and how little matter is involved

⁹⁹ᵐTc has T½ = 6.0 h. A generator is eluted at 08:00 giving 800 MBq. (a) What is the activity at 17:00? (b) When does it fall to 50 MBq? (c) What mass of ⁹⁹ᵐTc is present in 1.0 GBq?

  1. Decay constant: λ = ln2/T½ = 0.693/6.0 = 0.1155 h⁻¹.
  2. (a) t = 9.0 h, i.e. 1.5 half-lives: A = 800 e−0.1155×9.0 = 800 e−1.040 = 800 × 0.3536 = 283 MBq. Check with the power form: 800 × (½)1.5 = 800/2.828 = 283 MBq. Agreed.
  3. (b) 800 → 50 MBq is a factor of 16 = 2⁴, so exactly 4 half-lives: t = 4 × 6.0 = 24 h, i.e. 08:00 the next morning.
  4. (c) Convert to SI: λ = 0.693/(6.0 × 3600 s) = 3.21×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹. From A = λN, N = A/λ = 1.0×10⁹ / 3.21×10⁻⁵ = 3.12×10¹³ nuclei.
  5. Mass: m = N × (99 g·mol⁻¹) / (6.02×10²³ mol⁻¹) = 3.12×10¹³ × 1.64×10⁻²² g = 5.1×10⁻⁹ g ≈ 5 ng. A full diagnostic dose is a few nanograms — far below any pharmacological threshold, which is exactly why radiotracers can be given safely: they are detected by their radiation, not by their chemistry.

Trap · four things that go wrong with the decay law

(1) The symbol λ here is the decay constant in s⁻¹, not a wavelength. On this page it appears as both; read the units. (2) A half-life does not mean "all gone after two half-lives" — after two, a quarter remains, and the tail is infinite. (3) λ and t must share a unit: h⁻¹ with hours, s⁻¹ with seconds. (4) Activity is decays per second in the source. A detector subtending 1% of the solid angle records 1% of it, so a count rate is never an activity without a geometry and efficiency correction.

§ 11

Dose, radiotherapy and nuclear medicine

The ideaRadiation harms tissue by breaking things one at a time, not by warming it. The energy in an exposure large enough to kill would not raise your temperature by a thousandth of a degree, but it is enough to snap chemical bonds in an astronomical number of separate places, and a few of those places are in the instructions from which your cells are copied. So the quantity worth measuring is how much energy each kilogram of tissue absorbs — and then a second quantity that scales it by how densely the damage is packed along the track, because energy dumped entirely within the width of a few cells does far more harm than the same energy spread thinly. The rest of the section is what happens when those facts are used on purpose: to destroy a tumour, or to build a picture out of a tracer the patient is carrying around inside them.

Worked example 9 · Why radiation is dangerous at absurdly small energies

An acute whole-body absorbed dose of 4 Gy of X-rays is roughly the LD₅₀ for an untreated adult. (a) How much energy is that for a 70 kg person? (b) By how much would that energy warm the body if it all became heat? (c) What is the equivalent dose, and what would it be if the same 4 Gy were delivered by α particles?

  1. (a) E = D × m = 4 × 70 = 280 J — about the food energy in a sixtieth of a gram of sugar, or the work done lifting that same body 0.4 m.
  2. (b) Treating the body as water, c = 4186 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹: ΔT = D/c = 4 / 4186 = 9.6×10⁻⁴ K — one thousandth of a degree.
  3. Conclusion: the lethality has nothing to do with heating. It is that each ~30 eV of that energy creates one ionisation, so 280 J makes on the order of 10²⁰ ionisations, placed at random — including in DNA, where a double-strand break in a stem cell is not repairable by averaging.
  4. (c) X-rays: H = 1 × 4 = 4 Sv. The same absorbed dose from α particles: H = 20 × 4 = 80 Sv, because an α deposits its energy over ~40 μm instead of centimetres, so the ionisations are packed densely enough along one track to make clustered, unrepairable damage.

Three applications worth understanding, not memorising

External-beam radiotherapy. A linac accelerates electrons to 4–20 MeV and slams them into a tungsten target, producing high-energy bremsstrahlung. Two features of MV photons matter clinically. First, they are penetrating enough to reach deep tumours without depositing most of their dose in the skin: the secondary electrons they liberate travel forward before depositing energy, so the dose maximum lies 1–3 cm below the surface ("skin sparing"). Second, the dose is delivered in fractions, typically 2 Gy per day to a total of 60–70 Gy, because normal tissue repairs sublethal damage between fractions better than tumour tissue does. Beams are aimed from many angles so that the tumour receives the sum while each entry path receives a fraction — the same superposition logic as any other field problem.

Positron emission tomography. PET requires a β⁺ emitter specifically, and the reason is geometric. A positron travels about a millimetre in tissue, thermalises, meets an electron and annihilates. Momentum and energy conservation then force the products to be two photons of 511 keV each, emitted almost exactly 180° apart — 511 keV because that is mec², and back-to-back because the pair was essentially at rest. Detect both in coincidence and you know the annihilation happened somewhere on the straight line joining the two detectors, with no collimator needed. That is why PET is roughly two orders of magnitude more sensitive than SPECT, where a single γ leaves in an unknown direction and a lead collimator must throw away almost every photon to recover it. ¹⁸F (T½ = 110 min) in fluorodeoxyglucose is the workhorse: long enough to synthesise, deliver and image; short enough that the patient is not a source the next day.

Technetium-99m. About four in five diagnostic nuclear-medicine procedures use it, and every property that makes it ideal is a physics property.

  • Pure γ, 140 keV. ⁹⁹ᵐTc is a metastable isomer: it decays to ⁹⁹Tc by emitting a single γ, with no α and essentially no β. Particulate emissions would deposit dose in the patient without ever leaving the body to form an image, so their absence is a direct dose saving.
  • 140 keV is the right energy. Low enough to be efficiently absorbed by a thin NaI(Tl) crystal and collimated by lead; high enough to escape the body without excessive attenuation. At 30 keV it would never get out; at 1 MeV it would pass straight through the detector.
  • T½ = 6.0 h. Long enough to prepare the kit, inject, wait for biodistribution and image; short enough that the activity has fallen by ~99% within 40 hours. Compare worked example 8: after 24 h only 1/16 remains.
  • Generator-produced. ⁹⁹Mo (T½ = 66 h) decays to ⁹⁹ᵐTc and is eluted on demand, so a hospital with no cyclotron still has fresh tracer every morning. Long parent, short daughter — the standard trick.

Exam point · the two half-lives that actually matter

The physical half-life Tp is the nuclear one. The biological half-life Tb is how fast the body excretes the tracer. What determines patient dose is the effective half-life, and the rates add: 1/Teff = 1/Tp + 1/Tb, so Teff is always shorter than either. It is the same reciprocal-addition structure as resistors in parallel or lenses in contact — worth recognising as a pattern rather than a fifth formula.

Trap · becquerel, gray and sievert are not the same statement

A becquerel describes the source (decays per second) and says nothing about who is standing nearby. A gray describes the energy delivered to tissue. A sievert describes the biological significance of that energy. A 700 MBq injection does not tell you the dose without knowing the emitted energy, the biodistribution and the effective half-life. And because Gy and Sv share the unit J·kg⁻¹, a bare "4 J·kg⁻¹" is ambiguous — always name the quantity.
