Classical and Chromosomal Genetics

Mendel's achievement was not that he found two laws. It was that he found the right units. Once you accept that inheritance is carried by discrete particles that come in pairs, separate cleanly, and are handed on intact, everything on this page becomes arithmetic performed on a physical object — a chromosome moving in meiosis. That one commitment explains the 3 : 1 and the 9 : 3 : 3 : 1; it explains why those ratios break in exactly the ways they do; it explains why two genes on one chromosome refuse to assort independently, and how that refusal can be turned into a map; and it explains why a piece of chromosome moved, doubled, flipped or lost produces a clinical syndrome. Nothing here should be memorised as a ratio. Every ratio on this page is derivable, and the paper rewards derivation over recall.

§ 01

What Mendel's two laws actually claim

The ideaBefore Mendel, people assumed inheritance worked like mixing paint: a tall parent and a short parent should give a medium child, and the tallness would be diluted away for good. Mendel showed that the thing being inherited is not a shade of paint but a token. A parent carries two tokens for each character, hands on exactly one of them, and the token that seems to vanish in one generation comes back unchanged in the next. His two laws say nothing more than this: how the two tokens for one character behave, and how tokens for different characters behave with respect to each other. Keeping those two questions apart is worth more marks than anything else on this page.

Definition & key facts

Law of segregation（分離律）
The two alleles of a gene carried by a diploid individual separate during gamete formation, so each gamete receives exactly one of them, and each with equal probability. This is a statement about one gene.
segregation = one gene · independent assortment = two or more genes the distinction almost every conceptual item in this block is really testing
Full definition, symbols and units
Law of independent assortment（自由組合律）
The alleles of different genes segregate independently of one another, so knowing which allele of gene A a gamete received tells you nothing about which allele of gene B it received. Holds for genes on different chromosomes, or far enough apart on the same chromosome.
Allele
One of the alternative sequence forms of a gene at a given locus. A diploid individual carries exactly two (one per homologue); a population may carry many.
Homozygous / heterozygous / hemizygous
Two identical alleles / two different alleles / only one copy present at all — a male's X-linked genes, or a locus lying opposite a deletion.
Genotype / phenotype
The alleles present / the observable character. The mapping between them is what §§ 04–06 is about, and it is many-to-one and environment-dependent.
True-breeding (pure line)
Homozygous at the loci under study, so selfing reproduces the phenotype indefinitely. Mendel spent two years establishing pure lines before the experiment proper — which is largely why it worked.

Segregation is an event you can point at

The law of segregation is not an abstraction; it is a description of anaphase I. A heterozygote's two alleles sit at the same locus on two homologous chromosomes. Those homologues pair at prophase I, line up as a bivalent on the metaphase plate, and are pulled to opposite poles at anaphase I. One allele goes each way. Nothing chooses; nothing is destroyed; the two products are equally frequent because the spindle has no preference. That is the whole of the first law.

Independent assortment is a description of metaphase I. Each bivalent lines up with one homologue facing one pole and one facing the other, and which faces which is settled separately for every bivalent. Two bivalents therefore give two distinguishable alignments — the two panels in the figure below — and 2² = 4 gamete types in equal numbers; twenty-three bivalents give 223 = 8 388 608 gamete types. (Keep the two counts apart: n bivalents give 2n−1 distinguishable alignments and 2n gamete combinations, because reversing every bivalent at once only relabels the poles.) This is why the second law applies only to genes on different bivalents. Two genes riding the same chromosome are not two bivalents; they are one, and one bivalent cannot orient independently of itself. That is linkage (§ 10), and it violates the second law only — the first holds regardless.

"The law of independent assortment states that alleles separate independently during gamete formation"

Some version of this sentence appears in a great many revision summaries, and it is a merger of the two laws that destroys both. Alleles separating is segregation, and they do not do it "independently" — they do it obligatorily, one to each pole. Independent assortment is about pairs of genes: the assortment of one pair is independent of the assortment of another. Train yourself to check the arithmetic that follows. If the answer is a 3 : 1 or a 1 : 1, the question is about segregation. If it is a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 or a 1 : 1 : 1 : 1, independent assortment is doing work too.

What the laws do not say

Three limitations are examined more often than the laws themselves.

Dominance is not a law. It is often listed as Mendel's "third law", but it is not a general truth about anything — it is an observation that in the seven characters Mendel happened to choose, one allele's phenotype was fully expressed in the heterozygote. Change the character you score and the same alleles may show incomplete dominance or codominance (§ 04). Segregation and independent assortment, by contrast, are statements about the transmission of alleles and are indifferent to what those alleles do.

Independent assortment has a physical precondition. It requires the two genes to be on different chromosomes, or far enough apart on one chromosome that a crossover almost always separates them. Mendel's seven characters sit on only four of the pea's seven chromosome pairs, so several of his pairs are physically linked in principle. They happen to lie far apart on those chromosomes, and he did not publish dihybrid data for all twenty-one possible pairings — so the second law survived a piece of luck that a systematic search would have exposed.

The laws describe nuclear genes only. Mitochondrial and chloroplast genes travel with the cytoplasm, overwhelmingly from the mother, and obey neither law (§ 06). Imprinted genes obey both laws in their transmission, but violate the tacit assumption that a phenotype depends only on which alleles you have and not on which parent they came from.

The one-line answers examiners want

"Which law is violated by linkage?" → independent assortment only. "Which law explains a 1 : 1 test-cross ratio?" → segregation. "What is the cytological basis of independent assortment?" → the random orientation of each bivalent at metaphase I — not anaphase I, which is the basis of segregation, and not crossing over, which is a separate source of variation. "Which law would still hold for two genes 5 cM apart?" → segregation; independent assortment would fail.

Go deeper · a heterozygous locus does not always segregate at anaphase I

Textbook diagrams show the two alleles of a heterozygote parting at anaphase I, and for a locus close to its centromere that is what happens. But sister chromatids stop being identical the moment a crossover occurs between them. If a crossover falls between the centromere and the locus, each homologue arrives at anaphase I carrying one chromatid with A and one with a. The two alleles are still together after the first division, and they separate only at anaphase II, when sister chromatids part.

This is second-division segregation, and it is not a curiosity: it is the basis of ordered-tetrad analysis in Neurospora, where the eight ascospores lie in the order they were made and the pattern (4 : 4 for first-division segregation, versus 2 : 2 : 2 : 2 or 2 : 4 : 2 for second) reports directly on whether a crossover fell between the gene and its centromere. Centromere-to-gene distance = ½ × (fraction of second-division asci) × 100 cM — the ½ arising because only two of the four chromatids take part in each crossover, which is exactly the reasoning that caps recombination frequency at 50 % in § 10.

The exam-relevant residue: the law of segregation guarantees each gamete ends up with exactly one allele. It does not guarantee that the separation happened at the first division.

§ 02

Monohybrid, dihybrid and the test cross

The ideaThree crosses do almost all the work in this subject, and each answers a different question. Crossing two hybrids tells you the rules of the game — you watch the hidden variant come back, and you can count how often. Crossing an unknown against a pure recessive tells you what the unknown is carrying, because a recessive partner cannot hide anything. And crossing two hybrids for two characters at once tells you whether the two characters travel together or independently. Get into the habit of asking, of any cross in a question, which of those three jobs it is doing.

Definition & key facts

Test cross（試交）
A cross of an individual showing the dominant phenotype to a homozygous recessive. Because the recessive parent contributes only recessive alleles, the offspring phenotypes read out the unknown parent's gametes directly.
dihybrid F₂ = (3 : 1) × (3 : 1) = 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 a dihybrid ratio is the product of two monohybrid ratios — never a fact to memorise
Full definition, symbols and units
Backcross
A cross of an F1 to either parental line. A backcross of an F1 to the recessive parental line is also a test cross; a backcross to the dominant parent is not, and is uninformative about genotype. Examiners use these two words as a deliberate pair.
Reciprocal cross
The same cross with the sexes of the two parents swapped. If the two directions give different results, the gene is sex-linked, imprinted, or cytoplasmic — the single most useful diagnostic cross in classical genetics.
Monohybrid / dihybrid cross
A cross tracking one / two gene pairs. "Dihybrid" describes an individual heterozygous at two loci (AaBb), not merely a cross that mentions two genes.
Forked-line (branch) method
Solving a multi-gene cross as a series of independent monohybrid crosses whose outcomes are then multiplied. Linear in the number of genes, where a Punnett square is exponential.

Why 3 : 1, and why it is a phenotype ratio

Two heterozygotes each make ½ A and ½ a gametes. Fertilisation is random with respect to genotype, so zygote frequencies are the products: ¼ AA, ¼ Aa, ¼ aA, ¼ aa — that is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. If A is completely dominant, the first three classes are indistinguishable and you count 3 : 1. The genotypic ratio was never 3 : 1 and never changes; only the resolution of your assay changes. Everything in §§ 04–05 is a consequence of taking that sentence seriously: an underlying 1 : 2 : 1 or 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, observed through a lossy phenotypic filter.

Mendel's own counts show how ragged real ratios are even with thousands of plants: 5474 round : 1850 wrinkled is 2.96 : 1; 705 purple : 224 white is 3.15 : 1; 787 tall : 277 dwarf is 2.84 : 1. Pooled across all seven characters he had 14 949 dominant : 5010 recessive, or 2.98 : 1. A ratio is an expectation, not a promise, and § 03 supplies the tool for deciding when a deviation is real.

The test cross, and its statistical small print

An organism with the dominant phenotype is either AA or Aa and you cannot tell by looking. Cross it to aa. If it is AA, every offspring is Aa and shows the dominant phenotype. If it is Aa, half the offspring are aa and show the recessive phenotype. One recessive offspring settles the matter instantly.

An absence of recessive offspring settles nothing with certainty, and that is the part that gets tested. If the parent is Aa, the chance a given offspring fails to reveal it is ½, so the chance that n offspring all fail is (½)n. Five offspring leave a 1/32 ≈ 3 % chance of being fooled; ten leave 1/1024 ≈ 0.1 %. In a plant or a fly that is cheap. In cattle, or in a human family, it is why the test cross has been replaced by molecular genotyping.

"A test cross determines whether an organism is homozygous or heterozygous"

True as far as it goes, and it conceals two things examiners exploit. First, a test cross is informative only for an individual showing the dominant phenotype — a recessive individual's genotype is already known, so test-crossing it is pointless. Second, the evidence is asymmetric: a single recessive offspring proves heterozygosity, whereas no number of dominant offspring can ever prove homozygosity — it can only make it probable. Any option that says a test cross "confirms that the parent is homozygous" is overstating what the cross can deliver.

The dihybrid F2, taken apart

Cross true-breeding AABB to true-breeding aabb; the F1 is AaBb and uniform. Self it. If the two genes assort independently, then the phenotype at locus A and the phenotype at locus B are independent events, each following its own 3 : 1. Multiply them:

Worked example 1 — a trihybrid cross without a Punnett square

Three unlinked genes, complete dominance at each. Cross AaBbCc × AaBbCc. (a) What fraction of the offspring show all three dominant phenotypes? (b) What fraction is A_ bb C_? (c) What fraction has the exact genotype AABbcc? (d) How many genotypes and phenotypes appear? (e) Now cross AaBbCc × AabbCc: what fraction is aabbcc?

  1. Set up one locus at a time. For each locus in an Aa × Aa cross: P(dominant phenotype) = 3/4, P(recessive phenotype) = 1/4, P(AA) = 1/4, P(Aa) = 1/2, P(aa) = 1/4. The loci are unlinked, so the product rule applies.
  2. (a) All three dominant. 3/4 × 3/4 × 3/4 = 27/64 ≈ 42.2 %.
  3. (b) A_ bb C_. 3/4 × 1/4 × 3/4 = 9/64 ≈ 14.1 %. This is one of the three "9/64" classes in the 27 : 9 : 9 : 9 : 3 : 3 : 3 : 1 expansion — writing that expansion out is precisely the multiplication you just did, done eight times.
  4. (c) The exact genotype AABbcc. Switch to genotype probabilities: 1/4 × 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/32. This is the step people fumble — the question asks for a genotype, so 3/4 must not appear anywhere in the working.
  5. (d) Counts. Genotypes = 3ⁿ = 3³ = 27; phenotypes = 2ⁿ = 2³ = 8. A Punnett square would have had 4³ = 64 cells; the forked-line calculation took three lines.
  6. (e) AaBbCc × AabbCc → aabbcc. Treat the loci separately, because the cross is not the same at each. Locus A: Aa × Aa → P(aa) = 1/4. Locus B: Bb × bb is a test cross → P(bb) = 1/2. Locus C: Cc × Cc → P(cc) = 1/4. Product: 1/4 × 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/32.
  7. Sanity check. The answers to (c) and (e) are numerically equal for completely different reasons. If you cannot say why, redo them — that is the check that you multiplied the right things.
(a) 27/64 · (b) 9/64 · (c) 1/32 · (d) 27 genotypes, 8 phenotypes · (e) 1/32

Where the marks actually are

Two-mark items in this block almost never ask you to draw a sixteen-cell square; they ask for one class out of a three- or four-gene cross, which a square cannot deliver in a minute. Read the question for whether it wants a genotype fraction or a phenotype fraction, split it into independent single-locus factors, and multiply. The other reliable item is the inverse: given an observed ratio, name the parental genotypes. A 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 means a dihybrid test cross; a 3 : 1 in one character together with a 1 : 1 in another means one locus was heterozygous in both parents while the other was a test cross.

§ 03

Probability instead of Punnett squares

The ideaA Punnett square is a picture of a probability calculation, and pictures stop being useful once there are more than two genes. The whole of Mendelian arithmetic runs on three moves: when two things must both happen, multiply; when either of two things will do, add; and when you have already been told something, throw away the outcomes the information rules out and re-scale what is left. That third move — updating on what you already know — is what separates a candidate who answers 2/3 from one who answers 1/2, and it is worth drilling until it is automatic.

Definition & key facts

Product rule and sum rule
For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Almost every error in genetics arithmetic is a product rule applied to events that are not independent, or a sum rule applied to events that overlap.
AND → multiply · OR → add · "given that" → re-scale three moves; every problem in this section is a sequence of them
Full definition, symbols and units
Conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B) — "the probability of A given that B is known to be true". In genetics B is usually a phenotype you have already observed, and conditioning on it deletes the genotypes incompatible with it.
Binomial distribution
For n independent trials each with success probability p, the probability of exactly k successes is C(n,k) pk(1 − p)n−k, where C(n,k) = n!/[k!(n − k)!] counts the orders in which those k successes could occur.
Chi-square goodness of fit
A test of whether observed counts deviate from an expected ratio by more than sampling error would explain. Computed on counts, never on percentages. It can fail to reject a hypothesis; it can never confirm one.
Degrees of freedom
Number of phenotypic classes − 1, minus one further for each parameter estimated from the same data. A 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 test has df = 3; a 3 : 1 test has df = 1.
P(exactly k of n) = C(n,k) pᵏ (1 − p)n−kfamily-composition problems; p is the per-child risk

Conditioning: why the answer is 2/3 and not 1/2

From Aa × Aa the offspring are 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. Pick one at random and you have a 1/2 chance of Aa. But suppose you look at it first and see the dominant phenotype. The aa class is gone. The remaining sample space is 1 AA : 2 Aa, and the probability that your individual is heterozygous is 2/3. Nothing about the individual changed; what changed is what you know about it.

This is not a trick. It is the reason test crosses exist, the reason an unaffected sibling of a recessively affected child carries a 2/3 rather than a 1/2 carrier risk, and the starting point of every counselling calculation in § 09. Whenever a stem says "an individual showing the dominant phenotype", "a phenotypically normal sibling", or "a plant that is not wrinkled", it is asking you to condition.

"Each child of two carriers has a 1 in 4 risk, so in a family of four exactly one will be affected"

The per-child risk is genuinely 1/4 and genuinely independent of what came before — gametes have no memory, and three affected children do not protect the fourth. But 1/4 is an expectation, not a quota. In a four-child family the probability of exactly one affected child is C(4,1)(1/4)(3/4)³ = 27/64 ≈ 42 %; the probability of none is (3/4)⁴ = 81/256 ≈ 32 %; the probability of two or more is about 26 %. The "expected" family is not even a majority outcome. Ratios describe large samples; families are small ones.

Worked example 2 — a family of five, four questions

Both members of a couple are heterozygous carriers of an autosomal recessive condition (Aa × Aa), so each child carries an independent 1/4 risk. They plan five children. (a) What is the probability that exactly two are affected? (b) That at least one is affected? (c) That the first three are unaffected and the fourth affected? (d) Their first child is unaffected — what is the probability that this child is a carrier?

  1. (a) Exactly two of five. Binomial with n = 5, k = 2, p = 1/4: C(5,2) × (1/4)² × (3/4)³ = 10 × 1/16 × 27/64 = 270/1024 = 135/512 ≈ 26.4 %. The factor 10 counts the birth orders in which two of five could be affected; omitting it is the classic error and costs a factor of ten here.
  2. (b) At least one of five. Take the complement, always. 1 − (3/4)⁵ = 1 − 243/1024 = 781/1024 ≈ 76.3 %. Adding "exactly one" through "exactly five" gives the same number and takes five times as long.
  3. (c) A specified order. The order is fixed, so no binomial coefficient appears: (3/4)³ × (1/4) = 27/256 ≈ 10.5 %. Compare with "exactly one affected among the first four", which is 4 × 27/256 = 27/64 ≈ 42 % — four times larger, because four orders qualify.
  4. (d) Conditioning again. The child is known to be unaffected, so the sample space is 1 AA : 2 Aa and P(carrier) = 2/3 ≈ 66.7 %. Note this is higher than the unconditional 1/2: observing "unaffected" is evidence for heterozygosity, because among the unaffected, heterozygotes outnumber AA homozygotes two to one.
  5. Read the shape of the question. "Exactly k" → binomial coefficient. "At least one" → one minus the complement. "In this order" → straight product. "Given that…" → re-scale. Nothing else is needed.

Exam technique · classify the probability stem before you compute anything

  1. Look for one of exactly four shapes, and stop reading once you have it — "exactly k of n" → binomial, so a C(n,k) must appear in your working. "At least one" → one minus the complement, never a sum of cases. "In this order", "the first three unaffected and the fourth affected" → a straight product with no coefficient. "Given that…", "an individual showing the dominant phenotype", "a phenotypically normal sibling", "a plant that is not wrinkled" → conditioning: delete the class the observation rules out and re-scale what is left. There is no fifth shape anywhere in this block.
  2. Then say out loud whether the answer is a genotype or a phenotype fraction — 3/4 and 1/4 are phenotype numbers; 1/4 : 1/2 : 1/4 are genotype numbers. If the stem names an exact genotype (AABbcc), a 3/4 must not appear anywhere in the line. The distractors on this topic are overwhelmingly the correct arithmetic performed on the wrong one of the two.
  3. Recognise the planted trio on any conditioning item — the paper offers the unconditioned answer (1/2) and the quantity you conditioned on (3/4) beside the true 2/3. Spotting the trio removes two of five options in about five seconds, and under the biology half's −0.25 marking a guess among the remaining three already carries a positive expected value of +0.17 marks. Never leave such an item blank.
  4. Two free sanity checks — "at least one" must land above the single-child risk and below 1, and "exactly k" must always be smaller than the matching "at least one" for any k ≥ 1, because it is a subset of it. An option violating either is crossed off without arithmetic.
  5. Where the time goes — a conditioning item is a ten-second answer; take it immediately. A five-child binomial is about thirty seconds. A four-class χ² is the one item in this section worth the full 66 seconds, and even that has a shortcut: expected counts for 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 are N/16 multiplied by 9, 3, 3, 1, so N = 640 gives 360 : 120 : 120 : 40 before you write anything down.

Chi-square: deciding whether a deviation matters

Real counts never land exactly on a ratio, so you need a rule for when to abandon the hypothesis. The chi-square statistic measures the total squared discrepancy, each class weighted by how large a discrepancy that class should have produced by chance alone. Small χ² means the data sit comfortably with the ratio; large χ² means the ratio is in trouble. The conventional threshold is the value exceeded by chance only 5 % of the time when the hypothesis is true.

Worked example 3 — testing a dihybrid F₂ against 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

640 F2 plants are scored as 342 tall-purple, 122 tall-white, 128 dwarf-purple, 48 dwarf-white. Are the two genes assorting independently with complete dominance?

  1. State the null hypothesis. Two independently assorting genes, complete dominance at both, so the expected ratio is 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Note what is not being tested: nothing here can distinguish true independent assortment from very loose linkage.
  2. Expected counts. 640/16 = 40 per sixteenth, so 360 : 120 : 120 : 40. Convert the ratio to counts before subtracting — χ² on percentages is meaningless, because percentages throw away the sample size that gives the test its power.
  3. Term by term. (342 − 360)²/360 = 324/360 = 0.900; (122 − 120)²/120 = 4/120 = 0.033; (128 − 120)²/120 = 64/120 = 0.533; (48 − 40)²/40 = 64/40 = 1.600.
  4. Sum. χ² = 0.900 + 0.033 + 0.533 + 1.600 = 3.07.
  5. Degrees of freedom and decision. Four classes → df = 3; critical value 7.81. Since 3.07 < 7.81, do not reject: the data are consistent with 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
  6. Say it correctly. "Consistent with", not "proves". Notice too where the χ² came from — the smallest class contributed 1.60 of the 3.07, because the same absolute deviation is far more surprising in a small expected class. That is why the rarest class is the informative one, a point that returns with double crossovers in § 10.
  7. Contrast. Had the counts been 390 : 100 : 100 : 50 — the same N, but with both parental classes (tall-purple and dwarf-white) inflated at the expense of both recombinant classes — χ² would be 11.7 against the same critical 7.81, and the hypothesis would be rejected. That symmetrical excess of the two parental combinations, not a bulge in the largest class alone, is what linkage looks like in a χ² table.
χ² = 3.07, df = 3, critical 7.81 → do not reject 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Three things the paper checks about χ²

That you used counts, not percentages. That df is classes − 1 — four phenotypic classes give 3, not 4, because the total is fixed by the sample so the last class is not free. And that you phrased the conclusion as a failure to reject rather than as proof. A fourth, rarer item: χ² is a test of fit, not a measurement — it tells you whether linkage is detectable, but the map distance itself comes from counting recombinants (§ 10), never from χ².

Fisher's uncomfortable observation

R. A. Fisher noted in 1936 that Mendel's published counts fit his ratios too well — χ² values collectively far smaller than sampling error should allow. Mendel's dihybrid F2 of 556 seeds gives χ² = 0.47 against 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 where the average expected value is 3. The likeliest explanations are unconscious selection when scoring ambiguous seeds, and stopping a count once the ratio looked right — a useful reminder that a suspiciously good fit is itself a finding.

§ 04

Dominance, multiple alleles and lethal alleles

The idea"Dominant" sounds like a property an allele owns — as if one version of a gene were stronger than the other and pushed it aside. It is not. Dominance is a statement about what you can see in an individual carrying both versions, and that depends entirely on which feature you decided to look at and how closely you looked. The same pair of alleles can look completely dominant when you inspect the whole organism, exactly intermediate when you measure the amount of a pigment, and equal partners when you run the two proteins on a gel. Once that clicks, incomplete dominance and codominance stop being two extra categories to memorise and become two levels of magnification.

Definition & key facts

Complete dominance
The heterozygote is phenotypically indistinguishable from the homozygous dominant, for the character being scored. F2 = 3 : 1 phenotypes over a 1 : 2 : 1 genotypic ratio.
dominance is a property of a phenotype, not of an allele change the character you score and the same allele pair changes category
Full definition, symbols and units
Incomplete (partial) dominance
The heterozygote is intermediate. F2 = 1 : 2 : 1 for phenotypes as well as genotypes, because every genotype is now visible. Usually reflects a dosage effect: one functional copy makes roughly half the product.
Codominance
Both allele products are made and are separately detectable in the heterozygote — not blended, not averaged. AB blood group; MN blood group; HbA + HbS on electrophoresis.
Overdominance (heterozygote advantage)
The heterozygote lies outside the range of both homozygotes in fitness. HbA/HbS in malarial regions is the standard case, and it is the reason a deleterious allele persists at high frequency.
Multiple alleles
A gene with more than two alternative forms in the population. Any one diploid individual still carries exactly two. ABO, HLA, coat colour in rabbits (C > cch > ch > c).
Lethal allele
An allele that kills homozygotes (recessive lethal) or all carriers (dominant lethal) before they can be scored. A recessive lethal removes one F2 class and turns 3 : 1 into 2 : 1.

The three relationships, and how to tell them apart in one line

Cross a red snapdragon to a white one and the F1 is pink. Self the pink and you recover 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Two things are worth noticing. First, the alleles did not blend — if they had, pink × pink could never regenerate pure red, and the whole particulate theory would collapse. Incomplete dominance is what blending inheritance looked like from the outside, and Mendel's F2 is what refutes it. Second, the phenotypic ratio and the genotypic ratio have become the same thing, because each genotype is now separately visible. Any F2 reported as 1 : 2 : 1 in phenotype is telling you the heterozygote is distinguishable.

"Codominance and incomplete dominance both give a heterozygote that is a mixture, so they are essentially the same"

They give the same ratios and mean opposite things. In incomplete dominance there is one product present at an intermediate level, and the phenotype lies on a line between the two homozygotes — a pink flower has less pigment, not two pigments. In codominance there are two products, both fully expressed, and the phenotype is not intermediate at all — a person of group AB does not have "half-strength" antigen, they have full A antigen and full B antigen on the same red cell. Test by asking whether the heterozygote's phenotype could be produced by turning a dial (incomplete) or only by adding a second thing (codominant).

Sickle-cell: one allele pair, three answers, all correct

This is the example that makes the point unanswerable, and it is examined precisely because it defeats memorisation. Take a person heterozygous for the β-globin sickle allele, HbA/HbS.

  • Score protein on an electrophoresis gel — you see two bands, HbA and HbS, both present and both functional. That is codominance.
  • Score the behaviour of the red cells at low oxygen tension — the heterozygote's cells sickle, but only under more extreme hypoxia than a homozygote's. That is incomplete dominance.
  • Score "does this person have sickle-cell disease?" — no. The heterozygote is clinically well. Against that phenotype, HbS is recessive.
  • Score survival in a malarial region — the heterozygote out-survives both homozygotes. That is overdominance, and it is why the allele reaches frequencies above 10 % in parts of Africa and the Mediterranean rather than being eliminated.

Nothing about the alleles changed between those four sentences. Only the phenotype under examination changed. An option that says "HbS is a codominant allele" full stop is incomplete; an option that says "the sickle allele is codominant at the molecular level and recessive with respect to the disease" is the sentence the examiner is looking for.

Multiple alleles and the ABO system

A gene may have many alternative sequences in a population — HLA-B has thousands — but a diploid individual has two chromosome copies and therefore exactly two alleles. "This individual has multiple alleles" is always wrong; "this locus is multiallelic in the population" is right.

ABO earns its exam prominence because it packs two different dominance relationships into one locus. The gene (on 9q34) encodes a glycosyltransferase acting on a pre-existing sugar chain, the H antigen. The IA allele's enzyme adds N-acetylgalactosamine; the IB allele's enzyme, differing by four amino acids, adds galactose instead. The i allele carries a single-base deletion that shifts the reading frame and yields no functional enzyme at all. That is the entire explanation of the dominance pattern, and it is worth stating because it converts a memorised hierarchy into a mechanism: IA and IB are codominant because both enzymes work and both sugars get added to different chains on the same cell; both are dominant to i because i does nothing, and doing nothing cannot compete with doing something.

Worked example 4 — the blood group that cannot happen

(a) A group A man and a group B woman have four children of groups A, B, AB and O. What are the parents' genotypes, and what is the expected ratio? (b) A woman who types as group O has a child of group AB. Her partner is group A. Rather than dismissing the case, explain it genetically. (c) What is the probability that the couple in (a) have three children in a row who are not group O?

  1. (a) Read the O child backwards. A group O child is ii, so each parent contributed an i. The father is therefore IAi and the mother IBi.
  2. Expected ratio. IAi × IBi gives IAIB : IAi : IBi : ii = 1 AB : 1 A : 1 B : 1 O — the only cross in human genetics that produces all four ABO groups, and a favourite stem.
  3. (b) Test the impossibility properly. An AB child needs IA from one parent and IB from the other. The father, being group A, can supply IA. So the mother must have supplied IB while typing as O.
  4. Resolve it. She is genotypically IBi but hh at FUT1 — the Bombay phenotype. With no H antigen there is no substrate for her B transferase, so her cells carry no B and she types as O.
  5. Check the child. The child received IB and h from the mother, and IA plus a functional H from the father. Being Hh, the child makes H antigen, both transferases have substrate, and the child is group AB. No mutation and no non-paternity is required.
  6. (c) Three non-O children. Per child, P(not O) = 3/4, and conceptions are independent: (3/4)³ = 27/64 ≈ 42.2 %. Note this is the same arithmetic as the monohybrid case, because a 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 collapsed into "O versus not-O" is a 3 : 1.

Rh, and why it behaves nothing like ABO

Both are red-cell antigen systems, and the paper likes to contrast them. The ABO antibodies are naturally occurring IgM — they arise in infancy against gut bacterial antigens without any transfusion, and IgM does not cross the placenta. Anti-D is an immune IgG, made only after an Rh-negative person is exposed to D antigen, and IgG does cross the placenta. That single immunoglobulin-class difference is why ABO incompatibility between mother and fetus is common but usually mild, while Rh incompatibility is the classic cause of severe haemolytic disease of the newborn — and why it typically spares the first Rh-positive pregnancy, in which the mother is only being sensitised. Anti-D immunoglobulin given at about 28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery works by clearing fetal red cells before the maternal immune system can respond to them.

Why ABO incompatibility is usually mild and Rh incompatibility is not

Anti-D is IgG — made only after an Rh-negative person meets D antigen, and IgG crosses freely, which is why Rh disease is severe, why it typically spares the first Rh-positive pregnancy (that pregnancy is the sensitising event), and why anti-D immunoglobulin is given to clear fetal cells before the mother can respond. The G-for-Goes hook is the standard one and circulates widely in immunology material, so you will meet it again. It fixes only which class crosses. The rest of the contrast needs no hook because it follows: an antibody that appears without deliberate exposure is a primary, unswitched response and therefore IgM, while one that requires exposure has been class-switched and is therefore IgG.

Lethal alleles: the missing quarter

Cross two yellow mice and you never obtain a true-breeding yellow line. The offspring are always about 2 yellow : 1 agouti, and the litters are about a quarter smaller than usual. The explanation is that the AY allele kills homozygotes in utero. The underlying zygotic ratio is the ordinary 1 : 2 : 1; one class simply never appears to be counted, so the survivors show 2 : 1.

The conceptual payload is the sentence you should be able to produce on demand: AY is dominant with respect to coat colour and recessive with respect to lethality. One copy is enough to make a mouse yellow; two copies are needed to kill it. If dominance were a property of the allele, that sentence would be a contradiction. It is not, because dominance is defined per phenotype.

The same logic runs in humans. Achondroplasia (a gain-of-function mutation in FGFR3) is autosomal dominant; two achondroplastic parents produce ¼ homozygous offspring, who die in the neonatal period of a severe skeletal dysplasia with respiratory failure, so their surviving children are 2 achondroplastic : 1 average-statured. The Manx cat's tailless allele behaves the same way. And Huntington disease illustrates the opposite corner: a dominant lethal allele persists only because its effects appear after reproductive age — a dominant lethal expressed in childhood is eliminated in one generation and can exist only as a new mutation.

Go deeper · why wild-type alleles are usually dominant

It is not a coincidence that in the great majority of Mendelian disorders the disease allele is recessive and the functional allele dominant. Most mutations destroy function, and most enzymes are present in large excess: a heterozygote makes about 50 % of the normal amount of product, and for a typical metabolic step that is comfortably enough to keep flux normal. This is haplosufficiency, and it makes the loss-of-function allele recessive by default. Sewall Wright's argument, from metabolic flux, is that the relationship between enzyme concentration and pathway flux is a saturating curve — so halving the enzyme moves you very little along a plateau.

Dominant disease alleles therefore need a special reason, and there are three, each with a diagnostic signature:

  • Haploinsufficiency — 50 % really is not enough, because the gene product is needed in near-stoichiometric amounts or the pathway is not saturated. Familial hypercholesterolaemia (LDL receptor) is the textbook case, and it is also, tellingly, the textbook case of incomplete dominance in humans: heterozygotes have roughly double-normal LDL cholesterol, homozygotes about five times normal with childhood coronary disease. Same allele, dominant against "has hypercholesterolaemia", incompletely dominant against "how much cholesterol".
  • Dominant negative — the mutant polypeptide is made and poisons the complex it joins. Common in multimeric proteins: collagen in osteogenesis imperfecta, where one bad chain in a triple helix ruins the whole molecule, so a heterozygote can be worse off than someone with a null allele.
  • Gain of function — the mutant protein does something new or does the old thing constitutively. Achondroplasia's FGFR3 is constitutively active; most oncogenes work this way.

The exam payoff: if a stem tells you a disorder is dominant and asks why, "the mutant allele is stronger" is never the answer. The answer is one of haploinsufficiency, dominant negative, or gain of function — and the stem usually contains the clue (a structural protein points to dominant negative, a receptor or a transcription factor to haploinsufficiency, a constitutively active kinase to gain of function).

§ 05

Epistasis and the modified ratios

The ideaSuppose two different genes both have to work for a flower to end up purple — one builds a colourless intermediate, the next converts it to pigment. Break either one and the flower is white, and you cannot tell from looking which one was broken. The underlying genetics has not changed at all: two genes, each still segregating and assorting exactly as before, still giving sixteen equally likely combinations in the F2. What has changed is that several of those sixteen now look identical. Every "strange" ratio in this section — 9 : 3 : 4, 12 : 3 : 1, 9 : 7, 15 : 1 — is the ordinary 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 with some classes collapsed together. They all still add up to sixteen, and that is how you recognise one.

Definition & key facts

Epistasis（上位性）
The genotype at one locus masks or modifies the phenotypic expression of the genotype at a different locus. The masking locus is epistatic; the masked one is hypostatic. Contrast with dominance, which is a relationship between the two alleles at the same locus.
every modified dihybrid ratio still sums to 16 classes are merged, never created — count the sixteenths and you have the gene action

The homozygous recessive genotype at the epistatic locus does the masking (aa hides everything at B). F2 = 9 : 3 : 4.

A single dominant allele at the epistatic locus does the masking (A_ hides everything at B). F2 = 12 : 3 : 1.

"Epistasis is when one allele completely masks the effect of another allele"

That sentence defines dominance, and it is offered as a distractor constantly because it is a plausible paraphrase of what epistasis looks like. The distinguishing word is locus. Dominance is a relationship within one gene — between the two alleles an individual carries at one place on a chromosome pair. Epistasis is a relationship between two different genes, which may be on different chromosomes entirely. Two further traps ride on the same confusion: epistasis has nothing to do with the two genes being linked (they are usually not), and the epistatic locus need not be "upstream" in any simple sense, although in a biosynthetic pathway it usually is.

Worked example 5 — reading gene action out of an F₂ ratio

Two true-breeding white-flowered lines of a plant are crossed. The F1 is entirely purple. Selfing the F1 gives 528 purple and 408 white out of 936 plants. (a) How many genes are involved and how do they act? (b) Write the parental and F1 genotypes. (c) Test the fit. (d) What would a test cross of the F1 give?

  1. (a) Convert to sixteenths. 936/16 = 58.5 per sixteenth. 528/58.5 = 9.03 and 408/58.5 = 6.97 — a 9 : 7. A denominator of 16 means two genes; the merge of 3 + 3 + 1 into a single class means duplicate recessive epistasis: purple requires a dominant allele at both loci, so two enzymes act in sequence in one pigment pathway and blocking either one gives white.
  2. (b) Genotypes. The two white parents must be CCpp and ccPP — each homozygous recessive at a different locus, which is why each is white and why crossing them restores colour. The F1 is CcPp and purple.
  3. The deep point. The F1 being purple when both parents are white is complementation: two recessive mutations restore wild type when combined, which proves they lie in different genes. Had the two white lines carried mutations in the same gene, the F1 would have been white and no F2 would have been worth counting.
  4. (c) χ² against 9 : 7. Expected 936 × 9/16 = 526.5 purple and 936 × 7/16 = 409.5 white. (528 − 526.5)²/526.5 = 2.25/526.5 = 0.0043; (408 − 409.5)²/409.5 = 2.25/409.5 = 0.0055; total χ² = 0.0098 with df = 1 against a critical 3.84 — an excellent fit.
  5. (d) Test cross of the F1. CcPp × ccpp gives CcPp : Ccpp : ccPp : ccpp in equal numbers, and only the first is purple: 1 purple : 3 white. Notice the ratio changed but the biology did not — always confirm which cross the question is describing before you reach for a remembered ratio.

The two questions this topic actually asks

Either ratio → mechanism ("an F2 of 9 : 3 : 4 indicates…") or mechanism → ratio ("in Labradors, ee prevents pigment deposition; what F2 is expected from BbEe × BbEe?"). Both are done by writing the four base classes 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb and deciding which look alike. A third, subtler item asks you to identify which locus is epistatic: the merged classes always share a genotype at the epistatic locus. In 9 : 3 : 4 the class of four is everything that is aa, so the first locus is epistatic; in 12 : 3 : 1 the class of twelve is everything that is A_, so again the first locus is epistatic — but now through its dominant allele.

Exam technique · an F₂ ratio to a mechanism in twenty seconds

  1. Add up the parts of the ratio first — 9 + 3 + 4, 12 + 3 + 1, 9 + 7, 15 + 1, 13 + 3 and 9 + 6 + 1 all come to 16, and so does the underlying 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Two genes give sixteen equally likely gamete pairings, and epistasis merges classes without ever creating one, so the total cannot move. Any option explaining such a ratio by "three genes", "multiple alleles at a single locus" or "the two genes are linked" is dead before you think about the biology — that is one or two options gone in five seconds.
  2. Convert the counts to sixteenths once, then work in that unit — divide N by 16 and stop. For 936 plants, one sixteenth is 58.5; 528 and 408 are 9.03 and 6.97 of them, so the ratio is 9 : 7. Never try to simplify the raw counts against each other.
  3. Read the distinctive number off the hook — 4 on the floor (recessive), a dozen dominates (12), 7 needs both, 15 needs either, 6 stacks, 13 blocks.
  4. If the item asks which locus is epistatic, look at what the merged class has in common — the pooled class always shares one genotype, and that genotype is the answer. In 9 : 3 : 4 the four is every aa individual, so the first locus is epistatic through its recessive; in 12 : 3 : 1 the twelve is every A_ individual, so the first locus is epistatic through its dominant.
  5. Check which cross you were actually handed — every ratio above is an F2 self-cross ratio. A test cross of the same F1 divides into quarters, not sixteenths: the 9 : 7 plant test-crosses to 1 purple : 3 white. An option quoting an F2 ratio at a test-cross stem is a true textbook sentence attached to the wrong question, and that is precisely how these distractors are built.
  6. The reverse direction runs the same machinery — for "mechanism → ratio", write out 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb, apply the stem's rule to each of the four, and pool the ones that end up looking alike. Fifteen seconds, and it never requires a remembered ratio at all.

Two genes in a pathway, in one sentence

Recessive epistasis (9 : 3 : 4) is a broken early step: nothing downstream can be seen, so the later gene's variation is invisible. Duplicate recessive epistasis (9 : 7) is two steps in the same pathway, either of which can break it. Duplicate dominant (15 : 1) is two genes doing the same job, so you need to break both. If you can sketch the pathway, you can predict the ratio.

§ 06

Pleiotropy, polygenes, penetrance and expressivity

The ideaMendel chose seven characters where one gene produced one clean, all-or-nothing difference. Almost nothing in medicine looks like that. A single gene usually affects many things at once, because its product is used in many places. A single visible character usually depends on many genes at once, because it is the end of a long chain of processes. And even when a gene and a character do correspond one to one, some people carrying the allele show nothing at all and others show it mildly. None of this contradicts Mendel — the alleles are still transmitted exactly as § 01 says. What changes is the relationship between the alleles you inherit and the person you can see.

Definition & key facts

Penetrance vs expressivity
Penetrance is the proportion of individuals with a given genotype who show any of the associated phenotype — an all-or-nothing count across a population. Expressivity is the degree to which the phenotype is expressed among those who show it — a measure of variability within the affected.
penetrance = whether at all · expressivity = how severely a population count versus an individual measurement — the pairing is examined every year

Polygenic inheritance

Multifactorial

The proportion of phenotypic variance in a particular population in a particular environment attributable to genetic variance. It is a property of a population, never of an individual, and it says nothing about how modifiable a trait is.

Pleiotropy: one gene, a syndrome

The word "syndrome" is usually a clinical announcement of pleiotropy — a collection of apparently unrelated findings that turn out to share one molecular cause. In Marfan syndrome the mutated gene is FBN1, encoding fibrillin-1, a microfibril protein of the extracellular matrix. Fibrillin is used in the suspensory ligament of the lens, in the aortic media, in the periosteum and in skin, so the patient has lens dislocation, aortic root dilatation and dissection risk, long limbs and arachnodactyly, and joint hypermobility — one gene, four organ systems. Phenylketonuria is the same story in metabolism: loss of phenylalanine hydroxylase raises phenylalanine, which damages the developing brain, starves tyrosine-dependent melanin synthesis (hence the fair hair and skin) and produces the characteristic musty odour of phenylacetate. Nothing in either case requires more than one gene.

Two examination consequences follow. First, a stem describing several unrelated-looking features and asking for the genetic term wants pleiotropy, not "polygenic" — the words are similar and the concepts are opposite. Second, pleiotropy is the reason a single-gene disorder can look inherited in a complicated way: which features a patient shows depends on modifier genes and environment, which is variable expressivity layered on top of pleiotropy.

Polygenic inheritance: Mendel scaled up

Nilsson-Ehle's wheat crosses in 1909 settled the long argument between the Mendelians and the biometricians by showing that continuous variation is what a handful of Mendelian genes looks like when their effects simply add. Take three loci, each with an "additive" allele contributing one unit of red pigment and a neutral allele contributing none. Cross the two extreme homozygotes; the F1 is intermediate and uniform. Self it, and the F2 distributes across 2n + 1 = 7 classes in the proportions 1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1 out of 64 — the binomial coefficients, because what you are counting is how many of the six alleles in a zygote happen to be additive.

"Skin colour is controlled by three or four genes" and "heritability of height is 80 %, so height is 80 % genetic in any individual"

The first is a teaching simplification that has been overtaken: pigmentation involves dozens of loci, of which MC1R, SLC24A5, OCA2 and TYR are only the largest-effect ones. Use the three-gene model to understand the shape of the inheritance, not as a fact. The second is a harder error, and it appears verbatim in textbook prose. Heritability partitions variance in a population, not the causes in a person. Height in a well-fed population has high h² precisely because everyone is well fed, so the remaining differences are mostly genetic; introduce childhood malnutrition and h² falls, without any allele changing. A trait can have h² of 1.0 and still be perfectly correctable — dietary phenylalanine restriction in PKU is the standard demonstration.

Penetrance and expressivity

These two words are constantly offered as each other's distractor, and the distinction is genuinely simple once stated correctly. Penetrance is a proportion of people: of 100 individuals carrying a mutant RB1 allele, about 90 develop retinoblastoma, so penetrance is 90 % and the condition is incompletely penetrant. Expressivity is a description of severity among those affected: two people with the same polydactyly allele may have a fully formed extra digit on each hand and foot, or one small skin tag, so expressivity is variable. A condition can be fully penetrant with wildly variable expressivity (neurofibromatosis type 1), or incompletely penetrant with uniform severity when it does appear.

Incomplete penetrance is the reason a dominant condition can appear to skip a generation, and it is the single most common way a real pedigree breaks the tidy rules of § 09. When a pedigree shows an unaffected individual with an affected parent and an affected child, incomplete penetrance of a dominant is a better explanation than an autosomal recessive with improbable carrier matings.

Penetrance or expressivity — which of the two a stem is asking for

Penetrance counts People · Expressivity measures Extent

Each word shares its initial with what it is about, and the two verbs finish the job: penetrance is a head count, expressivity is a measurement. Penetrance — how many? A count needs a numerator and a denominator, so it is a proportion of everyone carrying the genotype and comes out as a percentage: of 240 people carrying the allele, 192 show signs, so 192/240 = 80 %. Expressivity — how much? A measurement needs a scale, so it grades severity among the affected only, and can never be one number for a population. The "how many versus how much" split is the standard English framing and you will meet it again; the People / Extent pairing is my own tightening of it. It tells you which word to choose and nothing more — the worked cases (a mutant RB1 allele ≈ 90 % penetrant; NF1 fully penetrant with wildly variable expressivity) have to be attached to the words separately.

Worked example 6 — counting genes, and putting a number on penetrance

(a) Two true-breeding varieties of a plant differ in fruit weight. Their F1 is uniform and intermediate. Among 1024 F2 plants, 4 are as light as the light grandparent and 3 as heavy as the heavy one. How many additive gene pairs are involved, and how many phenotypic classes are expected? (b) In a family study, 240 people are shown by genotyping to carry a dominant disease allele; 192 have clinical signs. What is the penetrance? (c) Of those 192, some have only café-au-lait spots while others have severe skeletal disease. Which term describes that?

  1. (a) Use the extremes. Each extreme class has expected frequency (1/4)ⁿ. Observed ≈ 3.5/1024, and 1024 = 4⁵, so (1/4)ⁿ ≈ 1/256 to 1/341. With n = 4, (1/4)⁴ = 1/256 → expected 1024/256 = 4 plants in each extreme class. That matches 4 and 3 within sampling error, so n = 4.
  2. Check against n = 5.   3. Classes. 2n + 1 = 2(4) + 1 = 9 phenotypic classes, in the proportions 1 : 8 : 28 : 56 : 70 : 56 : 28 : 8 : 1 out of 256.

Maternal effect versus maternal inheritance — the pair that is designed to be confused

Maternal (cytoplasmic) inheritance means the genes themselves came from the mother, because they are in the mitochondria. Maternal effect means the genes are ordinary nuclear genes, inherited from both parents in the normal Mendelian way, but the phenotype of an individual is dictated by its mother's genotype because her gene products were deposited in the egg. The diagnostic test is to go one generation further: with maternal effect, a Mendelian ratio does appear — it just appears delayed by one generation, in the grandchildren. With mitochondrial inheritance no Mendelian ratio ever appears, in any generation.

Prader-Willi or Angelman — whose copy went missing

Prader-Willi = Paternal loss · Angelman = mAternal loss

Both syndromes come from losing the same stretch of 15q11-q13; only the parental origin differs, and the initial letter of each name carries it. Lose the paternally expressed genes and you get Prader-Willi — neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding, then hyperphagia and obesity. Lose the maternally expressed UBE3A and you get Angelman — severe intellectual disability, absent speech, ataxia, seizures, inappropriate laughter. (Taiwanese material names them 小胖威利症 and 天使症, but sit the hook on the English initials — those are what the paper prints.) The P / A version is the standard one and is what you will meet in textbooks and 補習班 material. Go deeper · imprinting, uniparental disomy and the Prader-Willi / Angelman pair

About 100 human genes are imprinted: expressed from only one of the two parental alleles, with the choice fixed by methylation marks laid down during gametogenesis. The marks are erased in primordial germ cells and re-established according to the sex of the individual making the gametes, not the parent they came from — which is how the same chromosome can carry a paternal mark in a man and a maternal mark in his daughter.

The 15q11-q13 region is the standard teaching case because two entirely different syndromes come from losing the same piece of chromosome from different parents. Prader-Willi syndrome — neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding, then hyperphagia and obesity, short stature, hypogonadism, mild intellectual disability — results from loss of the paternally expressed genes (chiefly SNRPN and a snoRNA cluster). Angelman syndrome — severe intellectual disability, absent speech, ataxic gait, seizures, inappropriate laughter — results from loss of the maternally expressed UBE3A.

Each syndrome arises by three routes, and the proportions matter clinically. Roughly 70 % are a deletion of the relevant parental chromosome 15; about 25 % of Prader-Willi and 3–5 % of Angelman are uniparental disomy, in which the patient has two structurally normal chromosome 15s but both from the same parent, so the correctly imprinted copy is simply absent; and a small remainder are imprinting-centre defects or, for Angelman, point mutations in UBE3A. Uniparental disomy is worth knowing beyond this pair, because it also converts a heterozygous parent's recessive allele into homozygosity in a child — a route to recessive disease with only one carrier parent.

The mnemonic that survives the exam: Prader-Willi from the Paternal loss; Angelman from the mAternal.

§ 07

Sex determination and sex linkage

The ideaIn us, one chromosome is much smaller than its partner and carries almost nothing except a switch that makes an embryo male. Two consequences follow, and they are separate. First, sex itself is decided by whether that switch is present — which is not how every animal does it, and the differences are exactly what gets tested. Second, and more far-reaching, the genes on the other chromosome of the pair have no partner in a male. A male has one copy where a female has two, so a variant that a female could carry silently is unavoidably expressed in a male. That single asymmetry produces every pattern in this section.

Four ways to build a sex, and why the difference matters

Humans and Drosophila both have XX females and XY males, and it is tempting to conclude they work the same way. They do not, and one comparison exposes it. The number of X chromosomes is irrelevant to the decision. In Drosophila the opposite holds: sex is set by the ratio of X chromosomes to haploid autosome sets. An XXY fly has X : A = 1.0 and is female; an XO fly has X : A = 0.5 and is a sterile male. The fly's Y carries fertility factors but no sex switch at all.

"In mammals the female is the default sex — without a Y, the embryo simply develops as a female"

This sentence, or a close paraphrase, appears in a great many textbooks and it is at best half true. SRY is genuinely the switch, and its absence does mean no testis. But ovarian development is not a passive fallback: it is an active genetic programme driven by WNT4, RSPO1 and FOXL2, which also actively repress the testicular pathway. The decisive evidence is that deleting FOXL2 in an adult mouse ovary causes granulosa cells to transdifferentiate into Sertoli-like cells — the ovary begins turning into a testis in an animal that never had a Y. The two programmes are mutually antagonistic throughout life, not a switch and a default. Reject any option that says the female pathway "requires no genes".

The clinical corollaries of a single switch

Because sex determination in mammals hangs on one small piece of one chromosome, it can come apart in instructive ways, and these appear regularly as stems.

  • 46,XX testicular disorder ("XX male") — an aberrant crossover in male meiosis, just outside the pseudoautosomal boundary, transfers SRY onto the X. The resulting individual has two X chromosomes and a male phenotype.
  • 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome) — a loss-of-function mutation in SRY (or in a downstream gene) in an otherwise XY individual gives a female phenotype with streak gonads.
  • Complete androgen insensitivity — the switch works, but the target does not. A 46,XY individual with a loss-of-function mutation in the X-linked androgen receptor develops testes that make testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone; the Müllerian ducts regress, so there is no uterus, but the tissues cannot respond to androgen and the external phenotype is female. This is the neatest reminder available that a genotype specifies a phenotype only through a working signalling pathway — and it is inherited as an X-linked recessive from the mother.

X-linked inheritance: the whole logic from one asymmetry

A female has two X chromosomes and can be homozygous or heterozygous. A male has one, and is hemizygous: there is no second allele, so whatever is on his single X is expressed. Everything else follows mechanically.

For an X-linked recessive, a male needs one copy to be affected while a female needs two, so if the allele frequency is q, affected males occur at frequency q and affected females at q². For red-green colour blindness with q ≈ 0.08 that is 8 % of males against 0.64 % of females — a 12.5-fold excess — while 2pq = 14.7 % of women are carriers. The rarer the allele, the more extreme the skew: at q = 0.001, males outnumber affected females a thousandfold, which is why haemophilia in a female is a genuine rarity requiring an affected father and a carrier mother.

The transmission pattern is criss-cross. An affected male gives his X to every daughter and his Y to every son, so all his daughters are obligate carriers and none of his sons are affected by his allele. Those carrier daughters then transmit to half their sons. The disorder therefore travels through phenotypically normal women and reappears in the grandsons — which is why the classic pedigree looks as though it skips a generation and moves diagonally.

For an X-linked dominant, invert the same logic. An affected father transmits his single X to every daughter and no son, so all his daughters are affected and none of his sons are — an unmistakable signature. An affected heterozygous mother transmits to half of each sex. Because females have twice the chance of receiving an affected X, roughly twice as many females as males are affected, and some X-linked dominants (Rett syndrome, incontinentia pigmenti) are lethal in hemizygous males, so affected males are essentially absent and the pedigree shows excess miscarriage.

"Genes on the X chromosome are never transmitted from father to son"

Almost right, and the exception is examinable. The X and Y still have to pair in male meiosis, and they do so through the pseudoautosomal regions at their tips — PAR1 on the short arms, PAR2 on the long. Genes in PAR are present on both sex chromosomes, recombine freely between them, are present in two copies in both sexes, and therefore can pass father to son. SHOX is the clinically important one: its haploinsufficiency explains the short stature of Turner syndrome and causes Léri-Weill dyschondrosteosis, which is inherited in a plainly autosomal-looking way despite sitting on the X. The safe formulation is "genes in the differential (non-recombining) region of the X show no male-to-male transmission".

Sex-linked, sex-limited, sex-influenced — a three-way distractor set

Sex-linked is about where the gene is: on a sex chromosome. Sex-limited is about where it is expressed: only in one sex, usually because the relevant tissue or hormone exists in only one sex — the genes for milk yield are carried by bulls and transmitted by them, and are simply never expressed in them. Sex-influenced is about how dominance behaves: pattern baldness behaves as a dominant in men and a recessive in women, so a heterozygous man goes bald and a heterozygous woman does not. Both sex-limited and sex-influenced traits are usually autosomal. If an option says "pattern baldness is sex-linked", it is wrong for a reason worth being able to state: the trait is expressed in both sexes and the difference between them is one of dominance, not of gene location. One caveat, beyond the textbook and not what the paper will test: real androgenetic alopecia is polygenic, and its single largest association is in fact at the X-linked androgen-receptor locus. Treat the classical account as the clean model of sex influence, not as the last word on human hair.

Exam technique · karyotype stems, and the only arithmetic this section uses

  1. Mammal named → look only for SRY, and the number of X chromosomes is irrelevant. Fly named → count X : autosome sets. Bird named → the system is ZW, so the mother is the heterogametic parent and decides the offspring's sex. Reading the organism first turns a 60-second item into a 15-second one; the whole comparison table is generated by that one question and needs no mnemonic.
  2. An allele frequency in the stem means an X-linked recessive frequency item — there is nothing else in this section that uses q. Affected males = q; affected females = q²; carrier females = 2pq; and the male : female ratio among the affected is 1/q. At q = 0.08 that is 8 % of males, 0.64 % of females, 14.7 % carriers and a 12.5 : 1 skew. Since q < 1, q² is always the smaller number — any option making affected females commoner than affected males is out on sight.
  3. "Reciprocal cross" in a stem is a flag, not scenery — if the paper bothers to give you a cross and its reciprocal and the two differ, the answer is sex linkage, imprinting or cytoplasmic inheritance, and never a plain autosomal gene. If the two agree, autosomal.
  4. Male-to-male transmission still kills X-linkage — but know the one exception — invoke the pseudoautosomal regions only when the stem names SHOX, mentions the tips of the sex chromosomes, or says the gene recombines between X and Y. Otherwise the plain exclusion stands, and an option offering PAR without any such cue is decoration.
  5. Barr bodies need no hook — the count is n(X) − 1 (§ 08) because the cell keeps exactly one X active per diploid autosome set. Count the X's in the karyotype you were given, subtract one, and reject any option that inactivates the Y or that ties the count to the individual's sex rather than to the X number.
§ 08

X-inactivation and dosage compensation

The ideaA female has two X chromosomes and a male has one, so left alone a female would make twice as much of every X-encoded protein. That would be intolerable, and every animal with sex chromosomes has evolved some fix. The mammalian fix is drastic: early in development each cell picks one of its two X chromosomes, at random, and shuts it down almost completely — and every cell descended from it inherits the same choice. A female is therefore not a uniform organism at all, but a patchwork of two kinds of cell. That patchwork explains the colour of a tortoiseshell cat, why a woman who carries a serious X-linked disease can occasionally be affected by it, and why the fix does not quite work.

Why the tortoiseshell cat is the standard demonstration

The orange locus in cats is X-linked. A heterozygous female carries the orange allele on one X and the non-orange allele on the other. In every melanocyte precursor, one X was silenced at random and the choice was passed to all its descendants, so the coat is a patchwork of orange and black clonal patches. A male has one X, therefore one choice, therefore one colour — which is why virtually all tortoiseshell and calico cats are female, and why a tortoiseshell male is almost always XXY (or a chimaera of two embryos). Note the precision the exam sometimes wants: the orange/black mosaic is X-inactivation, but the white patches of a calico come from a separate autosomal piebald-spotting gene and have nothing to do with the X.

"Dosage compensation makes XXY equivalent to XY and XO equivalent to XX, so those karyotypes should be normal"

This is exactly the inference the naive model licenses, and the fact that it is false is the most instructive thing in this section. About 15 % of human X-linked genes escape inactivation, so the inactive X is not silent — it is quiet. A 45,X individual is therefore genuinely haploid for those escape genes, and a 47,XXY individual genuinely has an extra dose of them. The short stature of Turner syndrome is largely SHOX haploinsufficiency (a pseudoautosomal escape gene), and the tall stature of Klinefelter syndrome is its converse. Any option asserting that X-inactivation "completely equalises" X gene dosage should be rejected.

Consequences a medical candidate should be able to produce

Manifesting heterozygotes. Because the founding cell population is small, the 50 : 50 mix is only an average. A woman carrying a Duchenne muscular dystrophy allele who happens to have inactivated the normal X in most of her muscle precursors can develop weakness or, more commonly, a dilated cardiomyopathy. The same reasoning explains variable severity in female carriers of ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, G6PD deficiency and haemophilia. "A female heterozygous for an X-linked recessive is unaffected" is a good first approximation and a poor absolute.

Mosaicism is visible. In X-linked ocular albinism, a carrier's retina shows a mottled fundus; in hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia, carriers show patches of skin lacking sweat glands in a streaked pattern. Those streaks — Blaschko's lines — are literally clonal maps.

Not all mammals choose at random. In marsupials, and in the extraembryonic tissues of rodents, it is always the paternal X that is inactivated — imprinted X-inactivation. Random choice is a mammalian refinement, not a law of nature.

Three solutions to one problem

Dosage compensation is often taught as though X-inactivation were the mechanism, and a comparative item catches that. Mammals silence one X in females. Drosophila does the opposite — the MSL complex doubles transcription from the single male X, so compensation happens in the male. C. elegans halves transcription from both X chromosomes in XX hermaphrodites. Birds compensate only partially, which is itself the point. The common thread is the problem, not the answer: equalise X-linked output between the sexes without changing autosomal output.

Go deeper · how a cell counts its X chromosomes and makes the choice stick

Three separate problems have to be solved, and the XIST locus solves all three. The cell must count how many X chromosomes it has relative to its autosomes; it must choose which one to keep active; and it must maintain that choice through hundreds of subsequent divisions.

Counting and choosing happen at the X-inactivation centre, where XIST is transcribed on the future inactive X while its antisense partner TSIX is transcribed on the future active X and represses XIST in cis. Homologous X chromosomes transiently pair at this locus, and the asymmetry that results makes one X commit to XIST expression and the other to TSIX. The rule the cell converges on is to keep exactly one X active per diploid autosome set — which is precisely why the Barr-body count is n(X) − 1 and not something more complicated.

XIST RNA then spreads along the chromosome that made it, which is why the silencing is strictly cis-acting, and recruits Polycomb repressive complexes (depositing H3K27me3), the histone variant macroH2A, and DNA methyltransferases that methylate CpG islands at promoters. Each mark is individually reversible; together they are self-reinforcing, and once DNA methylation is in place the inactive state is heritable through S phase without XIST being needed any longer. That layering is the answer to the maintenance problem, and it is why the choice made on about day 16 is still visible in a coat pattern or a fundus decades later.

One postscript with clinical weight: the inactive X is reactivated in the female germ line before meiosis, because an oocyte needs both copies. Reactivation also occurs in reprogramming to induced pluripotency, which is why iPS cell lines from female donors are notoriously variable in X status.

The ideaYou cannot do a controlled cross on a human family, so the only data you get is a family tree with some members marked. "The trait appears in every generation, so it must be dominant"

Appearance in every generation is evidence for dominance, not proof, and there are two common ways it misleads. A common recessive can appear in consecutive generations whenever affected individuals marry carriers — a phenomenon called pseudodominance, seen with deafness alleles in communities where affected people frequently marry one another, and also whenever a recessive allele sits opposite a deletion (§ 11). Conversely, a genuinely dominant condition can skip a generation through incomplete penetrance (§ 06), or appear out of nowhere as a new mutation — 80 % or more of achondroplasia cases have unaffected parents. The exclusions in the table above survive both problems; the "looks like" descriptions do not.

Exam technique · the pedigree exclusion ladder, run in this order

  1. Scan for an affected father with an affected son. One instance kills both X-linked modes at once, and it also kills mitochondrial inheritance, since an affected father transmits to none of his children. Five seconds, three options potentially gone. Always do this scan first.
  2. Scan for two unaffected parents with an affected child. That kills every dominant mode. The stem will normally say "assume complete penetrance and no new mutation" precisely so this exclusion is clean; where it does not say so, the escape routes are incomplete penetrance (§ 06) and a new mutation, and the paper expects you to name them rather than ignore the observation.
  3. Scan for an affected female, then look at her father. If he is unaffected, X-linked recessive is dead — he is hemizygous, so a mutant X would have shown in him.
  4. Scan for an affected father with an unaffected daughter. That kills X-linked dominant, because he gives his only X to every daughter.
  5. Only now reach for the soft evidence, and know it is soft. A male excess supports X-linked recessive; a consanguineous loop supports autosomal recessive and nothing else, so an option offering consanguinity as evidence for a dominant mode is always wrong; and appearance in every generation supports dominance but proves nothing, because pseudodominance and incomplete penetrance both counterfeit it. If two modes are still standing, the item is asking "most likely", not "proved".
  6. Then commit. Even a pedigree that leaves two modes alive will have had two or three of the five options removed by steps 1–4, and under the biology half's −0.25 marking a guess among three carries an expected +0.17 and among two an expected +0.375. A blank scores exactly nothing. There is no pedigree item on this paper worth leaving empty.
Worked example 7 — two recurrence risks, one with Bayes

(a) A man's sister has cystic fibrosis; their parents are unaffected. He plans children with an unrelated woman from a population in which the CF carrier frequency is 1 in 25. What is the probability that their first child has CF? (b) A woman's brother has Duchenne muscular dystrophy (X-linked recessive), and so did her mother's brother. She has already had two unaffected sons. What is the probability that a third son would be affected?

  1. (a) The man's carrier risk. His parents must both be carriers (Aa × Aa) because they produced an affected child. He is unaffected, so conditioning removes the aa quarter and leaves 1 AA : 2 Aa: P(carrier) = 2/3.
  2. The partner and the transmission. Her carrier risk is the population figure, 1/25. If both are carriers, each child has a 1/4 risk. These are independent, so multiply: 2/3 × 1/25 × 1/4 = 2/300 = 1/150 ≈ 0.67 %.
  3. Read what that means. Roughly a 1 in 150 risk, against a population risk of about (1/25)² × 1/4 = 1/2500 — roughly a sixteenfold increase, entirely because of one affected relative. The relevant clinical action is to test him: if he tests negative for CFTR mutations, the 2/3 collapses and the risk becomes negligible.
  4. (b) The woman's prior carrier risk. Her mother has an affected son and an affected brother, so her mother is an obligate carrier. A carrier mother transmits the mutant X to half her daughters, so the prior is P(carrier) = 1/2.
  5. Update on the two unaffected sons. If she is a carrier, the chance that both sons escaped is (1/2)² = 1/4. If she is not a carrier, the chance is 1. Apply Bayes: posterior = (1/2 × 1/4) ÷ [(1/2 × 1/4) + (1/2 × 1)] = (1/8) ÷ (5/8) = 1/5. Two healthy sons have cut her carrier risk from 1/2 to 1/5.
  6. The answer. A third son is affected only if she is a carrier and transmits: 1/5 × 1/2 = 1/10. For a child of unspecified sex, halve again: 1/5 × 1/4 = 1/20.
  7. Why this matters. The naive answer, 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, ignores evidence that is already on the table. Note also the asymmetry: healthy sons are informative, healthy daughters are almost worthless as evidence, because a carrier's daughters are unaffected either way. Had she had three unaffected sons the posterior would fall to 1/9.

Consanguinity, in the form it is asked

First cousins share one pair of grandparents, and the probability that a particular allele in one cousin is also present in the other by descent is F = 1/16. The practical consequence is specific to rare recessives: for a common recessive, the partner's risk of carrying the allele is already high and relatedness adds little, whereas for a rare one, relatedness may be the only realistic way for two copies to meet. This is why consanguinity in a pedigree is strong evidence for autosomal recessive inheritance and none at all for dominant or X-linked inheritance — and why an option offering "consanguinity supports autosomal dominant inheritance" is always wrong.

Three exclusions worth having by heart

An affected son of an affected father kills every X-linked hypothesis. An affected daughter of an unaffected father kills X-linked recessive. An affected child of two unaffected parents kills every dominant hypothesis, unless penetrance is incomplete or the mutation is new.

§ 10

Linkage, recombination and mapping

The ideaTwo genes on the same chromosome are physically tied together, so they tend to be inherited as a package. They are not tied permanently: during meiosis the paired chromosomes swap segments, and a swap that happens to fall between the two genes breaks the package. The further apart the genes, the more room there is for a swap to land in between, so the fraction of offspring in which the package has been broken is a measure of distance. That is the whole of genetic mapping — a ruler built out of how often something goes wrong between two points.

The proportion of progeny carrying a non-parental combination of two markers, counted directly in a test cross. It estimates the probability that an odd number of crossovers fell between the two loci.

Why recombination frequency cannot exceed 50 %

This is the single most misunderstood number in genetics, and the explanation is entirely geometric. At the time crossing over happens, each homologue has already replicated, so the paired structure contains four chromatids. A crossover is an exchange between two of them — one from each homologue — and the other two are untouched. So a single crossover in an interval produces two recombinant chromatids and two parental ones: exactly 50 %.

Follow that through. If two genes are so far apart that a crossover falls between them in every meiosis, the yield is still 50 % recombinants, not 100 %. Adding further crossovers does not help either, because a second crossover between the same two markers puts them back together. The ceiling is therefore 50 %, and it is the same value you would get from genes on different chromosomes. RF = 50 % means "no detectable linkage", which includes both genuinely unlinked genes and genes at opposite ends of one long chromosome. The way you demonstrate that two such genes are on the same chromosome is to link them through intermediate markers, each pair of which is close enough to show RF below 50 %.

The two-point test cross

Bateson and Punnett found in 1905 that two characters in sweet peas did not give the expected 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 in a test cross: the parental combinations were far too common. They called the two arrangements "coupling" and "repulsion" and could not explain them. Morgan's insight in 1911 was that the two genes sit on the same chromosome and that the recombinant classes are produced by physical exchange — and that, because exchange is more likely between distant points, the recombinant frequency measures distance. Sturtevant, then an undergraduate in Morgan's laboratory, took the data home in 1913 and produced the first genetic map.

The procedure is simple and worth stating as an algorithm. Test-cross the double heterozygote to a double recessive. Count the four classes. The two most frequent are parental — and they tell you the arrangement (cis or trans) in the heterozygous parent, which you were never told. The remainder are recombinant. Divide and multiply by 100.

"Linkage means the genes are always inherited together"

Linked genes are inherited together more often than chance, which is not the same as always. Two genes 1 cM apart separate in 1 % of gametes; two genes 40 cM apart separate in 40 % — barely distinguishable from independence in a small experiment. Two further precision points ride on this. First, linkage is a property of a pair of loci, not of an allele: saying "the disease allele is linked to the marker allele" is loose shorthand for a specific haplotype in a specific family. Second, in a population, linked loci drift towards random association over many generations unless something maintains the association — which is why linkage (a property of chromosomes) and linkage disequilibrium (a property of a population's history) are different things.

The three-point cross

Two-point crosses give distances but not order, and they underestimate long intervals. A three-point cross fixes both problems at once by scoring three loci in the same progeny, and it is the calculation this block most reliably asks for. The method has five steps and never varies.

  1. Identify the parental classes — the two most frequent, always reciprocal to one another. These are the chromosomes the heterozygous parent started with.
  2. Identify the double-crossover classes — the two least frequent, because their probability is a product of two small numbers.
  3. Compare a parental class with a double-crossover class. Exactly one marker will have changed. That marker is the middle gene. This works because, as the figure shows, a double crossover swaps only the segment between the two exchange points.
  4. Compute each interval's RF as (single crossovers in that region + all double crossovers) ÷ N. Double crossovers must be added to both intervals: each represents one exchange in each region.
  5. Compute interference. Expected DCO = RF1 × RF2 × N; c.o.c. = observed ÷ expected; I = 1 − c.o.c.

The four ways this calculation is got wrong

Forgetting to add the double crossovers into both intervals — the single commonest error, and it always makes both distances too small. Reporting the two-point RF between the outer markers instead of the sum of the intervals. Confusing c.o.c. with interference: they are complements, and an option offering 0.4 when the answer is 0.6 is testing exactly that. And assuming the gene order given in the question is the map order — it is supplied in alphabetical or arbitrary order precisely so that step 3 has something to do.

Exam technique · running a three-point cross against the clock

  1. If the item only wants the gene order, compute nothing at all. Take the largest class and the smallest class, set them side by side, and name the one marker that differs. Ten seconds. The option saying the order "cannot be determined without calculating the recombination frequencies" is planted exactly for candidates who start with arithmetic — order comes from the identity of the rarest classes, never from a distance.
  2. Total the eight classes before doing anything else. They must sum to the N the stem quotes: 712 + 88 + 188 + 12 = 1000 ✓. A transcription slip caught here costs four seconds; caught after the arithmetic it costs the question.
  3. Rewrite every class in map order, then take each interval as (its own single crossovers + all the doubles) ÷ N. Omitting the doubles is the single commonest error in the topic, and it always makes both distances too small — so if two options are both smaller than your answer by similar amounts, you are looking at that trap laid twice.
  4. Free reconciliation check, and it costs one subtraction. The two interval distances must exceed the outer two-point RF by exactly twice the double-crossover frequency. Here 10.0 + 20.0 = 30.0 against a two-point 27.6 %, and 30.0 − 27.6 = 2.4 = 2 × 12/1000 ✓. If it does not reconcile, you dropped the doubles out of one interval.
  5. Two eliminations that need no work. Any option quoting an RF above 50 % is impossible, because a crossover uses only two of the four chromatids. And if two options are x and 1 − x — 0.60 against 0.40 — that pair is the question: c.o.c. is observed ÷ expected doubles, and interference is the leftover, I = 1 − c.o.c.
  6. Re-read which distance was asked for. "Between the two outside genes" means the sum of the two intervals (30 cM), not the two-point recombinant count (27.6 %). Both numbers will be sitting in the option list, because the paper knows you computed both.
  7. One species check that occasionally decides the whole item. There is no crossing over in male Drosophila, so a mapping cross whose heterozygous parent is male returns parental classes only — which makes an apparently impossible question a one-fact question.

Go deeper · why the genetic map is not a physical map

A map distance in centimorgans is a statement about how often chromosomes break and rejoin between two points, not about how many base pairs lie between them. Averaged over the human genome the two happen to correspond conveniently — a sex-averaged map of roughly 3600 cM over roughly 3100 Mb of sequence, so 1 cM ≈ 1 Mb — but that average conceals large and systematic departures.

  • Recombination is not uniform along a chromosome. It is heavily suppressed near centromeres and in heterochromatin, and concentrated in hotspots a few kilobases wide, largely positioned by the PRDM9 zinc-finger protein. Two markers straddling a hotspot can be 1 cM apart while being 50 kb apart physically.
  • Recombination differs between the sexes. The human female genetic map is about 1.7 times longer than the male map — roughly 4400 cM against 2600 cM — so one physical interval yields different map distances depending on which parent was heterozygous. Sex-averaged maps are a convenience, not a measurement of anything.
  • RF is a saturating function of physical distance. Over short intervals RF ≈ map distance, but as intervals lengthen, double crossovers accumulate and RF flattens towards 0.5. Mapping functions correct for this: Haldane's, m = −½ ln(1 − 2r), assumes crossovers are independent; Kosambi's builds in interference and fits real data better over intermediate distances. Neither is needed for exam arithmetic, but knowing that RF and map distance are different quantities is.

Two experiments are worth being able to name, because they are what turned "the chromosome theory" into a demonstrated fact. In 1931 Creighton and McClintock in maize, and independently Stern in Drosophila, used chromosomes carrying visible cytological markers — a knob at one end, a translocated segment at the other — and showed that genetic recombination between two genes was accompanied by physical exchange of those cytological markers. Genetic recombination and physical crossing over are the same event, observed two different ways.

§ 11

Chromosomal mutations and their syndromes

The ideaSo far every gene has stayed where it belongs and only its sequence has varied. Now the chromosome itself changes: a piece is lost, doubled, turned round, or moved somewhere else. Two quite different things can go wrong as a result. The obvious one is dosage — too little or too much of whatever the moved piece encodes. The subtler one is pairing: homologous chromosomes have to line up gene for gene in meiosis, and a chromosome that no longer matches its partner has to contort itself to do so. The contortions are harmless in themselves, but a crossover inside one of them produces chromosomes that are missing or duplicating whole regions. That is why a person can carry a rearrangement in perfect health and still have repeated miscarriages.

Balanced — no genetic material gained or lost, only rearranged; the carrier is usually phenotypically normal. Unbalanced — material is missing or present in excess; the carrier usually has a phenotype. Inversions and reciprocal translocations are balanced; deletions and duplications are not.

Loss of a chromosome segment, terminal or interstitial. In a heterozygote the intact homologue forms a deletion loop at pairing, and a recessive allele opposite the deletion is expressed — pseudodominance.

Deletions: pseudodominance and contiguous-gene syndromes

A deletion has two consequences worth separating. Genetically, the locus opposite the deleted segment is hemizygous, so a recessive allele there is expressed with nothing to mask it. This is pseudodominance, and it is both a trap in pedigree interpretation — a recessive trait appearing in an apparent heterozygote — and a classical mapping tool: cross a set of deletion strains to a recessive mutant and the deletions that uncover the mutant phenotype localise the gene.

Clinically, deleting a stretch of chromosome removes many neighbouring genes at once, giving a contiguous-gene syndrome whose combination of features has no single-gene explanation. Cri-du-chat syndrome (5p deletion) gives a characteristic high-pitched cat-like cry, microcephaly and intellectual disability; Williams syndrome (7q11.23) removes ELN among some two dozen genes, giving supravalvular aortic stenosis alongside a distinctive cognitive profile; 22q11.2 deletion gives the DiGeorge/velocardiofacial spectrum — thymic and parathyroid hypoplasia, conotruncal cardiac defects, hypocalcaemia. Recognising that these are deletions, not point mutations, is what explains why they are usually de novo and why the phenotype is a syndrome rather than a single defect.

Inversions: what "crossover suppressor" really means

An inversion heterozygote has one normal and one inverted chromosome. To pair gene for gene, one of them must form a loop. Crossing over inside that loop occurs at the normal rate — nothing prevents it — but the products are catastrophic, and which catastrophe depends on whether the centromere is inside the inverted segment.

In a paracentric inversion (centromere outside), a single crossover in the loop produces one dicentric chromatid, which is pulled towards both poles and forms an anaphase bridge that breaks, and one acentric fragment, which has no centromere and is lost. The two non-crossover chromatids are normal. In a pericentric inversion (centromere inside), the crossover products are monocentric and survive the division, but each carries a duplication of one region and a deletion of another, so the resulting zygotes are usually inviable.

"Inversions suppress crossing over"

This is standard textbook phrasing and it is mechanistically false, which makes it an ideal distractor. Crossing over inside the inversion loop happens perfectly normally; what is suppressed is the recovery of recombinant products, because they are dicentric, acentric, or duplicated and deleted, and therefore do not appear among viable offspring. The correct term is crossover suppressor in the operational sense only. The distinction has real consequences: it is why inversions lock groups of alleles together into supergenes that are inherited as a unit — the mimicry supergenes of Heliconius and Papilio, the mating-type systems of the white-throated sparrow — and why geneticists use inversion-bearing balancer chromosomes in Drosophila to maintain lethal mutations as stable stocks. None of that would work if crossovers were genuinely prevented.

Translocations: the healthy carrier problem

A reciprocal translocation carrier has all their genetic material, just distributed differently, and is normally healthy. The difficulty is meiotic. The two normal chromosomes and their two translocated partners must all pair, which they do by forming a cross-shaped quadrivalent. How that quadrivalent segregates decides the outcome: alternate segregation sends the two normal chromosomes to one pole and the two derivatives to the other, giving balanced gametes; adjacent segregation mixes them, giving gametes with a duplication of one region and a deletion of another. Roughly half the gametes are therefore unbalanced, which appears clinically as semisterility, recurrent miscarriage, or a liveborn child with an unbalanced karyotype — which is why couples with recurrent pregnancy loss are karyotyped.

A Robertsonian translocation is the fusion of two acrocentric chromosomes — 13, 14, 15, 21 or 22 — at or near their centromeres, with loss of the tiny short arms, which carry only redundant rRNA gene clusters. The carrier has 45 chromosomes and is phenotypically normal. rob(13;14) is the commonest, occurring in about 1 in 1000 people. A rob(14;21) carrier can produce a gamete containing both the fused chromosome and a free 21, giving a child with translocation Down syndrome who has 46 chromosomes but three doses of 21q. This is the reason Down syndrome is occasionally familial, and it is the answer to the classic stem in which a young mother has a second affected child.

The five acrocentric chromosomes — and why only they fuse

D group 13 · 14 · 15 + G group 21 · 22

Robertsonian translocations are assembled only out of these five, so any stem about a healthy carrier with 45 chromosomes, or about familial Down syndrome, is drawing from this list and no other. The D / G grouping is the standard Denver classification and is the form in which the set normally circulates. There is also a reason the set is exactly this, and the reason is worth more than the letters: these are precisely the chromosomes whose tiny short arms carry nothing but tandem rRNA gene clusters, present in many copies across all five. That is why fusing two of them at the centromere and discarding both short arms costs the carrier nothing, and it is why the carrier of a 45-chromosome karyotype is phenotypically normal. The grouping is standard; the reasoning attached to it is the note's own. Two caveats. The Y is acrocentric too and is classed in group G, but it carries no rRNA cluster and is not part of the Robertsonian set. And the hook gives membership only — which pair is commonest (rob(13;14)) and which is the counselling catastrophe (rob(21;21), where every viable conceptus is affected) has to come from the text below.

Aneuploidy: which division failed, and why it matters

Aneuploidy comes from nondisjunction — a failure of separation — and where it happened is both mechanistically and diagnostically distinguishable. At meiosis I the homologues fail to separate, so the resulting gamete carries both homologues, which are genetically different: at the centromere the gamete is heterozygous. At meiosis II the sister chromatids fail to separate, so the gamete carries two identical copies and is homozygous at the centromere. Using centromere-linked polymorphic markers, this distinction has shown that the great majority of trisomy 21 arises from maternal meiosis I errors, in oocytes that have been arrested in prophase I since before the mother was born — the leading explanation for the maternal age effect, since cohesion between sister chromatids deteriorates over decades of arrest. Nondisjunction in a mitotic division after fertilisation instead produces mosaicism, and mosaic trisomies are typically milder.

The three autosomal trisomies compatible with live birth: +21 Down (≈1/700), +18 Edwards (≈1/6000), +13 Patau (≈1/12 000). This is the hook that circulates in medical revision material, so take it — the name-to-number pairing is otherwise pure arbitrary matching. Then check it against a pattern that costs nothing: D, E, P run in alphabetical order while 21, 18, 13 run downwards, and that same descending order is also the order of decreasing frequency and of decreasing survival — Down into adult life, Edwards mostly under a year, Patau days to weeks. Two independent routes to the same answer is the cheapest insurance available on a recall item. The drinking- and voting-age anchors are American conventions and do not match Taiwan's, so treat the line as an arbitrary rhyme rather than as a fact about ages. It fixes the number and nothing else: the clinical features must be attached separately, and they are not arbitrary either — overlapping fingers and rocker-bottom feet for 18, midline defects and polydactyly for 13. Do not extend the hook to why only these three survive; that is derivable and better held as a reason, namely that 13, 18 and 21 are the three most gene-poor autosomes, so a 1.5× dosage error across them unbalances the least.

Why sex-chromosome aneuploidy is survivable and autosomal aneuploidy mostly is not

Two reasons, and both are worth being able to state. Extra X chromosomes are largely neutralised by X-inactivation (§ 08) — 47,XXX has two Barr bodies and only one active X — and the Y carries very few genes, so an extra one adds little. Autosomes have neither escape route: every gene on a trisomic autosome is present at 1.5 times normal dosage, and the only autosomal trisomies routinely compatible with live birth are 13, 18 and 21 — the three most gene-poor autosomes, 21 being also the smallest of all (13 and 18 are middling in size but unusually sparse in genes, which is the property that matters). Note also that the surviving X-aneuploidies still have phenotypes, precisely because X-inactivation is incomplete (about 15 % of X genes escape) — which is the connection the paper likes to test.

Worked example 9 — reasoning from a karyotype to a recurrence risk

A 26-year-old woman has a son with Down syndrome. His karyotype is reported as 46,XY,der(14;21),+21. (a) Explain the karyotype: why 46 chromosomes and yet three doses of 21q? (b) The mother is karyotyped and found to be 45,XX,der(14;21). What is her chromosome count, and why is she healthy? (c) What gamete types can she make, and what does each produce? (d) Why is the empirical recurrence risk important here in a way it would not be for standard trisomy 21?

  1. (a) Count the arms, not the chromosomes. The child has 46 counted chromosomes, but one of them is a fusion carrying the long arms of both 14 and 21. He therefore has two free 21s plus the 21q on the derivative — three copies of 21q — which is what causes the phenotype. Down syndrome is a dosage disorder of 21q, not of "a chromosome".
  2. (b) The mother. She has 45 chromosomes: one normal 14, one normal 21, and the der(14;21) fusion. Her 21q and 14q dosage is exactly normal — the only material lost is the acrocentric short arms, which contain redundant rRNA gene clusters present in many copies elsewhere. She is a balanced carrier and phenotypically normal.
  3. (c) Her gametes. The trivalent can segregate six ways, giving: normal (14 + 21); balanced carrier (der only); unbalanced with extra 21q (der + 21) → translocation Down syndrome; monosomy 21 (14 only) → lethal; trisomy 14 (der + 14) → lethal; monosomy 14 (21 only) → lethal. Each name here is the conceptus, the bracket its gamete. Only three of the six give a viable conceptus, and one of those three is affected.
  4. The naive prediction, and why it is wrong. Three viable outcomes, one affected, would suggest a 1/3 recurrence risk. The observed figure is far lower — about 10–15 % if the mother is the carrier and 1–2 % if the father is — because unbalanced conceptuses are preferentially lost before birth, and because unbalanced sperm compete poorly. This is why empirical risks are quoted in counselling rather than the segregation arithmetic.
  5. (d) The counselling point. For standard free trisomy 21 in a young mother the recurrence risk is about 1 %, and the parents' karyotypes are normal. For a translocation carrier the risk is an order of magnitude higher, it does not fall with maternal age, and — crucially — it applies to the carrier's relatives too, so the family should be offered karyotyping. And a rob(21;21) carrier is the extreme case: every gamete carries either both 21q arms or neither, so every viable conceptus has Down syndrome.

"Polyploidy is simply a more extreme aneuploidy"

They are different in kind, and the difference is the reason one is a cornerstone of plant breeding and the other is lethal. Aneuploidy unbalances gene dosage — some chromosomes at 1.5× or 0.5×, the rest at 1× — and it is imbalance, not absolute quantity, that organisms cannot tolerate. Polyploidy scales everything equally, so dosage relationships are preserved. That is why polyploidy is lethal in mammals largely for reasons of imprinting and sex determination rather than dosage, while in plants it is common, productive and a major route to speciation: bread wheat is an allohexaploid (2n = 6x = 42) combining three ancestral genomes. The exam-relevant corollary is the odd-number rule — a triploid cannot pair its chromosomes three at a time in meiosis, so it is sterile, which is exactly why seedless watermelons and bananas are triploid.

Exam technique · rearrangement stems, and counting the right thing

  1. Count arms, not chromosomes. Down syndrome is three doses of 21q, not "an extra chromosome". A Robertsonian carrier has 45 counted chromosomes and entirely normal dosage; a translocation Down child has 46 counted chromosomes and abnormal dosage. Any option that reasons from the chromosome count straight to the phenotype has been built to fail on exactly this, and a stem that quotes an unusual count is telling you it wants that distinction.
  2. "Healthy, but reproductive trouble" is a fixed cue. Recurrent miscarriage, semisterility, or a second affected child born to a young mother means a balanced carrier — reciprocal translocation, Robertsonian, or a large inversion. The explanation is always the same five words: balanced carrier, unbalanced gametes. Any option invoking a new point mutation or advanced maternal age for that vignette is answering a different stem.
  3. For an inversion, locate the centromere and the products fall out. Centromere outside the loop (paracentric, 臂內倒位) means the two crossover chromatids differ in how many centromeres they got — one dicentric bridge, one acentric fragment. Centromere inside the loop (pericentric, 臂間倒位) means each crossover chromatid keeps exactly one centromere, so they survive the division but carry a duplication and a deletion. There is nothing to memorise beyond where the centromere sits; the Chinese names say it outright.
  4. Two textbook sentences that are always wrong as options. "Inversions prevent or suppress crossing over" — crossovers occur normally inside the loop; what fails is recovery of the products. And "polyploidy is simply an extreme aneuploidy" — aneuploidy unbalances dosage, polyploidy scales it evenly. Both are lifted nearly verbatim from real textbooks, which is precisely why they are offered, and both are free eliminations once you have seen them named.
  5. Meiosis I or meiosis II needs no hook either. Ask what separates at each division. MI separates homologues, so an MI error delivers two different homologues into one gamete — heterozygous at the centromere. MII separates sisters, so an MII error delivers two identical chromatids — homozygous at the centromere. 47,XYY therefore has to be paternal MII, because no other event can put two Y chromosomes in one gamete.
  6. Where the time goes. A bare "which syndrome has which feature" item is a ten-second answer or a skip and return; do not spend a minute mining the table. The item worth the full 66 seconds is the segregation question — list the gamete types, then remember that the figure quoted in counselling is the empirical risk (≈10–15 % for a maternal Robertsonian carrier), well below what the segregation arithmetic alone would predict, because unbalanced conceptuses are lost before birth.

Which rearrangement, from the meiotic figure alone

A loop in one homologue means a deletion or a duplication. A loop involving both, with one chromosome turned back on itself, means an inversion. A cross with four arms means a reciprocal translocation. A trivalent means a Robertsonian. The figure tells you the rearrangement, and the rearrangement tells you which gametes are unbalanced.
