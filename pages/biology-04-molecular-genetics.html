Molecular Genetics and the Control of Gene Expression

Biochemistry note 05 asked how a gene is copied, transcribed and translated. This note asks the question the biology half of the paper actually sets: which genes, in which cell, at what level, and who decides. One idea organises all of it. A bacterium's genes are open by default and regulation is mostly about switching things off when they are not worth making; a eukaryote's genes are wound shut by default and regulation is mostly about prising particular ones open and keeping them that way through every cell division. Everything else on this page — operons, histone marks, enhancers, splicing, microRNA, imprinting, even why a one-base deletion is catastrophic and a three-base deletion is survivable — falls out of that difference in default state and of one further principle: the later in the pathway you intervene, the faster you can respond and the more you have already wasted.

§ 01

Why cells regulate, and where they can

The ideaEvery cell in your body carries the same instruction manual, and the only thing that makes a liver cell a liver cell is which pages it is reading. So the interesting question is never "what genes does it have" but "what is it currently making, and how much". A cell can interfere at any of about eight successive stages between the DNA and a working protein, and the choice of stage is a bargain: block things early and you waste nothing but you are slow, block things late and you can respond in seconds but you have already paid for everything you are about to throw away.

Definition & organising model

Differential gene expression
The production of different sets of proteins from an identical genome. It is the basis of cell differentiation in multicellular organisms — the genome itself is not edited, and the proof is that a differentiated nucleus can be reprogrammed backwards: to pluripotency by four transcription factors (induced pluripotent stem cells), and all the way to totipotency by transfer into an enucleated oocyte (§ 08).
[protein]ss ∝ (transcription rate) × (mRNA t½) × (translation rate) × (protein t½) four multiplicative controls — a cell can change the answer by touching any one of them
Full definition, symbols and units
Constitutive expression
Transcription that proceeds at a roughly fixed rate regardless of signals — housekeeping genes, and, importantly, the low-level "leaky" transcription of a repressed operon. No promoter is ever completely silent, and § 03 shows why the lac system would not work if it were.
Steady state
The level at which synthesis and degradation balance, so the concentration stops changing. Almost every statement about "how much of a protein there is" is a steady-state statement, and it depends on the ratio of two rates, never on synthesis alone.
Response time
How long after a signal the new steady state is approached. For a molecule removed by first-order decay this is set by its half-life alone — not by how fast it is made.

The control points, in order

The list below is not something to memorise as a list; it is a map you use to place any regulatory mechanism you are shown. Almost every exam item on eukaryotic regulation is really the question "at which of these levels does the described mechanism act?", and the distractors are the neighbouring levels.

Exam technique · answering "at which level does this act?" in ten seconds

  1. Look for a timescale before you look at the options — the stem usually hands you one. "Within seconds of adding the hormone" or "before any new transcription can occur" can only be level 7 or 8, acting on protein that already exists. "Over several hours", "persists through cell division", "in the daughter cells" is level 1. A time in the stem is the examiner telling you the answer.
  2. Otherwise match the named molecule to its level — one word is enough. HAT, HDAC, DNMT, HP1, SWI/SNF, any H3K… mark → 1. Enhancer, silencer, Mediator, activator, TFIID, response element → 2. SR protein, hnRNP, alternative poly(A) site, editing → 3. Nuclear pore, 3′ UTR zip-code → 4. AU-rich element, deadenylation, microRNA, nonsense-mediated decay, an iron-response element in the 3′ UTR → 5. eIF2α, 4E-BP, mTOR, IRES, an iron-response element in the 5′ UTR → 6. Zymogen cleavage, phosphorylation, allosteric ligand → 7. Ubiquitin, K48, 26S proteasome, APC/C, MDM2, VHL → 8. The iron pair is the one entry where you must read the position as well as the name (§ 07): 5′ blocks the scanning ribosome, 3′ shields the transcript.
  3. Expect the distractors to be the neighbouring levels, not distant ones. If you have decided "mRNA stability", the two options you must beat are "RNA processing" and "translation initiation" — so ask which molecule was actually named, not which sounds plausible.
  4. Kill on compartment. Anything requiring transcription and translation at once (attenuation, coupled degradation) is bacterial only. Anything keyed to exon junctions, splicing before export, or histones is eukaryotic only. That filter alone usually removes two options.
  5. Then stop. This shape of question is worth ten seconds, not sixty-six. Bank the time for the partial-diploid and half-life items, which genuinely need the full minute.

The comparison the paper actually asks for

Prokaryote versus eukaryote is the single most productive contrast in this topic, and it is almost never asked as "list the differences". It is asked as a scenario: a mechanism is described, and you must say whether it could occur in a bacterium. Three answers settle most of them. Is there a nucleus? If the mechanism needs transcription and translation to happen at the same time and place (attenuation, coupled degradation of the transcript by a translating ribosome), it is bacterial only. If it needs them separated (splicing before export, nonsense-mediated decay keyed to exon junctions), it is eukaryotic only. Is the mRNA polycistronic? Operons give one mRNA for several genes — bacteria only (with the striking exception of the trans-spliced operons of nematodes). Is the DNA in nucleosomes? If the mechanism is about histones or about chromatin state being copied through S phase, it is eukaryotic.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"Genes are switched on and off." Almost never a switch; almost always a rheostat. A repressed lac operon still makes a few molecules of β-galactosidase per cell per generation, and it has to, because the inducer is a product of that very enzyme. "Differentiated cells have lost the genes they no longer need." False except in the immune system, where V(D)J recombination genuinely deletes DNA, and in a few other specialised cases; the general rule is that the genome is intact, which is exactly what cloning by nuclear transfer demonstrated. "Regulation happens at transcription." Transcription initiation is the commonest single control point, not the only one — and it is the slowest, so any response measured in seconds is not transcriptional.

One last point of framing, because it decides how you read every subsequent section. A bacterium's promoter is naked DNA: RNA polymerase can reach it, so the resting state of a gene is somewhat on, and the regulatory problem is how to hold it shut. A eukaryote's promoter is wrapped round histones inside folded chromatin: polymerase cannot reach it, so the resting state is off, and the regulatory problem is how to open it. That is why bacterial regulation is dominated by repressors and eukaryotic regulation by activators, and why a eukaryotic gene typically needs several activators at once while a bacterial operon needs only one repressor removed.

§ 02

The operon and the four control logics

The ideaA bacterium that needs five enzymes to digest one sugar would be foolish to switch them on one at a time. So it keeps the genes for all five in a row, puts a single switch in front of them, and copies the whole row into one long message. The switch itself can work in either of two ways — a protein that sits on the DNA and blocks the way, or a protein that sits on the DNA and helps the machinery arrive — and the small molecule that the cell is sensing can either turn the system on or turn it off. Those two choices are independent, which gives four possible logics, and telling them apart is most of what this part of the paper is about.

Definition & key facts

Operon
A set of adjacent structural genes transcribed from a single promoter into a single polycistronic mRNA, together with the cis-acting sites — promoter and operator — that control that promoter. The genes it contains almost always encode enzymes of one pathway, so they are needed together and are therefore regulated together.
operon = promoter + operator + gene₁ gene₂ gene₃ … → one mRNA the regulatory gene that encodes the repressor is not part of the operon
Full definition, symbols and units
Operator
A short cis-acting DNA sequence, usually overlapping or immediately downstream of the promoter, to which a repressor binds. Being a piece of DNA, it can only affect genes on the same molecule of DNA — this is the whole basis of the partial-diploid analysis in § 03.
Regulatory gene
A gene encoding a diffusible regulatory protein. It has its own promoter, is usually expressed constitutively at low level, and need not be anywhere near the operon it controls. Because its product diffuses, it acts in trans.
Inducer / corepressor
The small molecule (effector) that the regulatory protein senses. An inducer turns expression on; a corepressor turns it off. Which one it is tells you what kind of pathway you are looking at.
Regulon
A set of operons at scattered locations that share a regulatory protein and are therefore controlled together — for example the roughly 200 promoters controlled by cAMP–CAP. Eukaryotes have no operons, but they do have the equivalent of regulons, which is how they achieve coordinate control (§ 06).

Deriving the logic instead of memorising it

Ask what the small molecule is, and the rest follows without any memory work at all.

If the operon encodes enzymes that break something down — a catabolic pathway — then the molecule the cell senses is the substrate. Enzymes for digesting lactose are worth making only when lactose is there. So the resting state is off, and the substrate turns it on: the operon is inducible, and the effector is an inducer.

If the operon encodes enzymes that build something — an anabolic pathway — then the molecule the cell senses is the end product. Enzymes for making tryptophan are worth making only when tryptophan is scarce. So the resting state is on, and the product turns it off: the operon is repressible, and the effector is a corepressor. This is simply end-product feedback moved from the enzyme to the gene — and note that the cell usually does both, inhibiting the first enzyme allosterically within milliseconds and shutting the operon within minutes.

There is no 口訣 for the four logics, and you should not want one. A mnemonic here would be a second thing to remember, and it would decay into exactly the confusion the warning below describes. Two questions, asked in this order, regenerate the whole table in about four seconds. Does this pathway take something apart or put something together? Apart → the sensed molecule is the substrate → inducible. Together → the sensed molecule is the end product → repressible. Does the regulatory protein, when it is sitting on the DNA, raise transcription or lower it? Lower → negative. Raise → positive. Practise saying both answers out loud for lac ("catabolic, so inducible; LacI lowers transcription, so negative — and CAP raises it, so the same promoter is also positively controlled") until it is automatic. That sentence is the memory hook, and unlike an acronym it cannot be recalled backwards.

The single commonest error in this topic

"Inducible means positive control; repressible means negative control." This is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that is designed into distractors. The two descriptions answer different questions. Inducible / repressible answers: what does the small molecule do to expression? Positive / negative answers: does the regulatory protein switch it on or off? The lac operon is the proof that they are independent — it is a negatively controlled inducible operon that is simultaneously under positive control by a completely different protein at the same promoter. Read a question twice to see which axis it is asking about.

Which way does the effector move the protein?

In an inducible system the effector disables the repressor. In a repressible system the effector enables the repressor. Same word — "the effector binds the regulatory protein and changes its shape" — opposite consequence, because the two proteins start from opposite resting states. LacI is born able to bind DNA; TrpR is born unable to.

Exam technique · five kills on any operon stem

  1. First decide which axis the question is on. If the stem or the options use the words inducible / repressible, it is asking what the small molecule does. If they use positive / negative, it is asking what the protein does. Answer the one asked. Options that answer the other axis correctly are the commonest distractor in this topic, and they are wrong for this stem even though every word in them is true.
  2. Kill any option that welds the two axes together — "inducible, and therefore under positive control", or "repressible, i.e. negative control". lac is the standing counterexample: negatively controlled, inducible, and positively controlled by cAMP–CAP at the same promoter.
  3. Kill any option in which the small molecule binds DNA. Effectors bind the regulatory protein. Tryptophan does not bind the trp operator; allolactose does not bind the lac operator. This single sentence kills an option in a surprising fraction of stems.
  4. Kill any option that puts the regulatory gene inside the operon, or that has one promoter producing separate mRNAs for each structural gene. One promoter, one polycistronic transcript; the repressor gene has its own promoter and is not part of it.
  5. Now count what is left. Three kills out of five options leaves you choosing between two, and with −0.25 in Part I a single elimination already makes answering strictly better than a blank. Never leave an operon question empty — the logic is small enough that you will always be able to strike something.

Things about operons that are quietly wrong in most revision notes

The repressor gene is not in the operon. lacI lies next to lacZYA but has its own promoter and its own terminator; a mutation in lacI is not a mutation in the operon. The operator is not always "between the promoter and the first gene". In lac, the main operator O1 is centred at +11 — downstream of the transcription start — and it overlaps the promoter; two auxiliary operators sit at −82 and at +412, the latter inside lacZ itself. A repressor does not always block polymerase binding. It sometimes lets polymerase bind and blocks the escape step instead. Polycistronic does not mean the products are made in equal amounts — ribosome binding sites within one mRNA differ in strength, which is why E. coli makes far more β-galactosidase than transacetylase from the same transcript.

§ 03

The lac operon: two switches on one promoter

The ideaA bacterium offered both glucose and lactose should eat the glucose first — it is the better fuel and needs no new enzymes. So the cell has to answer two separate questions before committing: is there any lactose? and is there anything better available? Two independent proteins answer them at the same promoter. One sits on the DNA and blocks it until lactose appears; the other only helps polymerase land when glucose has run out. Full expression needs a yes to both, which is why the operon behaves like an AND gate rather than a simple switch.

Definition & key facts

The lac operon
Three structural genes in one transcript: lacZ (β-galactosidase, splits lactose into glucose + galactose), lacY (lactose permease, a proton-symport transporter that brings lactose in) and lacA (thiogalactoside transacetylase, a detoxifier of non-metabolisable galactosides). Upstream and separately transcribed: lacI, encoding the tetrameric repressor.
maximal transcription ⇔ allolactose PRESENT AND glucose ABSENT two independent conditions; three of the four states give little or nothing
Full definition, symbols and units
Allolactose
The true inducer — a 1,6-isomer of lactose made as a side reaction of β-galactosidase itself. Lactose is not the inducer. This has a consequence worth stating out loud: the operon cannot be induced at all unless a few molecules of β-galactosidase already exist, so basal, "leaky" expression is not an imperfection but a requirement.
IPTG
Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside — a gratuitous inducer: it induces but cannot be hydrolysed, so it is not consumed and its concentration stays fixed. That is exactly why it is the laboratory inducer of choice (§ 11).
CAP (= CRP)
Catabolite activator protein, a dimer that binds cAMP. The cAMP–CAP complex binds a site centred about 61 bp upstream of the transcription start, bends the DNA by roughly 90°, and contacts the α-subunit C-terminal domain of RNA polymerase, recruiting it to a promoter that is otherwise weak (the lac −35 element is a poor match to consensus).
Catabolite repression
The general phenomenon: a preferred carbon source suppresses the operons for alternative ones. Also called the glucose effect. Its mechanism is not only the cAMP story — see below.

Which lac gene makes which protein, in the order they are transcribed

Z zaps, Y yanks, A acetylates

Our own phrasing rather than a standard hook, but three verbs carry three products in transcription order, and each verb is the reaction itself. Z zaps — lacZ → β-galactosidase, which cuts lactose into glucose + galactose. Y yanks — lacY → permease, which hauls lactose in across the membrane. A acetylates — lacA → thiogalactoside transacetylase, so the third verb is just the enzyme's name read back to you. Said left to right it gives the gene order and the three products together. The same order is the order of falling abundance — Z loudest, A faintest: one transcript, but ribosome binding sites of different strength, so E. coli makes far more β-galactosidase than transacetylase from it. What it does not cover, and what questions actually turn on: lacI is not in this list because it is not in the operon, and the inducer is allolactose, not lactose — made as a side reaction of the very enzyme the operon is trying to switch on, which is why a repressed operon must stay slightly leaky. Neither fact is derivable from the hook; hold them separately.

The glucose effect: what the textbook usually leaves out

The version in most notes says: glucose inhibits adenylyl cyclase, falls, CAP cannot be activated, the weak lac promoter is not recruited, so transcription stays low. That is true, and it is worth knowing. But it is not the dominant mechanism, and a question that offers it as the complete answer is offering you a textbook sentence that has aged.

The larger effect is inducer exclusion. Glucose enters E. coli through the phosphotransferase system, which passes a phosphate down a chain of proteins ending in EIIAGlc. When glucose is flooding in, that phosphate is being handed straight to the incoming sugar, so EIIAGlc spends its time dephosphorylated — and dephospho-EIIAGlc binds lactose permease and shuts it down. No lactose gets in; no allolactose is made; the repressor never comes off. The same phosphorylated form of EIIAGlc that accumulates when glucose is absent is also what stimulates adenylyl cyclase. So one sensor produces both arms of the response, and the cAMP arm is the smaller of the two.

Why does this matter for an exam? Because it explains an observation the simple story cannot: adding cAMP back to a glucose-fed culture does not fully restore lac expression. If the cAMP arm were the whole mechanism, it would.

Diauxic growth — what the curve is actually showing

Grow E. coli on glucose plus lactose and the growth curve has two exponential phases separated by a plateau. The first phase is glucose. The plateau is not starvation and not death: it is the lag while catabolite repression lifts, cAMP rises, permease and β-galactosidase are synthesised from scratch, and enough allolactose accumulates to finish de-repressing the operon. The second phase is lactose, and it is shallower because lactose is the worse substrate. Two predictions follow that examiners like: a lacI⁻ mutant still shows diauxie (the repressor is gone but catabolite repression is not), whereas a strain that cannot make cAMP shows almost no second phase at all.

cis and trans: the one rule that solves every partial-diploid question

An E. coli merodiploid (partial diploid, 部分二倍體) carries a second copy of the lac region on an F′ episome. You are given two genotypes separated by a slash and asked what is produced, with and without inducer. There is exactly one thing to know:

proteins diffuse → act in trans, on both copies · DNA sites cannot move → act in cis, on their own copy only everything below is a corollary

So a gene that encodes a protein (lacI, and the structural genes themselves) can be supplied by either copy; a site that is only a piece of sequence (the promoter, the operator) affects nothing but the genes physically attached to it. Work left to right along each chromosome and ask, for each functional structural gene, "is this copy's operator occupied?"

Exam technique · a partial-diploid genotype in forty seconds

Run these in order and never deviate. The whole difficulty of these items is that people read the allele list left to right and answer from the first striking symbol they see.

  1. Find the working structural gene first, before you look at any regulator. Circle every Z⁺ (or whichever gene the question asks about). If both copies are ⁻, the answer is never, whatever the regulators say, and you are done in five seconds.
  2. Read leftwards from that gene along the same chromosome only. Its own P and its own O are the only DNA sites that can reach it. P⁻ attached → that copy is silent, discard it. Oc attached → that copy is constitutive, and nothing on the other chromosome can change that.
  3. Only if the gene sits behind a normal O⁺ do you look around the whole cell for diffusible protein. Any Is anywhere → permanently off. Otherwise any I⁺ anywhere → inducible. Only I⁻ (or no I at all) anywhere → constitutive.
  4. Combine, remembering that expression is a sum. If any copy is producing enzyme, the cell is producing enzyme — so one constitutive copy makes the whole cell constitutive, regardless of what the other chromosome is doing.
  5. Check the sugars before you commit. If the stem leaves glucose present, cap your answer at "very low" however open the operator is — the AND gate of the four-state figure. If the stem says glucose is absent, CAP is active and the operator is the only question.
  6. Three standing kills. Any option that lets Oc act in trans is dead — it is cis-dominant only. Any option calling I⁻ dominant is dead — it is a loss, and one good copy supplies repressor to both chromosomes. Any option calling Is recessive is dead — it is a gain of an unremovable activity.
  7. Expect the swapped pair. Examiners set two stems with an identical allele list and the two Z alleles exchanged between chromosomes, giving opposite answers. If a genotype looks familiar, that is the reason to check which chromosome carries the working Z, not a reason to answer faster.
Worked example 1 — reading four partial-diploid genotypes

For each merodiploid, state whether β-galactosidase is made without inducer, with inducer, or never. Glucose is absent throughout, so CAP is active and the only question is the operator.

  1. (a) I⁻ P⁺ O⁺ Z⁺ / I⁺ P⁺ O⁺ Z⁻. The I⁺ on the second chromosome makes repressor, which diffuses and binds both operators. The only functional Z is on the first chromosome, behind a normal operator. Repressor comes off only with inducer → inducible. This is the experiment that first showed the repressor is a diffusible product rather than a property of the DNA.
  2. (b) Is P⁺ O⁺ Z⁺ / I⁺ P⁺ O⁺ Z⁺. The super-repressor cannot be prised off by allolactose. It is made continuously and occupies both operators. Adding wild-type repressor does not help — the problem is the presence of a protein, not the absence of one → uninducible; no β-galactosidase ever. Contrast with (a): I⁻ is recessive because it is a loss, Is is dominant because it is a gain of an unremovable activity.
  3. (c) I⁺ P⁺ Oc Z⁺ / I⁺ P⁺ O⁺ Z⁻. Repressor is present and binds the normal operator on the second chromosome — but that chromosome's Z is dead, so nothing is lost. The functional Z sits behind Oc, which no repressor can occupy → constitutive.
  4. (d) I⁺ P⁺ Oc Z⁻ / I⁻ P⁺ O⁺ Z⁺. Swap the two Z alleles from (c) and the answer inverts. Oc is now attached to a dead gene and is therefore invisible. The good Z sits behind a normal operator, and repressor is supplied in trans by the I⁺ on the other chromosome → inducible.
(a) inducible · (b) never · (c) constitutive · (d) inducible

The habit to build: ignore the alleles for a moment, find every functional structural gene, and then look only at what is physically attached to it, plus what diffusible proteins exist anywhere in the cell. Pairs (c) and (d) are the standard exam trick — identical allele lists, opposite arrangement, opposite answer.

Go deeper · three operators, a loop, and why the numbers in your notes are too small

The textbook lac operon has one operator. The real one has three: O1 at +11, O2 at +412 (inside lacZ), and O3 at −82 (inside the 3′ end of lacI). O2 and O3 bind repressor weakly and, on their own, do almost nothing.

What they do is make the tetramer's second DNA-binding surface useful. LacI is a tetramer — effectively a dimer of dimers — with two independent DNA-clamps. One dimer holds O1, the other reaches O2 or O3, and the DNA between them is forced into a loop. The consequence is a large increase in the local concentration of repressor at O1: whenever the repressor lets go of O1 it is still tethered nearby and rebinds almost immediately.

Quantitatively, O1 alone gives about 20-fold repression. The wild-type arrangement gives roughly 1300-fold. Deleting either auxiliary operator drops it back towards the low value. So the loop is responsible for the great majority of the repression, and any figure you have that says the lac operon is repressed "about twentyfold" is describing a crippled operon.

Two exam-relevant consequences. First, this is the prototype for action at a distance by DNA looping — the identical geometric trick that eukaryotic enhancers use in § 06, invented about two billion years earlier. Second, it explains why a repressed operon is still leaky: 1300-fold repression of a promoter that would give several thousand transcripts per generation still leaves a handful, which is exactly the handful needed to make the first allolactose.

§ 04

The trp operon, and attenuation on top of it

The ideaMaking tryptophan from scratch costs a bacterium a great deal, so the five enzymes that do it should be built only when tryptophan runs short. The obvious control is a repressor that tryptophan itself switches on — plenty of tryptophan, gene off. But the cell adds a second, cleverer device on top. Because a bacterial ribosome starts translating a message while it is still being made, the cell can put a short test sentence at the front of the transcript that is deliberately hard to read when tryptophan is scarce. If the ribosome sails through it, the RNA behind folds into a stop sign and transcription is abandoned; if the ribosome stalls, the RNA folds the other way and transcription carries on. The cell is measuring not tryptophan but how easily it can use tryptophan.

Definition & key facts

The trp operon
Five structural genes, trpE D C B A, encoding the enzymes that convert chorismate to tryptophan. It is a repressible operon under negative control: the product of trpR is an aporepressor that cannot bind DNA until tryptophan — the corepressor — binds it.

A student who has memorised a number pair will still hesitate over which condition goes with which; a student who asks "where is the ribosome, and what is it covering?" never will.

The four points about attenuation that are actually examined

1. It is impossible in a eukaryotic nucleus. Not because eukaryotes lack hairpins, but because the nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation, so no ribosome can ever stall on a transcript that polymerase is still making. This is the highest-yield sentence in the section. 2. It senses charged tRNA, not the amino acid. A mutant that cannot charge tRNATrp behaves as if starved of tryptophan even in a broth full of it. 3. It is a second layer, not an alternative. Repression and attenuation act on the same operon and multiply; deleting the leader leaves the operon still 70-fold regulated. 4. The same design recurs across operons — his, leu, phe, thr all have leader peptides stuffed with codons for their own amino acid (the his leader has seven histidines in a row), which is the strongest evidence that the mechanism is real rather than a curiosity.

Worked example 2 — what does deleting the leader actually cost?

Wild-type E. coli regulates the trp operon roughly 70-fold by repression and a further 8-fold by attenuation. (a) What is the overall dynamic range? (b) A strain is constructed in which the leader region is deleted but trpR is intact. Growing in excess tryptophan, how much trp enzyme does it make relative to wild type in the same medium? (c) A second strain is trpR⁻ with an intact leader. Same question.

  1. (a) The two devices act on the same transcription event in series, so the factors multiply: 70 × 8 = 560. Expression in tryptophan-rich medium is about 1/560 of the fully derepressed level.
  2. (b) Delete the leader and only repression remains, so expression in high tryptophan is 1/70 of maximum instead of 1/560. Relative to wild type that is 560/70 = 8× more enzyme — exactly the attenuation factor, which is the point.
  3. (c) Remove the repressor and only attenuation remains: 1/8 of maximum, which is 560/8 = 70× more enzyme than wild type.
  4. Sanity check on the biology. Neither single mutant is unregulated — each still has one working device — which is why an exam option saying "a trpR⁻ strain expresses the operon constitutively at full level" is wrong. Only the double mutant is genuinely constitutive.
(a) ≈560-fold · (b) 8× wild type · (c) 70× wild type

Note the general principle, which recurs in eukaryotic regulation: independent control layers acting on the same output multiply. Three modest 10-fold layers give a thousandfold range, which is why evolution builds regulation in layers rather than making one device very good.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"In the trp operon, tryptophan binds the operator." No — tryptophan binds the repressor protein; small molecules essentially never bind operators. "The trp operon is an example of positive control because tryptophan activates the repressor." No: "positive" and "negative" refer to the effect of the regulatory protein on transcription, not to the effect of the ligand on the protein. TrpR reduces transcription, so the control is negative, full stop. "Attenuation stops translation." It stops transcription — the polymerase falls off inside the leader — and it does so using translation as the sensor. "An attenuator is a kind of operator." An operator is a protein-binding DNA site; an attenuator is an RNA structure that forms after transcription has already begun.

§ 05

Eukaryotic layer one: chromatin, histone marks and DNA methylation

The ideaA human cell has to keep two metres of DNA inside a nucleus a few microns across, and the packaging it uses has an unavoidable side effect: most of the sequence is buried and unreadable. That turns out to be useful. A cell can decide which genes exist for it simply by choosing which stretches to unpack, and — this is the part that matters — it can write chemical tags onto the packaging that say "keep this bit open" or "keep this bit shut", tags that are copied onto the new packaging every time the cell divides. That is how a liver cell's daughters are still liver cells, without anything in the DNA sequence having changed.

Definition & key facts

Euchromatin and heterochromatin
Euchromatin is loosely packed, accessible and largely transcribable; heterochromatin is condensed and silent. Constitutive heterochromatin (centromeres, telomeres, satellite repeats) is silent in every cell type; facultative heterochromatin is silent in some cell types and not others — the inactive X is the extreme example (§ 08).
accessible gene = acetylated histone tails + H3K4me3 + unmethylated CpG island the three marks that travel together at an active human promoter

Histone code
The proposition that combinations of covalent marks on histone tails are read by effector proteins and specify a chromatin state. "Code" overstates it — the marks are correlative and partly redundant — but the operational content is sound: marks create binding surfaces, and it is the reader proteins that do the work.
DNA methylation
Addition of a methyl group to carbon 5 of cytosine, in mammals almost exclusively at CpG dinucleotides, using S-adenosylmethionine as donor. 5-methylcytosine is sometimes called the fifth base. It does not alter base pairing.
CpG island
A stretch of at least ~200 bp with GC content above ~50 % and an observed-to-expected CpG ratio above ~0.6. Roughly 60–70 % of human promoters sit in one, and in normal cells those islands are unmethylated — including at tissue-specific genes that are switched off by other means.

An ATP-driven machine (SWI/SNF, ISWI, CHD, INO80 families) that slides, ejects or swaps nucleosomes. Distinguish it from the enzymes that write covalent marks: remodellers move nucleosomes, writers decorate them.

Acetylation: the one modification whose mechanism you can derive

Histones are extraordinarily basic proteins, roughly a quarter lysine and arginine, and the DNA they hold is a uniformly negative polyanion. The grip between them is electrostatic. Now acetylate a lysine: the positively charged ε-amino group becomes a neutral amide. Do that on many lysines across the tails and the grip weakens, the DNA loosens from the octamer, and the region becomes accessible. That is a genuinely mechanical explanation and you should be able to produce it in one sentence.

Acetylation is written by HATs (p300/CBP, GCN5, PCAF — many of which are the coactivators recruited by the transcription factors of § 06) and erased by HDACs. It is read by bromodomain proteins such as BRD4, which is how an open mark gets converted into recruitment of more machinery. The equilibrium is fast — acetyl groups turn over in minutes — so acetylation is a responsive mark, not an archival one.

The mark that breaks the pattern: methylation is not a synonym for silencing

This is the most reliably mis-stated fact in the whole topic, and distractors are written straight from it. Adding a methyl group to a lysine does not change its charge — the nitrogen stays positive. So methylation cannot loosen chromatin the way acetylation does, and it has no intrinsic direction. What it does is create a shape that particular reader domains recognise, and the consequence depends entirely on which residue was marked and therefore which reader arrives. H3K4me3 at a promoter is the signature of an active gene. H3K36me3 through the body of a gene marks active elongation. H3K9me3 recruits HP1 and builds constitutive heterochromatin. H3K27me3, written by the Polycomb complex PRC2 through its catalytic subunit EZH2, silences developmental genes reversibly. Any option that says "histone methylation condenses chromatin and switches genes off" is describing two of the four and calling it a rule.

Why DNA methylation is the mark that can be inherited

A CpG dinucleotide has a beautiful property: its complement is also CpG, read the other way. So a methylated site is symmetric — methyl groups on both strands — and after replication each daughter duplex is hemimethylated: the parental strand carries the mark, the new one does not. DNMT1 sits at the replication fork and does exactly one thing: it recognises hemimethylated CpG and methylates the new strand. The pattern is therefore copied, base for base, along with the sequence. That single fact is why DNA methylation, and not acetylation, is the substrate of long-term cellular memory and of imprinting in § 08.

Which of the DNMTs is which needs no mnemonic either, and the derivation is one line: after replication a methylated CpG has one marked strand, and the enzyme that reads that one-strand state and completes it is DNMT1 — maintenance. Anything that has to put a mark where there was no mark on either strand cannot be reading a template at all, so it is the other family, DNMT3A/3B — de novo, and therefore the one that operates in the germ line and the early embryo where imprints are being set rather than copied. If you ever find yourself hesitating, count strands.

The same chemistry has a destructive consequence. Cytosine deaminates spontaneously to uracil, which is not a DNA base, so uracil-DNA glycosylase excises it and the site is repaired. But 5-methylcytosine deaminates to thymine — a perfectly legitimate base — leaving a G·T mismatch that repair often resolves the wrong way. Over evolutionary time this has eaten the CpG dinucleotide out of the genome: you would expect about 4.2 % of dinucleotides to be CpG at 41 % GC content, and the observed figure is close to 1 %. The exceptions are the CpG islands, which stayed unmethylated and therefore were never eroded. Their existence is a fossil record of which promoters have been kept methylation-free.

Worked example 3 — is this promoter region a CpG island?

A 1 000 bp region upstream of a human gene has a GC content of 62 % and contains 78 CpG dinucleotides. (a) How many would be expected by chance? (b) Does the region meet the usual CpG-island criteria? (c) For comparison, the genome average is 41 % GC with about 1 % of dinucleotides being CpG — what is the genome-wide ratio, and why is it so low?

  1. (a) If bases were independent, p(C) = p(G) = 0.62/2 = 0.31. A 1 000 bp window contains 999 overlapping dinucleotide positions, so expected CpG = 999 × 0.31 × 0.31 = 96.0.
  2. (b) Observed/expected = 78 / 96.0 = 0.81. Length ≥ 200 bp ✓; GC 62 % ≥ 50 % ✓; ratio 0.81 ≥ 0.6 ✓. It is a CpG island. Note that all three criteria matter: a GC-rich region is not automatically an island, because GC richness alone does not tell you whether the C and G are next to each other on the same strand.
  3. (c) At 41 % GC, expected CpG frequency = 0.205 × 0.205 = 0.042 = 4.2 %. Observed ≈ 1 %, so the genome-wide ratio is 1/4.2 ≈ 0.24 — about a quarter of chance expectation. The cause is the deamination asymmetry above: methylated CpG has been converting to TpG (and, on the other strand, CpA) for hundreds of millions of years.
  4. Why the exam cares. Promoter CpG islands are normally unmethylated. Their hypermethylation is one of the commonest ways a tumour switches off a tumour-suppressor gene — MLH1 in sporadic microsatellite-unstable colorectal cancer, CDKN2A, BRCA1 — and it is a mutation-free silencing, invisible to sequencing unless you look for the methyl groups.

Bacterial DNA methylation does something completely different

Bacteria methylate their DNA too — Dam methylase at GATC, Dcm at CCWGG — and a question will occasionally test whether you assume it means the same thing. It does not. Bacterial methylation marks the parental strand so that mismatch repair knows which base to correct, controls the timing of replication initiation at oriC, and protects the genome from the cell's own restriction enzymes (§ 11). It is not a transcriptional silencing system, and bacteria have no histones to modify at all — their nucleoid-associated proteins (HU, H-NS) are not histones. Archaea are the interesting exception among prokaryotes: they carry genuine histones that wrap DNA into nucleosome-like particles.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"Methylation of DNA and methylation of histones are the same process." Different substrates, different enzymes, opposite reliability of meaning: DNA methylation at a promoter CpG island is almost always repressive, histone methylation depends on the residue. They share only the methyl donor, . "Heterochromatin contains no genes." It contains plenty — they are simply not expressed in that cell, and facultative heterochromatin is by definition full of genes that are expressed elsewhere. "Acetylation adds a negative charge to the histone, which repels DNA." Nearly right and wrong in the detail: acetylation removes a positive charge, giving a neutral amide. There is no new negative charge. "Epigenetic marks are permanent." HDAC inhibitors and DNMT inhibitors are licensed drugs precisely because they are not.

§ 06

Eukaryotic layer two: promoters, enhancers and transcription factors

The ideaOnce a stretch of chromatin is open, something still has to tell RNA polymerase to start, and how often. In a bacterium the promoter does that on its own. In a eukaryote the promoter is deliberately feeble: left to itself it produces almost nothing. The instructions come from separate control sequences that may sit tens of thousands of bases away, in front of the gene, behind it, or inside one of its introns, and that reach the promoter by the DNA between them looping out of the way. Because several such sequences feed into one promoter, and each is read by a different protein, the cell can demand that four or five conditions be met at once before a gene is switched on — which is how a few thousand regulatory proteins specify hundreds of cell types.

Definition & key facts

Core promoter
The ~50 bp around the transcription start site where the preinitiation complex assembles: a TATA box at about −25 in some genes, plus or instead an initiator element, a downstream promoter element, and GC-boxes. It determines where transcription starts and gives only a low basal rate. It is position- and orientation-dependent.
transcription rate = f( Σ activators − Σ repressors ), integrated at the promoter by Mediator combinatorial, and continuous rather than binary
Full definition, symbols and units
Enhancer
A cis-acting element, typically 50–1500 bp, containing clustered binding sites for sequence-specific activators. Its defining property is that it works at any distance and in either orientation, upstream, downstream or intronic. A silencer is the same thing with repressors bound.
General (basal) transcription factors
TFIIA, B, D, E, F, H — required at essentially every Pol II promoter, not regulatory. TFIID contains TBP, which bends the DNA about 80° at a TATA box. TFIIH carries a helicase that melts the DNA and the kinase CDK7, which phosphorylates Ser5 of the Pol II C-terminal domain to release the polymerase from the promoter.
Specific transcription factors
Sequence-specific activators and repressors — about 1600 of them in humans. Modular: a DNA-binding domain plus a separable activation (or repression) domain. Swap the domains between two factors and the specificity follows the DNA-binding domain, which is the experiment that proved modularity.
Mediator
A ~26-subunit bridge that carries the signal from activators bound at an enhancer to the preinitiation complex at the promoter. It is the reason distal activators can influence initiation at all.
Insulator
A boundary element bound by CTCF with cohesin that blocks an enhancer from reaching a promoter on the far side of it, and stops heterochromatin spreading. Insulators partition the genome into topologically associating domains, which is the real answer to "how far can an enhancer act": as far as the edge of its domain.

Begin with a correction, because it is the kind of detail that separates a two-mark answer from a one-mark one. The TATA box is not a universal eukaryotic promoter element. Only a minority of human protein-coding genes — commonly quoted as 10–25 %, and concentrated among tissue-specific, highly regulated genes — have a recognisable TATA box at all. The majority are TATA-less, sit in CpG islands, use dispersed start sites, and recruit TFIID through other contacts. So a question option that says "every eukaryotic gene has a TATA box at −25" is a textbook sentence that has been overtaken, and a question that asks you to compare a housekeeping promoter with a tissue-specific one is asking about exactly this.

Why the enhancer definition is worth memorising word for word

An enhancer is defined operationally, by what happens when you move it. Take the element out, put it back 3 kb upstream, 20 kb downstream, backwards, or inside an intron — and the gene is still enhanced. Do the same to a promoter and transcription stops or starts in the wrong place. Distance- and orientation-independence is the definition, and it is the discriminator in almost every question on this topic.

The mechanism that makes such indifference possible is looping. Activators bound at the enhancer recruit coactivators — HATs that open the local chromatin, and Mediator, which physically contacts the preinitiation complex. The DNA between simply bulges out. Notice that this is the same geometric device as the lac repressor bridging O1 and O3 in § 03: what is new in eukaryotes is not the trick but the scale, and the insulators that stop it running out of control.

Two clinical consequences follow directly and are worth carrying. First, a mutation in an enhancer can cause disease while leaving the coding sequence perfect — limb malformations from mutations in the ZRS enhancer of SHH, which lies about a megabase away inside an intron of a different gene, are the standard example. Second, a structural rearrangement that deletes an insulator can let a strong enhancer reach a gene it was never meant to reach — enhancer hijacking, a recognised mechanism of oncogene activation.

Nothing in this section is worth an acronym, and it is worth saying why, because § 06 looks like a section full of lists. The enhancer, the promoter, the insulator and the silencer are told apart by one operational test — move it and see whether it still works — so a hook would only get in the way of the test. The general factors are the single arbitrary item here, and the honest advice is to carry the assembly order as the bare six-letter string D · A · B · F · E · H rather than wrap a sentence round it: it is shorter than any mnemonic you could build, and in practice only two of the six are examined for content — TFIID, which contains TBP and bends the DNA at a TATA box, and TFIIH, which carries the helicase and the CDK7 kinase that phosphorylates Ser5 of the CTD to release polymerase from the promoter. If a stem names a general factor and asks what it does, it is almost always one of those two.

How a eukaryote does what an operon does

Ask yourself how a human cell switches on the twenty-odd genes of a stress response at once, given that they lie on different chromosomes and each has its own promoter. The answer is that they share a short DNA sequence — a response element — and one activated factor binds all of them. Glucocorticoid enters the cell, binds its receptor, releases it from HSP90, and the receptor dimer travels to every GRE in the genome. Heat shock activates HSF1, which finds every HSE. activates PKA, which phosphorylates CREB, which sits on every CRE. This is coordinate control without an operon, and questions often set it against the operon directly. The one-line contrast: bacteria co-regulate by putting the genes together; eukaryotes co-regulate by putting the same address on genes that are far apart.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"An enhancer is the part of the promoter that increases transcription." No — it is a separate element, often far away, and the whole point of the word is that it is not part of the promoter. "Transcription factors bind RNA polymerase and carry it to the gene." Sequence-specific factors bind DNA; the contact with polymerase is indirect, through Mediator and coactivators. "General transcription factors determine which genes are expressed in which tissue." The opposite: they are needed at every gene and carry no tissue information; the specific factors do that. "One gene, one promoter." Many human genes have two or more alternative promoters producing transcripts with different first exons and different tissue distributions. "Eukaryotic mRNA can be polycistronic if the genes are close enough." It is not; the ribosome scans from the cap and stops at the first stop codon, so a second cistron would never be translated.

§ 07

After the transcript: splicing, stability, small RNAs and translation

The ideaMaking the transcript is not the end of the decision. The same string of RNA can be cut and rejoined in different ways to yield different proteins; it can be kept for ten hours or destroyed in ten minutes; it can be sitting in the cytoplasm perfectly intact and simply not read. Each of those is a separate control, and each is faster than going back to the gene. The cost of that speed is waste — the cell has already paid for the transcript it is about to throw away — which is exactly why these controls are used for things that must be switched off quickly: inflammatory signals, growth factors, anything whose overproduction is dangerous.

Alternative splicing, and why it is the answer to a question you should expect

When the human genome turned out to contain about 20 000 protein-coding genes — fewer than a grape vine, and not obviously more than a nematode's 20 000 — the resolution of the paradox was that a gene is not a protein. Roughly 95 % of multi-exon human genes are alternatively spliced, and the modes are worth being able to name because a diagram of one is a natural exam figure: cassette exon skipping (the commonest), mutually exclusive exons, alternative 5′ or 3′ splice sites that shorten or lengthen an exon, intron retention (common in plants and in regulated human transcripts), and alternative polyadenylation, which changes the 3′ UTR and therefore which microRNAs can act.

Two illustrations do most of the teaching work. The calcitonin/CGRP gene produces calcitonin in thyroid C cells and the quite unrelated neuropeptide CGRP in neurons, purely by choosing a different poly(A) site and therefore a different final exon. And Dscam in Drosophila offers 12, 48, 33 and 2 alternatives at four positions, giving 12 × 48 × 33 × 2 = 38 016 possible isoforms from one gene — more distinct proteins than the fly has genes. The general point: splicing is combinatorial, so possibilities multiply.

Worked example 4 — mRNA half-life, steady state, and how fast a gene can be switched off

A cell transcribes two genes at the identical rate of 10 molecules per minute. Transcript A (a cytokine mRNA with AU-rich elements) has a half-life of 20 min; transcript B (a structural protein mRNA) has a half-life of 10 h. (a) What is the steady-state number of each? (b) Transcription of both is now stopped completely. How long does each take to fall to 10 % of its steady-state level? (c) What does this tell you about which control point a cell should use for a signal that must be shut off fast?

  1. (a) First-order decay gives k = ln2 / t½. For A, k = 0.693/20 = 0.03466 min⁻¹; for B, k = 0.693/600 = 0.001155 min⁻¹.
  2. Steady state is v/k. A: 10/0.03466 = 289 molecules. B: 10/0.001155 = 8656 molecules. Same synthesis rate, a 30× difference in abundance — and the ratio is just the ratio of the half-lives, 600/20 = 30.
  3. (b) Falling to a fraction f takes t½ · log₂(1/f), and log₂10 = 3.32. A: 20 × 3.32 = 66 min. B: 600 × 3.32 = 1993 min ≈ 33 h.
  4. (c) The transcription rate does not appear in the answer to (b) at all. How fast a message disappears depends only on its half-life, never on how fast it was made. So a cell that needs to withdraw a signal within an hour has no choice but to give the transcript a short half-life — which is exactly why cytokine, c-fos and c-myc mRNAs carry AU-rich elements, and why a mutation that deletes those elements is oncogenic.
(a) 289 vs 8 656 molecules · (b) 66 min vs ≈33 h · (c) speed is set by t½ alone

Exam technique · the half-life question, and the two shortcuts that skip the algebra

  1. Recognise it instantly. A stem that gives a synthesis rate and a half-life, or two transcripts and their half-lives, is this question. Nothing conceptual is being tested; every mark is in one ratio and one unit conversion.
  2. Convert to one time unit first, before anything else. Half the errors here are 5 h versus 5 min. Write "5 h = 300 min" on the paper.
  3. If the question asks for a comparison, never compute k. Same synthesis rate ⇒ [mRNA]ss is directly proportional to t½, so the abundance ratio is the half-life ratio. 300 min against 15 min is 20-fold, and that is the whole calculation. Only reach for k = ln2 / t½ and [X] = v/k when an absolute number of molecules is demanded.
  4. If the question asks how fast, use t = t½ × log₂(1/f) and carry one constant: log₂10 = 3.32. So 90 % gone ≈ 3.3 half-lives, 99 % gone ≈ 6.6 half-lives. A 20 min transcript reaches 10 % in 20 × 3.32 ≈ 66 min; a 10 h one takes 600 × 3.32 ≈ 1 990 min ≈ 33 h.
  5. The sanity check that kills options for free: the synthesis rate must not appear in any answer about clearance time. If an option makes the shut-off faster because transcription was faster, or slower because it was slower, it is wrong on dimensional grounds and you can strike it without doing arithmetic at all.
  6. Second free kill: direction. The shorter half-life must give the lower steady-state level. An option that makes the rapidly turned-over transcript the more abundant one — usually justified with something plausible about "continuous replacement" — is beatable on sight.
  7. This one deserves the full 66 seconds. It is the only arithmetic in this note that recurs, the distractors are numerically close, and a careless unit conversion loses a mark you had already earned.

Small RNAs: one machine, two jobs

A microRNA gene is transcribed by Pol II like any other, producing a long pri-miRNA with a hairpin in it. In the nucleus, Drosha with its partner DGCR8 crops the hairpin out as a ~70 nt pre-miRNA; exportin-5 carries it to the cytoplasm; Dicer cuts off the loop to leave a ~22 nt duplex; one strand is loaded into Argonaute and the other discarded. What happens next depends entirely on how well the guide matches its target. Imperfect pairing — essentially just the seed, nucleotides 2–8 — represses translation and recruits deadenylases, and this is the normal animal microRNA mode. Perfect pairing lets Argonaute act as an endonuclease and cut the target outright, which is the normal plant microRNA mode and the mode of experimental siRNA.

Two numbers make the scale clear. Because a seed match is only seven base pairs, one microRNA has hundreds of targets, and more than half of all human genes are thought to be under microRNA control. That is why microRNAs behave like rheostats fine-tuning whole programmes rather than switches for single genes, and why several are classical oncogenes or tumour suppressors (the miR-17~92 cluster; let-7, which represses RAS).

The miRNA/siRNA distinctions that get mangled

"miRNA and siRNA use different pathways." They share Dicer, Argonaute and RISC. What differs is origin — miRNA is endogenous, transcribed from the organism's own genome as a hairpin; siRNA is derived from long double-stranded RNA, typically viral, transposon-derived or introduced — and degree of complementarity, which then determines the outcome. "RNA interference silences the gene." It acts on the transcript, in the cytoplasm, after the gene has already been transcribed; the DNA is untouched. (Plants do have a genuine RNA-directed DNA-methylation pathway, and fission yeast uses the same small-RNA machinery to direct H3K9 methylation and build heterochromatin — but fission yeast has essentially no DNA methylation at all, and neither case is what the term normally means.) "A microRNA binds the coding sequence." Almost always the 3′ UTR — which is why alternative polyadenylation that shortens the 3′ UTR is a way for a proliferating cell to escape microRNA control.

Iron: the same signal, two opposite outcomes, decided by position alone

Ferritin stores iron; the transferrin receptor imports it. When iron is scarce the cell wants little ferritin and many receptors; when iron is plentiful, the reverse. Both mRNAs carry the same stem-loop, the iron-response element, and the same protein — iron regulatory protein — binds it whenever iron is low. The difference is where the element sits. In ferritin mRNA the IRE is in the 5′ UTR, so a bound IRP physically obstructs the scanning ribosome: translation is blocked. In transferrin receptor mRNA the IREs are in the 3′ UTR, where a bound IRP shields the transcript from nucleases: the mRNA is stabilised and more receptor is made. One signal, one binding protein, two opposite responses, purely from the geometry. If you can reconstruct this from first principles you can answer any question about untranslated regions.

Two things in this section look like candidates for a 口訣 and are not, and it is worth being explicit about both. The iron story needs no hook: ask only where the ribosome is going. It enters at the cap and scans forwards, so a protein parked in the 5′ UTR is a roadblock in front of it and translation stops — ferritin. It never travels through the 3′ UTR, so a protein sitting there cannot obstruct anything; all it can do is cover the transcript against nucleases, which stabilises it — transferrin receptor. Position relative to the scanning ribosome gives you both answers and also tells you why a microRNA site, which works by recruiting deadenylases rather than by blocking traffic, lives in the 3′ UTR. miRNA versus siRNA needs no hook either: they share Dicer, Argonaute and RISC, so the only two questions are where did it come from (endogenous hairpin versus long double-stranded RNA) and how well does it pair. Perfect pairing lines the target up in Argonaute's catalytic site, so it is cut; imperfect pairing cannot, so all that is left is repression and deadenylation. The outcome follows from the pairing, every time.

§ 08

Epigenetics, X-inactivation and imprinting

The ideaTwo cells with identical DNA can behave differently and pass that difference on to their descendants. The information that makes them different is not in the sequence — it is written on top of it, as methyl groups on the DNA and marks on the histones, and it is copied when the cell divides. Sometimes the mark is applied to a whole chromosome, as when a female mammal shuts down one of her two X chromosomes so that she does not make twice the dose a male makes. Sometimes it is applied in the egg or the sperm, so that a gene remembers which parent it came from and behaves accordingly — with the uncomfortable consequence that for such genes you effectively have only one working copy.

Definition & key facts

Epigenetic
A change in gene expression that is heritable through cell division and does not involve a change in DNA sequence. Both halves matter: a transient change in expression is regulation, not epigenetics, and a change in the sequence is a mutation, not an epigenetic mark.

X-inactivation, and the part that is usually left out

A female mammal has two X chromosomes and a male has one, so without intervention every X-linked gene would be transcribed at twice the dose in females. The solution is to silence one X per cell, early in development, at random, and then to keep the same choice in every descendant of that cell. The visible result is the Barr body; the biological result is that every female is a mosaic. Calico coat colour is the textbook demonstration; the medically useful ones are the patchy sweat-gland distribution in carriers of X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia, and the fact that a carrier of an X-linked disease can be mildly affected — or, if the inactivation happened to be skewed, severely affected.

Now the part that is usually left out, and that resolves an obvious objection. If the inactive X were completely silent, Turner syndrome (45,X) ought to be indistinguishable from a normal female, since both have one active X — and Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) indistinguishable from a normal male. Both clearly have phenotypes. The resolution is that silencing is incomplete: about 15 % of human X-linked genes escape inactivation, including everything in the pseudoautosomal regions, which are the parts of the X that pair with the Y and are present in two active copies in normal males and females alike. It is dosage of those escaping genes — SHOX among them, hence the short stature in Turner and the tall stature in Klinefelter — that produces the syndromes. Note also that Barr-body counting is a chromosome count, not a sex test: it tells you the number of X chromosomes minus one and says nothing about the Y.

Three species, three different solutions to the same dosage problem

Man off · Fly up · Worm down

Our own phrasing, not a standard hook. This is a genuinely arbitrary set — nothing about being a fly makes doubling the obvious answer — and it is flagged in the note above as a favourite comparison, so it is worth six words. Man off: mammals switch one X off, one per diploid cell, so the female is a mosaic and shows a Barr body. Fly up: Drosophila instead turns the male's single X up, doubling transcription from it. Worm down: C. elegans turns both of the hermaphrodite's Xs down, halving output from each. The direction also tells you which sex is adjusted, so you never have to store that separately: only a two-X sex has an X to switch off or a pair to turn down, so mammals and worms act on the female/hermaphrodite, while turning one X up can only mean the one-X sex, the fly's male. That is the discriminator a question will use. It says nothing about the two facts that carry most of the marks here, and those are derivable rather than memorable. Barr bodies = (number of X) − 1, because exactly one X stays active in every cell. And silencing is incomplete — about 15 % of X-linked genes escape, including the pseudoautosomal region — which is the only reason 45,X and 47,XXY have phenotypes at all.

Imprinting: why the parent of origin can matter more than the mutation

For most genes, the two alleles you inherit are functionally interchangeable. For an imprinted gene they are not: one is marked in the germ line as silent, and the cell obeys the mark regardless of whether the underlying sequence is intact. So an imprinted gene is functionally hemizygous — you have one working copy, not two — and a single hit removes it entirely. That is the whole clinical significance of imprinting, and it is the reasoning an exam question wants.

The mechanism at the best-understood locus is worth one paragraph because it explains how a methyl group turns into a decision. At 11p15, IGF2 (a growth factor, paternally expressed) and H19 (maternally expressed) share a set of downstream enhancers. Between them lies an ICR. On the maternal chromosome the ICR is unmethylated, so CTCF binds it and forms an insulator: the enhancers are walled off from IGF2 and act on H19 instead. On the paternal chromosome the ICR is methylated, CTCF cannot bind — its binding is methylation-sensitive — the insulator does not form, and the enhancers reach IGF2. One methyl mark, one protein that cannot bind through it, and the enhancers of § 06 do the rest. Loss of imprinting here causes overgrowth: Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, and biallelic IGF2 expression in Wilms tumour.

Ask only: which parent's contribution is missing? A deletion removes that parent's copy directly. Three inheritance patterns that look like imprinting and are not

Mitochondrial inheritance is maternal, but for a completely different reason — the sperm contributes essentially no mitochondria — and it affects all offspring of an affected mother, whereas an imprinted disorder depends on the parent's sex in each generation. X-linked recessive inheritance also shows a parent-of-origin pattern (affected males inherit from carrier mothers) but the gene is on the X and the allele is genuinely defective. Anticipation (§ 09) makes a disease worse down the generations and has a paternal or maternal bias, but the cause is a physically expanding repeat, i.e. a sequence change. Imprinting is the only one of the four in which the transmitted DNA is normal and only the mark differs.

Where popular accounts overstate the evidence

"Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance" — the idea that your grandparents' diet or stress is written into your genome — is far weaker in humans than headlines suggest, and it is worth knowing why. There are two waves of genome-wide demethylation: one immediately after fertilisation, and one in the primordial germ cells of the developing embryo, which erases the imprints so they can be reset according to that individual's sex. For a mark to reach a grandchild it must survive both. A handful of loci appear to; the great majority do not. The famous human observations — the Dutch Hunger Winter cohorts, the Överkalix records — are epidemiological associations with plausible but unproven mechanisms. The mouse agouti experiments are real, but they involve a transposon inserted next to the gene, which is a special case. An exam option asserting that acquired characteristics are routinely inherited epigenetically in humans is overreaching; an option saying that some marks escape reprogramming at some loci is defensible.

One final observation that ties the section to the first: the whole edifice is reversible. Somatic cell nuclear transfer takes an adult nucleus, puts it in an enucleated oocyte, and the oocyte's factors strip the marks and restore totipotency. Four transcription factors — OCT4, SOX2, KLF4 and MYC — do something similar in a dish, producing induced pluripotent stem cells. Both experiments make the same point twice over: the genome of a differentiated cell is intact, and the differences between cell types are written in marks that can be erased.

§ 09

Mutation and its consequences for the protein

The ideaA change in the DNA matters only in so far as it changes what the protein does, and the size of the change in the DNA is a poor guide to the size of the change in the protein. Swapping one base can be completely harmless, or can convert a codon into a stop and destroy the gene. Removing one base is nearly always a catastrophe, while removing three is often survivable — because it is the reading frame, not the number of bases, that the ribosome cares about. Learn to trace a change through to the protein and the classification names itself.

Definition & the rule that decides most cases

Point mutation
A change at a single base pair. A transition replaces a purine with a purine or a pyrimidine with a pyrimidine (A↔G, C↔T); a transversion swaps the classes. Every site has one possible transition and two possible transversions, so pure chance would give a transition/transversion ratio of 0.5 — the observed human genome-wide figure is about 2.0, meaning transitions are roughly fourfold enriched, largely because of 5-methylcytosine deamination at CpG (§ 05).

Full definition, symbols and units
Silent (synonymous)
The codon changes but the amino acid does not, usually because the third position is degenerate. Standard textbooks call these "no effect"; that is an approximation — see the warning below.
Missense
One amino acid replaces another. Conservative if the replacement has similar chemistry (Asp→Glu), non-conservative if not (Glu→Val). Consequence depends far more on where in the protein than on the crudeness of the swap: an active-site or buried residue matters, a surface loop residue often does not.
Nonsense
A sense codon becomes a stop codon. The product is a truncated protein — or, more often, no protein at all, because nonsense-mediated decay destroys the transcript (§ 07).
Loss of function vs gain of function
Loss-of-function alleles are usually recessive, because one good copy normally suffices; they are dominant when 50 % is not enough (haploinsufficiency) or when the bad product interferes with the good one (dominant negative). Gain-of-function alleles — a protein that is constitutively active, or expressed in the wrong place — are almost always dominant.

Worked example 5 — one gene, four changes, four different answers

A gene's coding (sense) strand begins 5′-ATG GAG CGA AAG CTG TGG GAT TAA-3′. Give the wild-type peptide, then classify each of the following and predict the protein: (a) the G of codon 5 (CTG) becomes A; (b) the A of codon 2 (GAG) becomes T; (c) the C of codon 3 (CGA) becomes T; (d) the first A of codon 4 (AAG) is deleted.

  1. Wild type. The coding strand equals the mRNA with U for T, so no complementing and no reversing: 5′-AUG GAG CGA AAG CUG UGG GAU UAA-3′ = Met–Glu–Arg–Lys–Leu–Trp–Asp, seven residues, then stop. (The stop codon is not an amino acid; counting it is the classic slip.)
  2. (a) CTG → CTA. Both are leucine. Silent, and a transition (G→A, purine to purine). Predicted protein: unchanged. Add the caveat that "silent" means "synonymous", not "consequence-free".
  3. (b) GAG → GTG. Glu → Val: a negatively charged residue replaced by a branched hydrophobic one. Non-conservative missense, and a transversion (A→T). This is precisely the sickle-cell change, and its consequence is instructive — the new hydrophobic patch on the surface of deoxyhaemoglobin lets molecules polymerise, so the defect is not loss of oxygen binding but gain of an unwanted interaction.
  4. (c) CGA → TGA. Arg → stop. Nonsense, and a transition (C→T). Note why this one is over-represented in real mutation databases: the C sits in a CpG dinucleotide, is therefore usually methylated, and 5-methylcytosine deaminates to thymine (§ 05). Predicted protein: none, if the transcript is subject to NMD; a two-residue fragment otherwise.
  5. (d) Deleting one A. The message becomes AUG GAG CGA AGC UGU GGG AUU AA → Met–Glu–Arg–Ser–Cys–Gly–Ile… The first three residues are right and every residue after them is wrong. Frameshift. The original UAA has been shifted out of frame, so translation continues into whatever follows until a new stop appears — on average within about 21 codons, since 3 of the 64 triplets are stops. Deleting three bases here would have removed lysine and left the rest of the protein perfect.
(a) silent, transition · (b) non-conservative missense, transversion · (c) nonsense, transition, CpG hotspot · (d) frameshift

Method to reuse: write the mRNA (coding strand → same sequence, U for T; template strand → complement it, watching the primes), read it in triplets from the AUG, stop at the stop codon, and only then reach for a classification name.

Exam technique · sequence in, mutation class out — a fixed seven-step order

  1. Check which strand you were given, once, before you write anything. Coding (sense) strand → the mRNA is the same sequence with U for T; no complementing, no reversing. Template strand → complement it and watch the primes. Getting this wrong makes every later step wrong, and it costs three seconds to check.
  2. If it is an indel, count the bases and take mod 3 first. Not a multiple of 3 → frameshift, and you can immediately strike every option that talks about a single altered residue. A multiple of 3 → in-frame, the rest of the protein is intact, and ΔF508 is the model.
  3. If it is a substitution, classify the chemistry before the consequence. Purine↔purine or pyrimidine↔pyrimidine (A↔G, C↔T) = transition; across the classes = transversion. This is independent of what happens to the protein and is often half the answer.
  4. Now read the new codon off the table — do not infer it. Same amino acid → silent. Stop codon → nonsense. Different amino acid → missense, and only then ask conservative or not.
  5. The trap that catches most people: a first- or second-position change need not change the amino acid. CGA → AGA is still arginine; CGA → CGG is still arginine. Both appear as options precisely because they look like "must be missense". Check the table.
  6. Apply the NMD filter before you answer "truncated protein". A premature stop more than ~50–55 nt upstream of the last exon junction destroys the transcript, so the honest prediction is no protein — and the "short protein" option is the trap. Only a stop in the final exon escapes NMD and really does give a truncated product, which is why last-exon nonsense alleles are the dominant-negative ones.
  7. Read the stem back for a CpG cue. If the changed C sits in a CG dinucleotide, the examiner is pointing at 5-methylcytosine deamination, so the expected change is C→T (a transition), and CGA→TGA turning arginine into a stop is the specific case they mean.

Repeat expansions: the location tells you the mechanism

A tandem repeat that lengthens as it passes down the generations produces anticipation — earlier onset and greater severity in each successive generation — which is otherwise almost unknown in genetics. But the four classic diseases work by four different mechanisms, and the discriminating fact is simply where in the gene the repeat sits. This is one of the tidiest pieces of reasoning in the syllabus.

Why a nonsense mutation often gives less protein than you predicted

The intuitive answer to "what does a premature stop codon produce" is "a short protein". In most cases the real answer is nothing, because nonsense-mediated decay recognises the transcript and destroys it before much of it is translated. The rule is positional: during splicing an exon junction complex is deposited just upstream of each junction, and the first ribosome to traverse the message knocks them off. A stop codon more than ~50–55 nucleotides upstream of the last junction leaves at least one complex undisplaced, and that is the signal. Three consequences the paper likes. A PTC in the final exon escapes NMD and does give a truncated protein — which is why last-exon nonsense mutations are the ones that behave as dominant negatives. NMD is also why a nonsense mutation is usually less damaging than a frameshift producing a near-full-length garbled product. And it is why "exon-skipping" therapy works in Duchenne muscular dystrophy: an antisense oligonucleotide forces the spliceosome to omit an exon, restoring the reading frame and converting a Duchenne (frameshift, no dystrophin) genotype into a milder Becker (in-frame, partly functional) one.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"Silent mutations have no effect." They have no effect on the amino acid sequence. They can still abolish an exonic splicing enhancer, change mRNA secondary structure, or slow the ribosome enough to alter folding. "Missense mutations are less serious than nonsense mutations." Often, but not reliably — a missense in an active site can be lethal while a nonsense in the last exon of a non-essential domain may be silent clinically. "A mutation is a change that causes disease." A mutation is any heritable change in sequence; the great majority are neutral, because most of the genome is non-coding and most coding changes are synonymous or conservative. "Transversions are commoner because there are more ways to make one." There are twice as many possible transversions per site, and they are still about half as frequent — hence the fourfold enrichment of transitions. "Somatic mutations can be inherited." Only germ-line mutations reach the next generation; a somatic mutation is confined to the clone that carries it, which is precisely what makes cancer a somatic genetic disease.

§ 10

Transposons and mobile DNA

The ideaSome stretches of DNA can move. A few carry only the instructions for moving themselves, which means they spread through a genome for no reason other than that they are good at spreading — they are, in a real sense, parasites of the chromosome. About half of your genome is made of their remains. Most of those copies are broken and go nowhere, but the process is not finished: a new insertion can land inside a gene and destroy it, and two identical copies in different places give the recombination machinery a chance to line up the wrong pair and delete everything in between.

Definition & the primary split

Transposable element
A DNA sequence that can change its position within a genome. Discovered by Barbara McClintock in maize in the 1940s — the Ac/Ds system that produces the mottled kernels — and disbelieved for thirty years; Nobel Prize 1983.
Class I (retrotransposon): DNA → RNA → DNA, copy-and-paste · Class II (DNA transposon): DNA → DNA, cut-and-paste the split that decides whether copy number can grow
Full definition, symbols and units
Terminal inverted repeats (TIR)
Short sequences at each end of a DNA transposon, in opposite orientation, that the transposase recognises. Their presence in a sequence is the diagnostic sign of a class II element.
Target-site duplication (TSD)
Short direct repeats of host sequence flanking a newly inserted element, created because the enzyme cuts the target DNA in a staggered fashion and the single-stranded gaps are then filled in. Every transposition event leaves this fingerprint — it is how you recognise an insertion in a sequence.
Autonomous vs non-autonomous
An autonomous element encodes the enzymes it needs. A non-autonomous one has lost them and can move only when an autonomous relative supplies the machinery in trans. Alu is the great example: 1.1 million copies, none of which can move on their own.

Two mechanisms, and what each implies

A DNA transposon encodes a transposase that binds the inverted repeats at both ends, cuts the element out, and pastes it somewhere else. Because the element leaves its old home, cut-and-paste does not by itself increase copy number — the way such elements nevertheless multiply is by moving from an already-replicated region into an unreplicated one during S phase, so that the old site is copied again.

A retrotransposon is transcribed into RNA, and that RNA is reverse-transcribed back into DNA at a new site. The original never leaves. So copy number can only go up, and it does: this is why retrotransposons dominate mammalian genomes. LINE-1 does it by target-primed reverse transcription — its ORF2 endonuclease nicks the target DNA, and the free 3′-OH created by that nick serves as the primer on which reverse transcriptase copies the L1 RNA directly into place. That mechanism is why L1 insertions are so often 5′-truncated: reverse transcription frequently fails before it reaches the far end, leaving a dead fragment.

The same L1 machinery, acting on the wrong RNA, produces two further features of the genome. Grab a cellular mRNA instead of L1's own and you make a processed pseudogene — a copy of a gene with no introns and no promoter, flanked by target-site duplications and ending in a poly(A) tract. Grab an Alu transcript, and you make another Alu; Alu is derived from 7SL RNA, the RNA of the signal recognition particle, and it has ridden L1's machinery to over a million copies.

Inverted or direct — which pair of repeats is which, reading a sequence outwards

Inner Inverted · Distal Duplicated

Our own phrasing, not a standard hook, but four words fix an ordering that questions genuinely exploit, and they map straight onto the figure above read from the middle outwards — each pair sharing its initial with the thing it names. Inner Inverted — the inner pair, immediately flanking the transposase, are the terminal inverted repeats — two I's facing each other, as the repeats themselves do: they point inwards, they belong to the element, and they are what the transposase grips. Distal Duplicated — the outer pair are the target-site duplications, and the D carries all three facts you need: distal, direct (they point the same way) and duplicated host sequence, which exists only because the enzyme cut the target DNA in a staggered fashion and the gaps were filled in. So the order along the DNA is TSD → TIR → element → TIR → TSD. Why it is worth having: a stem can show you a stretch of sequence and ask what it is, and the two repeat types answer different questions. Inverted repeats tell you what kind of element this is — their presence is the diagnostic sign of a class II DNA transposon. Direct repeats tell you that a transposition happened here at all, whatever the class, which is why a processed pseudogene — no introns, a poly(A) tract, no promoter — is recognised by its target-site duplications despite having no inverted repeats of its own.

What mobile DNA actually does to a genome — the four consequences

1. Insertional mutagenesis. An element landing in an exon or a splice site destroys the gene. Documented human examples include L1 insertions into factor VIII causing haemophilia A, and into APC, NF1 and DMD. Roughly one birth in twenty to fifty carries a new retrotransposition event. 2. Unequal recombination. Two Alu elements at different positions can mis-align during meiosis, and crossing over between them deletes or duplicates everything in between — the mechanism behind many LDLR deletions in familial hypercholesterolaemia and behind α-thalassaemia deletions. This is arguably the larger clinical effect, and it does not require the element to be active at all. 3. Regulatory innovation. Elements carry promoters and binding sites with them, so an insertion can put a gene under new control; a substantial fraction of mammalian transcription-factor binding sites are transposon-derived. 4. Domestication. The RAG1/RAG2 recombinase that performs V(D)J recombination in lymphocytes is a domesticated transposase, and the recombination signal sequences it recognises are the descendants of terminal inverted repeats — which is why V(D)J recombination excises a circle of DNA in exactly the way a cut-and-paste transposon does. The adaptive immune system was built out of a parasite.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"Transposons are junk DNA with no function." The elements themselves are best described as selfish rather than functional, but their sequences are a major raw material for regulatory evolution, and at least one — syncytin — is essential to human reproduction. "No function" and "not there for the organism's benefit" are different claims. "A retrotransposon is a retrovirus." Endogenous retroviruses and LTR retrotransposons are close relatives, but a retrovirus has a functional env gene and can leave the cell and infect another. "Transposons move by breaking and rejoining at random sequences." Most have marked target preferences; L1 prefers a degenerate TTAAAA-like nick site. "Because half our genome is transposons, half our genome is currently jumping." Almost all copies are dead fossils — of half a million L1 copies, roughly a hundred still work.

§ 11

The laboratory toolkit, as biology examines it

The ideaEvery technique in this section is an application of two properties you already know: DNA is uniformly negatively charged, and a single strand will find and stick to its complement. Charge gives you separation by size; complementarity gives you detection, copying and targeting. The exam does not ask you to run the experiments. It asks you which one answers a given question — and the way to get that right is to ask, every time, what is being separated, what is being detected, and what would the negative result look like?

Definition & the counting rule

Restriction endonuclease
A bacterial enzyme that cuts double-stranded DNA at a specific, usually palindromic, recognition sequence of 4–8 bp. In its natural setting it is half of a restriction–modification system: the host methylates its own copies of the site, so only invading DNA is cut.

Electrophoresis: the technique everything else is measured on

Every phosphate in a nucleic acid backbone carries one negative charge, and every nucleotide carries exactly one phosphate. So charge is strictly proportional to length, the charge-to-mass ratio is constant regardless of sequence, and mobility in a gel depends only on how much the matrix impedes the molecule — which is to say, on size. DNA always runs towards the anode, and small fragments run further. Over the resolving range of a gel, distance migrated is roughly linear in log(size), which is why a size ladder lets you read an unknown fragment off a straight line.

Two traps live here. The first is direction: an option saying "larger fragments migrate further" is simply backwards, and it appears often. The second is conformation. A plasmid preparation typically shows three bands from one molecule — supercoiled, which is compact and runs fastest; linear; and nicked open-circular, which is floppiest and runs slowest. Their apparent sizes are meaningless. If you want to know how big a plasmid is, you linearise it with a single-cutter first.

Worked example 6 — building a restriction map from digest data

A circular plasmid of 10.0 kb gives the following fragments. EcoRI alone: 6.5 and 3.5 kb. HindIII alone: a single 10.0 kb band. EcoRI + HindIII together: 4.0, 3.5 and 2.5 kb. Construct the map.

  1. Check the sums first. 6.5 + 3.5 = 10.0 ✓; 4.0 + 3.5 + 2.5 = 10.0 ✓. If a set of fragments does not add to the total, you have either missed a co-migrating band or misread a partial digest.
  2. Count the sites. The molecule is circular, so fragments = sites. EcoRI gives two fragments → two EcoRI sites. HindIII gives one fragment of full length → this is not "no cut": an uncut circular plasmid does not run as a 10.0 kb linear band. One cut opens the circle into one linear molecule, so there is one HindIII site. The double digest should therefore give 2 + 1 = 3 fragments — and it does, which confirms the reading.
  3. Place the EcoRI sites. Set the first at position 0. The two arcs are 3.5 and 6.5 kb, so the second is at 3.5 kb.
  4. Place HindIII. The double digest still contains a 3.5 kb fragment, so HindIII did not cut the 3.5 kb arc — it must lie in the 6.5 kb arc, splitting it into 2.5 + 4.0. Measuring from the EcoRI site at 3.5 kb, HindIII is either 2.5 kb further round (position 6.0) or 4.0 kb further round (position 7.5).
  5. Are those two different maps? No — they are mirror images of one another, and a restriction map is only ever determined up to reflection, because nothing in the data says which way round the circle you are reading. To break the tie you need extra information: a third enzyme whose sites are already placed, a partial digest referenced to a labelled end, or the known orientation of a gene on the plasmid.
2 EcoRI sites (0 and 3.5 kb) + 1 HindIII site at 6.0 kb — or its mirror image at 7.5 kb

The transferable method: sum-check, then count sites from the number of fragments (remembering linear = k+1, circular = k), then use the fragments that survive the double digest to say where the second enzyme did not cut. Fragments that persist unchanged are usually more informative than the new ones.

Cloning, and what a colony actually tells you

The standard workflow is: cut vector and insert with the same enzyme so their sticky ends match, join them with DNA ligase, transform the mixture into competent E. coli, and plate on a selective medium. Two details decide most questions. The vector will happily re-circularise on itself, so it is usually treated with alkaline phosphatase to strip its 5′ phosphates — without them, ligase cannot seal the vector to itself, but it can still seal the insert in. And cutting with two different enzymes gives incompatible ends, which both prevents self-ligation and forces the insert in one orientation — "directional cloning".

Selection is not screening — and blue/white tells you less than you think

A selectable marker kills the cells that lack it: plate on ampicillin, and only cells carrying the plasmid grow. A screenable marker lets you look at survivors and tell them apart. Blue/white screening is the classic screen: the vector carries lacZα, a fragment of β-galactosidase that complements a defective enzyme in the host, and the multiple cloning site sits inside it. Plate on X-gal with IPTG (the gratuitous inducer of § 03) and an empty vector makes functional enzyme, cleaves X-gal and turns the colony blue; an insert interrupts lacZα, so complementation fails and the colony stays white. Now the exam point. Growth on ampicillin proves only that the cell took up a plasmid — not that it has an insert. A white colony proves only that something disrupted lacZα — not that it is the right insert, not that it is intact, and not that it is in the right orientation or reading frame. Those need a restriction digest, a PCR across the junction, or sequencing.

Exam technique · six filters that answer most laboratory stems without any biology

  1. "Which technique?" is decided by the molecule in the question, not by the disease. Is the gene there, deleted, rearranged, or how long is the repeat → Southern. Is it transcribed, in which tissue, and what size is the message → Northern. Is the protein there, at what size, modified → Western. Which protein binds this DNA → Southwestern. Read the stem for a noun, then answer.
  2. Recognition rule worth memorising outright: "mRNA is normal but the protein is absent." That sentence is the examiner pointing at translational or post-translational control (§ 07). Kill every option involving a Northern blot, promoter methylation or a transcription factor — all of them act before the message, and the message is fine.
  3. Two free kills on gel physics. Any option saying larger fragments migrate further is backwards — charge per base is constant, so mobility falls with size. And any option reading a plasmid's size off an uncut preparation is dead: supercoiled, linear and open-circular forms give three bands from one molecule and their apparent sizes are meaningless.
  4. Sum-check any mapping data before you think about positions. The fragments of every digest must add to the total. If they do not, either two bands co-migrate or you are looking at a partial digest — and either way, the option offering a tidy map is wrong.
  5. Count sites from fragments with the right rule. Circular → fragments = sites. Linear → fragments = sites + 1. The specific trap is a single full-length band from a circular plasmid: that is one cut, not zero, because uncut plasmid never runs as a clean linear band. Then confirm by addition — the double digest should give the sum of the two site counts.
  6. On cloning stems, downgrade every claim by one step. Growth on the antibiotic proves a plasmid got in, not that it has an insert. A white colony proves lacZα was disrupted, not that the insert is the right one, intact, or in the right orientation. Options that over-claim from a selection or a screen are wrong as written, and this is the single most reliable elimination in § 11.

PCR and sequencing, in the register the biology paper uses

The chemistry of PCR and of Sanger sequencing is covered in biochem 05 § 11, including the yield and qPCR arithmetic. What the biology paper asks is different, and usually reduces to what does the method require, and what does it therefore fail at?

  • PCR needs known sequence at both ends, because you must design two primers. It cannot amplify something you know nothing about, and it is why a completely novel insertion is easier to find by sequencing than by PCR.
  • PCR products are exponentially amplified, so contamination is catastrophic. A single stray molecule becomes a billion. This is why forensic and diagnostic PCR obsesses over negative controls.
  • Only the two original template strands ever give rise to products longer than the target. Those run-off products keep being made in every cycle, but only two new ones per cycle, so they grow linearly while everything else grows exponentially: after 30 cycles they are about 60 strands out of roughly 2 × 10⁹ — utterly negligible. The first strands of exactly the target length appear in cycle two, and the first double-stranded molecules of that length at the end of cycle three.
  • RT-PCR starts from RNA, using reverse transcriptase to make cDNA first. Because cDNA has no introns, a primer pair spanning an intron gives a shorter product from cDNA than from genomic DNA — which is the standard way to prove you have amplified message rather than contaminating genomic DNA.
  • Sanger sequencing reads a few hundred to about a thousand bases per reaction, accurately. Next-generation sequencing reads hundreds of millions of short fragments in parallel, cheaply, and then assembles or aligns them — which is why it is superb for resequencing a known genome and weaker at resolving long repeats.

CRISPR-Cas9

Bacteria and archaea keep a genetic memory of past infections: short pieces of invading DNA, called spacers, filed between repeats in a CRISPR array. The array is transcribed and processed into small crRNAs, each of which loads into a Cas nuclease and directs it to cut any DNA matching that spacer. The laboratory version fuses crRNA and its partner tracrRNA into one single guide RNA, whose first 20 nucleotides you choose.

Two mechanistic points carry almost all the exam weight. First, the PAM. Cas9 will not cut unless the target DNA carries a short motif — 5′-NGG-3′ for the common S. pyogenes enzyme — immediately 3′ of the 20 bp matched by the guide. The PAM is in the DNA and not in the guide RNA, and that is precisely how a bacterium avoids destroying its own CRISPR array: the array contains the spacer sequence but no adjacent PAM, so it is not a substrate. Second, what happens after the cut. Cas9 makes a blunt double-strand break about 3 bp 5′ of the PAM, and the cell repairs it. Non-homologous end joining is error-prone and leaves small insertions or deletions — usually a frameshift, hence a knockout, and it works in any phase of the cell cycle. Homology-directed repair can install a precise sequence, but it needs a donor template and is restricted to S and G2, which is why precise editing is far harder than knocking a gene out.

The variants, in one line each

Nickase (Cas9-D10A) — one nuclease domain inactivated, so it cuts one strand; paired nickases on opposite strands give a staggered break with far fewer off-target effects. dCas9 — both domains dead, so it binds without cutting; fuse a repressor domain for CRISPRi, an activator for CRISPRa, a fluorophore for imaging. Base editors — a cytidine or adenosine deaminase tethered to a nickase, converting C·G→T·A or A·T→G·C directly, with no double-strand break and no donor. Prime editing — a nickase fused to reverse transcriptase, guided by an extended pegRNA that carries the desired new sequence as a template. Cas13 — targets RNA, not DNA; note that Cas9 never does. The unifying idea: the guide RNA supplies the address and the fused protein supplies the action, so the platform is a general-purpose way of delivering any enzyme to any sequence.

Applications: transgenic organisms and gene therapy, in outline

Transgenic plants are usually made with the Ti plasmid of Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a soil bacterium that naturally transfers a segment called T-DNA into a plant cell and gets it integrated into the plant genome — a natural genetic engineer that was simply disarmed and reloaded. Monocots, which Agrobacterium infects poorly, are more often done by biolistics ("gene gun"). The standard examples: Bt crops carrying a cry gene from Bacillus thuringiensis, whose protein is toxic to specific insect larvae and requires the alkaline midgut of those insects to be activated; glyphosate-resistant crops carrying an insensitive EPSP synthase; and Golden Rice, engineered with a phytoene synthase and a bacterial carotene desaturase so that the endosperm accumulates β-carotene.

Recombinant proteins raise one exam-worthy point about host choice. E. coli is cheap and fast and makes human insulin and growth hormone perfectly well — but bacteria cannot glycosylate, cannot form many disulfide bonds correctly in the cytoplasm, and have no secretory pathway of the eukaryotic kind. So erythropoietin, monoclonal antibodies and clotting factors are made in mammalian cells (usually CHO). If a question asks why a protein is not made in bacteria, glycosylation is the first thing to check.

Gene therapy divides on two axes that questions exploit. Somatic versus germ-line: somatic therapy treats one patient and is not inherited; germ-line editing changes every cell including the gametes and is prohibited in essentially every jurisdiction. Ex vivo versus in vivo: ex vivo removes the patient's cells, modifies them in culture where you can check the result, and returns them (haematopoietic stem cells for ADA-SCID and for sickle cell disease; CAR-T cells for leukaemia); in vivo injects the vector into the patient (AAV carrying RPE65 into the retina for Leber congenital amaurosis; AAV9 carrying SMN1 for spinal muscular atrophy). The historical caution belongs with it: the early X-SCID trials cured the immunodeficiency and then produced leukaemia in several children, because the retroviral vector integrated near the LMO2 proto-oncogene and activated it — insertional mutagenesis, the same mechanism as a transposon landing in the wrong place (§ 10). And not every genetic therapy is gene transfer: nusinersen for spinal muscular atrophy is an antisense oligonucleotide that redirects the splicing of an existing gene, SMN2, to include exon 7 — a § 07 mechanism used as a drug.

Sentences that sound right and are not

"Restriction enzymes cut foreign DNA because they recognise it as foreign." They recognise a sequence; what makes the host's own copies safe is that a methylase has marked them. "A larger DNA fragment travels further through the gel." Backwards. "Growth on the antibiotic shows the cloning worked." It shows a plasmid got in. "cDNA and genomic DNA for the same gene are the same length." cDNA lacks the introns, which in a human gene are usually the bulk of it. "CRISPR can insert a new gene by itself." Cas9 cuts; the cell repairs. Without a donor template you get an indel, not an insertion. "Northern blotting measures protein." It measures RNA — and the commonest way this appears is a stem in which mRNA is normal and protein is absent, which is the whole point of the question. "PCR can amplify a gene you know nothing about." You need enough known sequence at both ends to design primers.
