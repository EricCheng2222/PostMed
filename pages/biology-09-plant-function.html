Photosynthesis, Plant Reproduction and Plant Responses

A plant is a stationary organism that has to make its own food, find a mate it cannot walk to, and survive a season it cannot walk away from. Every topic on this page is one of those three problems solved by the same underlying trick: light is used to run chemistry, and chemistry is used to run development. Photosynthesis converts photons into two portable currencies and spends them on air; reproduction converts the sporophyte's meiosis into a protected, dispersible package; and the hormones and photoreceptors convert an environmental measurement — the direction of the light, the length of the night, the pull of gravity — into differential growth. Learn the currencies and the measurements and most of the facts on this page stop needing to be memorised.

§ 01

The chloroplast and the two-stage logic

The ideaA leaf does two completely different jobs, in two different places inside the same organelle. The first job turns light into two portable chemicals: one that carries energy, one that carries electrons. The second job spends those two chemicals on turning air into sugar. Almost every confusion in this topic comes from mixing the two up, so the first habit worth building is to ask of any fact: which job, and which compartment?

Definition & key facts

Photosynthesis
The light-driven reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrate, using water as the electron donor. The oxygen released is a waste product of the donor, not of the carbon.

Three compartments, and why the third one is the point

A chloroplast has a double envelope, like a mitochondrion, and it also has something a mitochondrion does not: a third membrane system, the thylakoids, folded into stacks (grana) joined by unstacked stroma lamellae. That third membrane encloses a third compartment, the lumen. Protons are pumped from the stroma into the lumen, and the ATP synthase head faces the stroma, so ATP appears in the stroma — exactly where the Calvin cycle needs it.

This is the point of the whole architecture, and it is where the mitochondrial analogy has to be handled with care. Both organelles run chemiosmosis; but the chloroplast has evolved a proton reservoir that is topologically outside the cell's cytoplasm and that can be made genuinely acidic, and it puts the ATP where the biosynthesis is. Note also the direction: in a mitochondrion the matrix is the alkaline side and matrix ATP is exported; in a chloroplast the stroma is the alkaline side and stromal ATP is spent on the spot.

"Photosynthesis is just respiration run backwards"

The two summary equations are reverses of one another. Nothing else is. The pathways share no enzymes; the carriers are different (NADPH versus NADH, plastoquinone versus ubiquinone); the compartments differ; the electron flow in a chloroplast runs from a positive potential to a negative one, which is uphill and only possible because photons pay for it, while respiration runs downhill. A leaf cell does both at once, all day, which is why the number an experiment measures is usually net photosynthesis and why light-response curves start below zero.

Two derived quantities follow immediately and both are examined. The light compensation point is the irradiance at which gross photosynthesis exactly matches respiration, so net gas exchange is zero. The CO₂ compensation point (Γ) is the CO₂ concentration at which the same is true at a given light level — about 50 ppm in a C₃ plant and below 10 ppm in a C₄ plant, a difference that § 07 will explain entirely.

Exam technique · settle "which compartment?" before you read the options

  1. Label the stem in five seconds — photons, a wavelength, O₂ evolution, NADP⁺, plastoquinone, a proton gradient or an inhibitor means thylakoid membrane / lumen. CO₂, RuBP, 3-PGA, G3P, "ATP consumed", starch or sucrose means stroma. It is the cheapest filter on the page and it costs no thinking at all.
  2. Then run the three geography checks — protons are pumped into the lumen, never into the intermembrane space; the ATP synthase head faces the stroma, so ATP and NADPH both appear in the stroma; the alkaline side is the stroma (pH ≈ 8) and the acidic side is the lumen (pH ≈ 5). An option that breaks any one of the three is dead without further reading.
  3. Strike out anything wearing mitochondrial clothes — ubiquinone, cytochrome c, complex I/III/IV, "O₂ is the terminal acceptor", "the intermembrane space acidifies". The chloroplast equivalents are PQ, PC and cytochrome b₆f, and the terminal acceptor is NADP⁺. The examiner writes these options precisely because the two chains genuinely are analogous, so the swap reads as respectable prose.
  4. Two claims that are wrong every single time — "the O₂ comes from CO₂" (it comes from the hydrogen donor, i.e. water) and "photosynthesis is respiration run backwards" (only the two summary equations are reverses; no enzyme, carrier or compartment is shared). Either phrase in an option removes it.
  5. Where the time goes — compartment items are ten-second questions. Bank the time here and spend it on the § 04 arithmetic. And note that with five options and −0.25 marking, ruling out even one option makes answering strictly better than leaving it blank, so a half-recognised stem is still worth an answer.
§ 02

Catching the light: pigments, antennae and spectra

The ideaA pigment is simply a molecule that is coloured because it absorbs some wavelengths and not others. A leaf carries several of them, tuned to different parts of the spectrum, and they are arranged so that hundreds of them feed a single working molecule at the centre. What they pass along is not electrons and not light — it is excitation, handed from neighbour to neighbour like heat down a poker, until it reaches the one place where an electron can actually be knocked out.

Definition & key facts

Photosystem
A light-harvesting antenna of a few hundred pigment molecules plus a reaction-centre complex containing a special pair of chlorophyll a molecules and a primary electron acceptor. The antenna transfers energy; only the reaction centre transfers an electron.
absorption spectrum ≠ action spectrum what a pigment absorbs versus what actually drives the process — the classic distractor pair
Full definition, symbols and units
Absorption spectrum
Absorbance of a purified pigment plotted against wavelength. A property of a molecule in a cuvette.
Action spectrum
Rate of a biological process — usually O₂ evolution — plotted against wavelength. A property of the intact system.
Chlorophyll a / b
A chlorin ring chelating Mg²⁺, with a long hydrophobic phytol tail that anchors it in the membrane. Chlorophyll a has a methyl group where chlorophyll b has a formyl (—CHO); that one electron-withdrawing group shifts both absorption peaks.
Carotenoids
Forty-carbon isoprenoids: carotenes (pure hydrocarbons, e.g. β-carotene) and xanthophylls (oxygen-containing, e.g. lutein, zeaxanthin, violaxanthin). Accessory absorbers and photoprotectants.

The energy of a photon is inversely proportional to its wavelength, so blue photons carry more energy than red ones. That sounds as if blue light should be better, and it is not: a blue photon excites chlorophyll to a higher singlet state which decays to the same lowest excited state within picoseconds, wasting the excess as heat. Every absorbed photon does exactly the same amount of photochemical work regardless of its colour. A 680 nm photon carries 176 kJ·mol⁻¹ and a 430 nm photon 278 kJ·mol⁻¹; both deliver exactly one excited chlorophyll, so the two are equally efficient per photon — but red light is the more efficient per joule absorbed, because none of its energy is thrown away as heat on the way down to the lowest excited state.

Why the action spectrum is broader than chlorophyll a's absorption spectrum

If chlorophyll a were the only pigment, photosynthesis would run only in the narrow blue and red bands where it absorbs. It does not: the action spectrum has a filled-in shoulder through the blue-green and yellow-green, because chlorophyll b and the carotenoids absorb there and hand their excitation on to chlorophyll a. Engelmann's 1882 experiment showed this beautifully without any spectrophotometer at all: he laid a filamentous alga in a bacterial suspension, projected a spectrum across it, and let aerotactic bacteria report where oxygen was coming out. They crowded in the blue and the red — an action spectrum measured with living detectors.

The comparison that gets asked

Given two curves, decide which is which. An absorption spectrum is measured on an extract and tells you what a molecule can absorb. An action spectrum is measured on a living tissue and tells you what actually drives the process. They disagree in two informative places: the action spectrum is higher in the mid-spectrum, because accessory pigments funnel energy in, and it collapses above 680 nm even though chlorophyll still absorbs there. That collapse is the red drop, and it is the single best piece of evidence that there are two photosystems.

Robert Emerson pushed the red drop further. Far-red light beyond 680 nm alone gives a poor yield; light at around 650–680 nm alone gives a good one; but the two together give more oxygen than the sum of the two separately. That enhancement effect makes no sense for one photosystem and perfect sense for two working in series: far-red excites only photosystem I, which then runs out of electrons, while shorter red excites photosystem II, which supplies them. Supply both and the chain runs. This is a lovely example of an exam-friendly experiment where the reasoning, not the result, is what you are being asked for.

"Leaves are green because they reflect green light, so green light is useless"

Half right, and the half that is wrong matters. A leaf reflects only about 10–20 % of the green light falling on it and transmits a similar amount; it still absorbs roughly 70–80 %, because light bounces repeatedly between cells and air spaces and gets many chances to be caught. Worse for the simplification, green light is absorbed weakly per pass, so it penetrates deeper and drives photosynthesis in the lower mesophyll where red and blue have already been used up. In a dense canopy or a thick leaf, green photons can be the more productive ones at the margin. Leaves are green because chlorophyll happens to absorb poorly there — not because green light is worthless.

The antenna's second job is defensive, and it is the reason the carotenoids are not optional. If excitation arrives faster than the reaction centre can use it, excited chlorophyll lingers long enough to cross into a triplet state, which reacts with O₂ to make singlet oxygen — a reactive species that destroys the membrane it is made in. Carotenoids quench both triplet chlorophyll and singlet oxygen directly, and their conjugated double-bond systems are also the reason β-carotene is a dietary antioxidant and the precursor of retinal.

Go deeper · the xanthophyll cycle, non-photochemical quenching, and why a shaded plant burns in full sun

A leaf at midday absorbs several times more photons than it can use. The surplus has to go somewhere, and the safe route is heat. The switch is the xanthophyll cycle: when the lumen becomes strongly acidic — which happens precisely when electron flow is outrunning the Calvin cycle — a lumen-facing enzyme, violaxanthin de-epoxidase, converts violaxanthin → antheraxanthin → zeaxanthin. Zeaxanthin, together with the protonated PsbS protein, converts LHCII from an energy-transferring state into an energy-dissipating one. In the evening the reaction runs backwards (zeaxanthin epoxidase) and the antenna is re-armed for the next dim morning.

Two consequences worth carrying. First, this is why a plant grown in deep shade is scorched when moved into full sun: it has neither the xanthophyll pool nor the induced NPQ capacity, so the excess energy goes into singlet oxygen and photoinhibition instead of heat. Second, NPQ relaxes over minutes, which means a leaf in a fluctuating canopy spends a lot of time over-protected; engineering faster relaxation has produced measurable field yield gains, and it is one of the few genuine successes in improving photosynthesis by design.

The most damaged component under high light is the D1 protein of photosystem II. Plants deal with this by continuously degrading and replacing it — a repair cycle that runs at a rate of once every 20–30 minutes in bright light. Photosystem II is, in effect, a consumable.

Which pigment, which colour

A pigment's colour is the light it fails to absorb. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red, so it looks green. Carotenes absorb blue and blue-green, so they look orange. If a question tells you a pigment is yellow-orange, it has told you its absorption band is around 450 nm.

§ 03

The light reactions: two photosystems, the Z scheme, and where the oxygen comes from

The ideaThe whole light stage is one electron being moved from a place it does not want to leave to a place it does not want to go. Water holds its electrons tightly; NADP⁺ does not want them. Light is used twice, like two lifts in a building, to raise the electron high enough each time that it can then roll downhill through a chain of carriers — and it is that rolling downhill, not the light itself, that pumps the protons.

Definition & key facts

Linear (non-cyclic) electron flow
Electrons travel once, from water to NADP⁺, through both photosystems in series. Products: O₂, ATP and NADPH.
H₂O → PSII → PQ → cyt b₆f → PC → PSI → Fd → FNR → NADPH learn this string forwards and backwards; most light-reaction questions are a query on it
Full definition, symbols and units
Photosystem II (P680)
Absorbs maximally at 680 nm. Its oxidised special pair, P680⁺, is the strongest oxidant known in biology (E°′ ≈ +1.2 V) — strong enough to strip electrons from water (E°′ = +0.82 V). Sits mostly in the stacked grana.
Oxygen-evolving complex (OEC)
A Mn₄CaO₅ cluster on the lumenal face of PSII. Accumulates four oxidising equivalents, then splits two waters in one concerted step.
Photosystem I (P700)
Absorbs maximally at 700 nm. Excited P700* is the strongest reductant in biology (E°′ ≈ −1.3 V) — strong enough to reduce ferredoxin and hence NADP⁺. Sits mostly in the unstacked stroma lamellae, where NADP⁺ can reach it.

Where the oxygen comes from, and how we know

Until the 1930s the standard textbook account had the O₂ coming from CO₂ — which is chemically reasonable, since CO₂ is the molecule being reduced. Van Niel demolished it by generalisation rather than by experiment: purple sulfur bacteria photosynthesise using H₂S instead of water and deposit sulfur rather than releasing O₂. Write the general equation CO₂ + 2 H₂A → [CH₂O] + H₂O + 2 A and it is obvious that the by-product comes from the hydrogen donor, not from the carbon. Ruben and Kamen then confirmed it directly with H₂¹⁸O: the label appeared in the O₂. Supply C¹⁸O₂ instead and the label appears in the sugar and in the water — never in the gas.

The mechanism is worth one extra sentence because it explains an oddity. Splitting water yields four electrons but a photochemical reaction centre can only take one at a time. The OEC resolves this by acting as a capacitor: P680⁺ oxidises it once per photon, and it steps through five states (S₀ → S₄) before releasing O₂ in a single concerted event. This is why a dark-adapted chloroplast given single flashes of light evolves oxygen in a four-flash periodicity — a fingerprint result that a question may describe without naming.

Go deeper · the Kok cycle, the manganese cluster, and why this reaction has never been copied

Pierre Joliot and Bessel Kok flashed dark-adapted chloroplasts with microsecond pulses and counted the O₂ per flash. The yield peaked on flash 3, then 7, then 11 — a period of four, damping out over successive cycles. Four flashes, four oxidising equivalents, one O₂: the cluster stores charge. The first peak comes on flash 3 rather than 4 because the resting state in darkness is S₁, not S₀, and the damping comes from misses and double hits.

The catalytic core is Mn₄CaO₅ — four manganese ions, one calcium and five bridging oxygens, arranged as a distorted chair. The Ca²⁺ is not decorative: remove it and water oxidation stops. A tyrosine residue (YZ) shuttles each electron from the cluster to P680⁺.

Two payoffs. First, this reaction is the origin of every oxygen molecule you have ever breathed and of the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4 billion years ago — the largest chemical change in Earth's history, and the event that made aerobic respiration, and therefore animals, possible. Second, it is the hardest reaction in bioenergetics: a four-electron, four-proton oxidation of a very stable molecule at close to zero overpotential, performed by an earth-abundant metal cluster. Artificial water-splitting catalysts remain far behind it, which is why the OEC is the single most-studied active site in inorganic chemistry.

Four statements about the photosystems that look right and are not

"Photosystem I acts before photosystem II." The numbering is the order of discovery, not of function. Electrons go PSII → PSI. Every time.

"PSI contains chlorophyll a and PSII contains chlorophyll b." Both reaction centres are chlorophyll a. P680 and P700 differ because of the protein environment around them, not because of a different pigment. Chlorophyll b is an antenna pigment in both.

"The antenna pigments pass electrons to the reaction centre." They pass excitation energy, by resonance transfer, with no charge moving. The first and only electron transfer is from the special pair to the primary acceptor.

"Photosystem II produces NADPH." PSII produces O₂ and reduced plastoquinone. NADPH is made at the far end of PSI by ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase. A question that says "the complex that reduces NADP⁺" is pointing at FNR, downstream of PSI.

Which photosystem carries which number, and which one goes first

Two before one · six before seven

Ours, not a standard hook — but two short phrases fix the pair that gets swapped more than any other on this page. Two before one: the electron passes PSII first, PSI second, because the numbering records the order of discovery, not the order of use. (Water's own formula agrees — it is H₂O that feeds PSII.) Six before seven: walk that same journey in wavelengths and it reads P680 then P700, so the 6 travels with the II and the 7 with the I. Lay the two phrases side by side and the pairing is forced — and you can see why it is misread so often, since the Roman numeral and the wavelength run in opposite directions. It does not carry the three facts that hang off it, and you should hold those separately: both reaction centres are chlorophyll a (P680 and P700 differ by their protein environment, not their pigment); PSII sits mostly in the stacked grana and PSI in the unstacked stroma lamellae where NADP⁺ can reach it; and P680⁺ is biology's strongest oxidant while P700* is its strongest reductant — the two extremes sit at opposite ends of the same chain.

The carrier string — no mnemonic, and you do not need one

Do not encode H₂O → PSII → PQ → b₆f → PC → PSI → Fd → FNR → NADP⁺ as a word; rebuild it. The chloroplast chain is the mitochondrial chain with the prefix changed. Between the two photosystems sit exactly the three kinds of carrier that sit between complexes I and IV in a mitochondrion: a lipid-soluble quinone that ferries two electrons and two protons across the membrane (plastoquinone ↔ ubiquinone); a cytochrome complex running a Q cycle (b₆f ↔ complex III); and a small soluble one-electron carrier in the aqueous compartment (plastocyanin, a blue copper protein, ↔ cytochrome c). "Plasto-" is simply the plant badge on a familiar molecule. Only the two ends are new — water in front, and Fd → FNR → NADP⁺ behind — and both are stated by the Z scheme's redox axis, since every carrier in a downhill run must be a better oxidant than the one before it. Derive it that way and you get the string in both directions, plus the respiratory chain for free.

§ 04

Photophosphorylation: chemiosmosis in the thylakoid, and why cyclic flow exists

The ideaMoving electrons downhill is used to shove protons into a sealed sac. The sac fills up, and the pressure of protons trying to get out again is what turns the enzyme that makes ATP. The clever part is that the plant can choose: send the electron all the way to NADP⁺ and get both currencies, or send it round in a circle back into the chain and get only ATP. Which route it uses depends on which currency it is short of.

Definition & key facts

Photophosphorylation
ATP synthesis driven by a light-generated proton-motive force across the thylakoid membrane. Non-cyclic photophosphorylation accompanies linear flow; cyclic photophosphorylation uses photosystem I alone.

Same rotary machine as the mitochondrial enzyme. The chloroplast c-ring has 14 subunits, and one full revolution makes 3 ATP.

Why linear flow alone cannot balance the books

Count what four electrons deliver. They come from two waters, so 4 H⁺ are dumped straight into the lumen; passing through cytochrome b₆f with the Q cycle translocates 8 H⁺ more; total 12 H⁺. Those same four electrons reduce 2 NADP⁺. Now spend the protons: at 14 per revolution and 3 ATP per revolution, one ATP costs 4.67 H⁺, so 12 H⁺ buys 2.57 ATP. Linear flow therefore hands the stroma ATP and NADPH in a ratio of about 1.29, while the Calvin cycle wants 1.50. Every turn of the cycle, the chloroplast falls behind on ATP.

That gap is not a bookkeeping curiosity; it is the reason cyclic electron flow exists. Send some electrons from ferredoxin back into the plastoquinone pool and they pass through cytochrome b₆f again, pumping protons again, without ever reaching NADP⁺. Photosystem I runs alone, ATP is made, no NADPH is made and no water is split, so no oxygen is released. Cyclic flow is up-regulated exactly when you would predict: under high light, under drought when stomata close and the Calvin cycle stalls, in C₄ bundle-sheath cells that need extra ATP, and in guard cells. Some ancient photosynthetic bacteria have only one photosystem and therefore only cyclic flow — which is why they cannot use water as a donor and never made an oxygenated atmosphere.

Worked example 1 — the complete light-reaction ledger for one glucose

A leaf makes one molecule of glucose. Using 12 H⁺ per O₂ and a 14-subunit c-ring making 3 ATP per revolution, account for the photons, the water, the protons, the ATP and the NADPH — and work out how much cyclic flow is needed to close the ATP gap. The Calvin cycle requires 18 ATP and 12 NADPH per glucose (§ 05).

  1. Electrons. 12 NADPH require 2 electrons each = 24 e⁻.
  2. Water. Each water gives 2 electrons, so 24 / 2 = 12 H₂O are split, releasing 24 / 4 = 6 O₂ ✓ — which matches the overall equation's twelve waters in and six oxygens out.
  3. Photons. Every electron is lifted twice, once per photosystem: 24 × 2 = 48 photons. Equivalently 8 photons per CO₂ fixed.
  4. Protons. 12 H⁺ per O₂ × 6 O₂ = 72 H⁺ delivered to the lumen.
  5. ATP from linear flow. 72 ÷ (14/3) = 72 × 3/14 = 15.43 ATP. The cycle needs 18, so the shortfall is 18 − 15.43 = 2.57 ATP.
  6. How much cyclic flow? 2.57 ATP × 4.67 H⁺ = 12 H⁺, and each cyclic electron pumps 2 H⁺ at b₆f, so 6 cyclic electrons — each requiring one extra photon at photosystem I.
  7. Total photons. 48 + 6 = 54, i.e. 54/6 = 9 photons per O₂. The measured quantum requirement of intact leaves is 9–10 photons per O₂ — the model and the measurement agree, which is the best reason to trust the model.
  8. Efficiency check. 54 mol of 680 nm photons carry 54 × 176 = 9500 kJ; storing one mole of glucose costs about 2870 kJ, so the light-to-sugar efficiency is 2870/9500 = 30 %. Crop canopies achieve 1–2 % of total incident sunlight, because only ~45 % of sunlight is PAR and most of it is reflected, transmitted, or arrives when the leaf is already saturated.

The entire ledger of photosynthesis in four digits

Eight photons, then A-N-O: 3, 2, 1

Ours, not a standard hook. Per one CO₂ fixed: 8 photons in, and then the other three quantities in alphabetical order with their counts running down — 3 ATP spent, 2 NADPH spent, 1 O₂ out. The initials climb A → N → O while the numbers fall 3 → 2 → 1, so neither the order nor the digits can slip. Multiply by three for one export cycle (24 photons, 9 ATP, 6 NADPH, 3 O₂ → 1 G3P leaves) and by six for one glucose (48 photons, 18 ATP, 12 NADPH, 6 O₂, from 12 H₂O). Nearly every number in § 03–§ 05 is one of those four multiplied by 3 or by 6. Two limits it does not carry. The 8 is the theoretical minimum, from linear flow alone: because linear flow yields ATP : NADPH ≈ 1.29 against the Calvin cycle's demand of 1.50, cyclic flow has to top the ATP up, so the measured requirement is 9–10 photons per O₂ and a real glucose costs about 54. And the 3 ATP is the C₃ figure — a C₄ plant pays 5 (§ 07).

Inhibitor logic — a favourite two-mark item

Cut the chain in one place and predict what accumulates. DCMU (diuron, a herbicide) blocks the QB site of PSII: linear flow stops, O₂ evolution stops, NADPH stops — but cyclic flow around PSI still makes ATP. Paraquat steals electrons from PSI's iron–sulfur centres and hands them to O₂, generating superoxide; the plant dies of oxidative damage in the light and is unharmed in the dark, which is also the basis of its notorious human toxicity (lung concentration, redox cycling, no antidote). DNP or another uncoupler makes the thylakoid leaky to protons: electron transport actually speeds up, O₂ is still evolved, NADPH is still made, but ATP synthesis collapses. Match the symptom to the site and these questions become free.

Exam technique · light-reaction stoichiometry, as one fixed chain

  1. Recognise the type — the stem hands you a c-ring subunit count, an H⁺-per-O₂ figure, or asks for photons, waters, protons, ATP or NADPH. Nothing conceptual is being tested; every mark is in the unit chain, so do not re-read the physiology.
  2. Always start from electrons, always in the same direction — e⁻ = 2 × NADPH; H₂O = e⁻ ÷ 2; O₂ = e⁻ ÷ 4; photons = 2 × e⁻ (one lift at each photosystem); H⁺ into the lumen = 12 per O₂ (4 from water + 8 from the Q cycle); ATP = H⁺ ÷ (c/3), which is 4.67 H⁺ per ATP for a 14-subunit ring. Going the other way, invert each step. Write the chain down before you look at the options.
  3. One ratio checks the whole answer — linear flow alone must land at ATP : NADPH ≈ 1.29, below the 1.50 the Calvin cycle demands. If your working gives 1.5 or more from linear flow alone, you used the wrong c-ring or dropped the Q cycle. And any option asserting that linear flow exactly meets the Calvin demand is wrong by construction: it deletes the reason cyclic flow exists. That option is always present and always sounds the tidiest.
  4. Three option shapes that are always wrong here — "one H⁺ makes one ATP" (no rotary synthase works that way); "the photosystems pump the protons" (they do not — water splitting on the lumenal face and the b₆f Q cycle do); and any photon count below 8 per CO₂, which is a hard floor set by two lifts per electron and four electrons per O₂.
  5. Inhibitor stems answer themselves in ten seconds — find the cut, read off what stops. Cut before PSI (DCMU): O₂, NADPH and linear ATP stop, cyclic ATP survives. Cut at PSI's acceptor side (paraquat): electrons go to O₂, superoxide, damage in the light only. Cut the membrane (uncoupler): electron transport speeds up, O₂ and NADPH continue, ATP collapses. One of those three patterns is the answer nearly every time.
§ 05

The Calvin cycle: three phases and the arithmetic that goes with them

The ideaCarbon dioxide is not built into sugar directly; it is stuck onto a five-carbon molecule that is already there, and the enlarged molecule immediately breaks in half. That is why the cycle has to exist at all — the acceptor is consumed and must be rebuilt, and rebuilding it costs most of the effort. Once you see the cycle as "attach, reduce, rebuild the acceptor", the numbers stop being a list and become a consequence.

Definition & key facts

Calvin cycle
The stromal pathway that reduces CO₂ to carbohydrate using ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. Three phases: carbon fixation → reduction → regeneration of RuBP. The product exported is G3P, a three-carbon sugar phosphate — not glucose.

Worked example 2 — from three turns to one glucose, and an energy audit

(a) Verify the carbon and cofactor totals for one glucose. (b) Check whether the ATP and NADPH delivered actually contain enough free energy to make glucose, taking ΔG°′ for ATP hydrolysis as −30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ and for the oxidation of NADPH by O₂ as −220 kJ·mol⁻¹, with glucose synthesis from CO₂ and water costing +2870 kJ·mol⁻¹.

  1. (a) Turns. Glucose is 6 C and each turn fixes 1 C, so 6 turns.
  2. Cofactors. 6 × (3 ATP + 2 NADPH) = 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.
  3. Carbon check. 6 turns make 12 G3P = 36 C. Two G3P (6 C) leave and condense to one glucose; ten G3P (30 C) regenerate 30/5 = 6 RuBP, exactly the six consumed ✓.
  4. (b) Energy delivered. Per CO₂: 3(30.5) + 2(220) = 91.5 + 440 = 531.5 kJ. Per glucose: 6 × 531.5 = 3189 kJ.
  5. Efficiency of the spending step. 2870 / 3189 = 0.90 — the Calvin cycle converts about 90 % of the free energy handed to it into stored sugar. Losing only a tenth is extremely good; the losses are concentrated upstream, in the light reactions and in the antenna.
  6. Sanity check on the ratio. Notice that the NADPH, not the ATP, carries the energy: 440 of the 531.5 kJ. Reduction is expensive; phosphorylation is cheap. That is why the cycle needs a reductant at all and why a plant deprived of NADPH cannot substitute more ATP.

What happens to the G3P, and why the phosphate matters

G3P leaving the chloroplast goes out through the triose-phosphate translocator, a strict one-for-one antiporter that imports inorganic phosphate as it exports the sugar. In the cytosol two G3P are condensed into fructose-1,6-bisphosphate and on to sucrose, the transport sugar of the phloem (§ 09 of the previous note). G3P that stays behind is built into starch inside the stroma during the day and remobilised at night. The phosphate exchange is the hidden control: if sucrose synthesis in the cytosol stalls, phosphate stops coming back, the stroma runs out of Pi, ATP synthesis stops and photosynthesis is inhibited by its own unsold product. This is end-product inhibition, and it is why a plant in high CO₂ often shows a smaller yield gain than the gas exchange alone predicts.

"The dark reactions" — a name that teaches the wrong thing

The Calvin cycle is light-independent in the sense that no step absorbs a photon. It is not a dark reaction: put a leaf in the dark and the cycle stops within minutes even if ATP and NADPH are supplied, because light switches its enzymes on by three separate routes. (1) The ferredoxin–thioredoxin system: reduced ferredoxin from PSI reduces thioredoxin, which reduces regulatory disulfide bridges on GAPDH, FBPase, SBPase, phosphoribulokinase and rubisco activase, activating all of them — and only in the light. (2) Protons leaving the stroma raise its pH from ~7 to ~8, close to the optimum for RuBisCO. (3) Mg²⁺ moves from the lumen into the stroma to balance the proton flux, and RuBisCO requires Mg²⁺ for activity. Three light-dependent switches on a "light-independent" pathway. Shutting it down at night is not laziness — it prevents a futile cycle in which the plant would spend ATP fixing carbon it is simultaneously respiring.

Where does each cofactor go?

NADPH is spent once; ATP is spent twice. Reduction takes both; regeneration takes ATP only. Any answer offering equal numbers of ATP and NADPH per turn has forgotten the three ATP that rebuild RuBP.

§ 06

RuBisCO's double life: photorespiration and what it costs

The ideaThe enzyme that grabs carbon dioxide cannot reliably tell carbon dioxide from oxygen. When it grabs oxygen by mistake it produces a molecule the plant cannot use, and rescuing that molecule costs energy and throws away a quarter of the carbon it contains. The mistake gets commoner as it gets hotter and drier — precisely when a plant can least afford it. Everything in the next section is a workaround for this one flaw.

Definition & key facts

Why the mistake happens, and why it gets worse when it gets hot

Three separate effects push the balance toward oxygenation as temperature rises, and a question will usually want at least two of them:

  1. Gas solubility. Both gases dissolve less as water warms, but CO₂'s solubility falls faster. The dissolved [CO₂]/[O₂] ratio in the stroma therefore drops with temperature even before the plant does anything.
  2. The enzyme itself. Sc/o declines with temperature — RuBisCO becomes intrinsically worse at discriminating when it is warm, falling from roughly 90 at 25 °C to around 70 at 35 °C.
  3. Stomatal closure. Heat means water stress, water stress means closed stomata, and closed stomata mean CO₂ is consumed without replacement while photosynthetic O₂ accumulates. This is by far the largest of the three in a real field.

The deeper answer to "why does an essential enzyme have such a stupid side reaction?" is evolutionary. RuBisCO arose more than three billion years ago in an atmosphere rich in CO₂ and effectively free of O₂; there was no selection against an oxygenase activity that essentially never occurred. By the time oxygen accumulated, the enzyme was central to every autotroph on the planet and the chemistry was hard to fix: the reactive enediol intermediate is inherently open to attack by O₂, and across species there is a measured trade-off — the RuBisCOs with the highest specificity also have the lowest turnover numbers. There is no free lunch available, which is exactly why plants evolved plumbing solutions (§ 07) rather than a better enzyme.

Worked example 3 — how much carbon does a C₃ leaf actually lose?

(a) A well-watered leaf at 25 °C has stromal CO₂ ≈ 10 µM and O₂ ≈ 250 µM, with Sc/o = 90. What fraction of the carbon RuBisCO fixes is released again by photorespiration? (b) Repeat for a hot, water-stressed leaf at 35 °C with CO₂ down to 6 µM, O₂ up to 280 µM and Sc/o fallen to 70.

  1. (a) Partition. Vc/Vo = 90 × 10/250 = 3.6 — about 3.6 carboxylations for every oxygenation. Inverted: Vo/Vc = 0.278.
  2. Carbon lost per oxygenation. Two oxygenations make two 2-phosphoglycolates = 4 C; the salvage route returns one 3-PGA (3 C) and releases one CO₂. So one oxygenation costs 0.5 CO₂.
  3. Fraction lost. 0.5 × 0.278 = 0.139 → about 14 % of gross fixation is released again. Net carbon per carboxylation = 0.861.
  4. (b) Stressed leaf. Vc/Vo = 70 × 6/280 = 1.5, so Vo/Vc = 0.667 and the loss is 0.5 × 0.667 = 0.333 → 33 %.
  5. Read the result. The partition swung from 3.6 : 1 to 1.5 : 1 for a 10 °C rise plus partial stomatal closure, and the carbon penalty more than doubled. On top of the carbon there is an energy penalty — the salvage pathway consumes ATP and reductant and the released NH₃ must be re-assimilated by glutamine synthetase at further ATP cost — so the loss in growth terms is larger than the loss in carbon terms.
  6. Compare a C₄ bundle sheath. Concentrate the stressed leaf's CO₂ tenfold, from 6 µM to 60 µM, hold O₂ at 250 µM, and take a slightly poorer Sc/o of 80: Vc/Vo = 80 × 60/250 = 19.2 — a loss of only 0.5/19.2 = 2.6 %. That single number is the entire argument for § 07.
(a) Vc/Vo = 3.6 → ≈14 % lost · (b) Vc/Vo = 1.5 → ≈33 % lost · C₄ bundle sheath ≈2.6 %

Three numbers that separate the right answer from the near-miss

25 % is the fraction of the carbon entering the salvage pathway that is lost (1 CO₂ out of 4 C). ~14 % is the fraction of total fixation lost by a comfortable C₃ leaf. Vo/Vc on its own — 0.28 in that example — is neither of those, and a distractor quoting it without the factor of one-half is the commonest trap on this calculation. Always ask: a fraction of what?

The photorespiratory salvage route, in three letters

CPM out, then the same road home

Ours, not a standard hook. Chloroplast → Peroxisome → Mitochondrion — and then the identical route in reverse, peroxisome and chloroplast again. Three letters cover all five stops because the itinerary is a palindrome, and that is the whole device: the carbon leaves through the peroxisome, turns round in the mitochondrion, and comes home the same way it went. Three organelles, five stops — and the single irreversible loss, one CO₂ and one NH₃ released by glycine decarboxylase, happens at the M, the turning point in the middle. It encodes the itinerary, not the arithmetic. Hold those separately: two 2-phosphoglycolates (4 C) go in and one 3-PGA (3 C) comes back, so 25 % of the carbon entering salvage is lost — which is not the ≈14 % of total fixation a comfortable C₃ leaf loses. Nor does it say why a peroxisome is in the route at all: glycolate oxidase makes H₂O₂, and H₂O₂ needs catalase.

Exam technique · the Vc/Vo calculation, and "a fraction of what?"

  1. Recognise it instantly — a specificity factor S, two gas concentrations, and the word "fraction" or "percentage". There is exactly one equation: Vc/Vo = S × [CO₂]/[O₂]. Write it before you look at the options.
  2. Three moves, then stop — (1) compute Vc/Vo; (2) invert to Vo/Vc; (3) halve it, because it takes two oxygenations to lose one CO₂. The answer is 0.5 × Vo/Vc and nothing else.
  3. The planted distractor sits a factor of two away — after step (2), scan the options for a number equal to Vo/Vc itself. It will be there; it is the commonest slip on this calculation; and the answer you want is exactly half of it. A second planted option is 25 % — a genuine figure attached to the wrong denominator, being the loss from carbon entering the salvage pathway, not from total fixation.
  4. Cheap magnitude check — in the stroma [O₂] runs roughly 20–50× [CO₂], so Vc/Vo for a C₃ leaf lands in the low single digits (≈3.6 when comfortable, ≈1.5 when hot and stressed). Double digits means either you inverted the ratio or the stem described a C₄ bundle sheath (≈15–20), where the loss is a couple of per cent.
  5. If the stem says "warmer" or "drier", the direction is fixed — CO₂ solubility falls faster than O₂'s, Sc/o itself declines (≈90 at 25 °C → ≈70 at 35 °C), and stomata close. All three push toward oxygenation, so the loss rises. Any option in which warming reduces photorespiration is dead on sight, and one elimination is already enough to make answering better than a blank.
Go deeper · the three-organelle salvage route, and why photorespiration is not simply a defect

The route. In the chloroplast, a phosphatase converts 2-phosphoglycolate to glycolate, which is exported to the peroxisome. There glycolate oxidase makes glyoxylate — producing H₂O₂, which is why the reaction needs a peroxisome and its catalase — and a transaminase makes glycine. Two glycines enter the mitochondrion, where the glycine decarboxylase complex plus serine hydroxymethyltransferase convert them to one serine, releasing CO₂ and NH₃. Serine returns to the peroxisome, is transaminated to hydroxypyruvate and reduced to glycerate, and the glycerate goes back to the chloroplast where glycerate kinase spends 1 ATP to make 3-PGA. The ammonia is recaptured in the chloroplast by the GS/GOGAT cycle at the cost of another ATP and a reduced ferredoxin. Mesophyll cells of C₃ leaves are packed with peroxisomes pressed against chloroplasts for precisely this traffic.

Why it is not pure waste. Two arguments, both defensible and both worth knowing because textbook prose sometimes overstates the "wasteful" line. First, photoprotection: when CO₂ is scarce and light is plentiful, the Calvin cycle cannot consume ATP and NADPH fast enough, the electron chain over-reduces, and singlet oxygen destroys photosystem II. Photorespiration is an alternative electron and ATP sink that keeps the chain turning over; mutants blocked in the C2 cycle die in normal air unless grown in high CO₂, and are strikingly prone to photoinhibition. Second, the C2 cycle is deeply woven into nitrogen and one-carbon metabolism — glycine and serine production, and the folate-linked C1 pool, run through it.

Can it be engineered away? This is one of the most active areas in crop science. Synthetic "photorespiratory bypasses" that decarboxylate glycolate inside the chloroplast instead of routing it through three organelles have given yield increases above 20 % in field-grown tobacco. Direct attempts to install a better RuBisCO have largely failed, for the trade-off reason above.

Worked example 4 — what C₄ costs, and the condition under which it pays

(a) Compute the ATP and NADPH per glucose for a C₄ plant and express the surcharge over C₃. (b) At what carboxylation-to-oxygenation ratio does a C₃ leaf lose enough carbon that the C₄ surcharge is worth paying, on a purely carbon basis? Use the loss expression from § 06.

  1. (a) Regenerating PEP by PPDK converts ATP to AMP + PPi, i.e. 2 high-energy bonds per CO₂ shuttled. So C₄ costs 3 + 2 = 5 ATP per CO₂ and the NADPH requirement is unchanged at 2.
  2. Per glucose: C₃ = 6 × 3 = 18 ATP; C₄ = 6 × 5 = 30 ATP; both need 12 NADPH.
  3. Surcharge: (30 − 18)/18 = 0.667 → 67 % more ATP. Since ATP is the cheap currency (§ 05, worked example 2), this is much less painful than it sounds — it costs extra photons, not extra reductant, which is why C₄ plants need bright light.
  4. (b) A C₃ leaf's net carbon per carboxylation is 1 − 0.5(Vo/Vc). Its effective ATP cost per net CO₂ is therefore at least 3 / (1 − 0.5 Vo/Vc), ignoring the salvage pathway's own ATP.
  5. Set that equal to 5 and solve: 3/(1 − 0.5x) = 5 ⇒ 1 − 0.5x = 0.6 ⇒ x = 0.8, i.e. Vc/Vo = 1.25.
  6. Interpret it honestly. The break-even is at most Vc/Vo ≈ 1.25 on carbon alone; adding the salvage pathway's ATP and the nitrogen re-assimilation cost pushes it up toward the 1.5–2 range that the § 06 calculation gave for a hot, stressed leaf. That is why the observed C₃/C₄ crossover sits near 25–30 °C, why C₄ grasses dominate tropical savannas and C₃ grasses dominate cool temperate ones, and why in a cool wet spring a maize field is out-performed by wheat.

Four sentences about C₄ and CAM that sound like textbook prose and are false

"C₄ and CAM plants do not use RuBisCO / do not run the Calvin cycle." Both do. All the carbon that ends up in a C₄ or CAM plant's sugar passes through RuBisCO. PEP carboxylase is a delivery service, not a replacement.

"CAM plants photosynthesise at night." They carboxylate at night. The light reactions obviously cannot run in the dark, so the ATP and NADPH that reduce the released CO₂ are made the next day. What happens at night is PEPC + vacuolar acid storage.

"PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂." Its substrate is HCO₃⁻, which is exactly why it is blind to O₂ — there is no bicarbonate-shaped oxygen molecule for it to confuse. If a question asks why PEPC has no oxygenase activity, this is the answer.

"C₄ is simply better." C₄ costs 67 % more ATP per CO₂ and requires a whole extra layer of leaf anatomy. Under cool, moist, low-light conditions the photorespiratory penalty it avoids is smaller than the ATP it spends, and C₃ wins outright. C₄ evolved independently more than sixty times — but only in lineages exposed to heat, drought, salinity or low CO₂.

Diagnosing a plant from an experiment

Examiners love describing an unnamed plant and asking what it is. Titratable acidity high at dawn and low at dusk → CAM, unambiguously; that acid swing is the definitive assay. Net CO₂ uptake measured at night → CAM. CO₂ compensation point below 10 ppm, or photosynthesis that does not increase when ambient O₂ is lowered from 21 % to 2 % → C₄ or CAM, because there is no photorespiration to suppress; a C₃ plant's rate jumps 20–35 % under low O₂ (the Warburg effect in plants). Chloroplasts present in a ring of thick-walled cells around the vein → Kranz anatomy → C₄. And note that some species (Mesembryanthemum crystallinum) are facultative, switching from C₃ to CAM under salt or drought stress — so the same individual can give different answers in different weeks.

Exam technique · C₃ / C₄ / CAM — what to strike out before you start thinking

  1. No mnemonic is needed for the comparison table and you should not build one — it is one sentence unpacked. Both C₄ and CAM run the same two carboxylations; C₄ separates them in space, CAM in time. Every row follows: two cell types versus one, Kranz anatomy versus succulence, stomata open by day versus by night, an acid store needed only when the gap is twelve hours long.
  2. "True of C₄ but NOT of CAM" is always about geometry — PEP carboxylase first, bicarbonate as its substrate, a 4-carbon acid as first stable product, RuBisCO and the Calvin cycle doing the net fixation: all shared, all strikeable unread. The answer is whichever option names two cell types, mesophyll versus bundle sheath, Kranz anatomy, or plasmodesmata.
  3. Four sentences that read like textbook prose and are false — "C₄ and CAM plants do not use RuBisCO"; "CAM plants photosynthesise at night" (they carboxylate at night; the light reactions run the next day); "PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂" (its substrate is HCO₃⁻, which is exactly why it has no oxygenase activity); "C₄ is simply better". Each is lifted from prose that exists somewhere, which is why each looks respectable. Spotting one is a free elimination.
  4. Use the ATP counter as a numerical filter — 3 ATP per CO₂ in C₃, 5 in C₄ (the two extra are PPDK's single ATP → AMP + PPi, i.e. two high-energy bonds), and 2 NADPH in all three. Any option in which C₄ saves ATP, or in which the NADPH requirement changes, is dead arithmetic.
  5. Rank the diagnostic cues by what they can actually decide — titratable acidity high at dawn and low at dusk, or net CO₂ uptake at night → CAM specifically. Kranz anatomy → C₄ specifically. But Γ < 10 ppm, or a rate that does not rise when O₂ is dropped from 21 % to 2 %, distinguishes only C₄ or CAM from C₃ — if both C₄ and CAM are on offer you need one of the first two cues, and if only one of them is on offer you already have the answer.
  6. If the stem describes the weather, it has told you the answer — cool, moist, dim or CO₂-enriched favours C₃; hot, bright, moderately dry favours C₄; extreme drought favours CAM. The crossover sits near 25–30 °C, which is why wheat beats maize in a cold wet spring.
§ 08

Alternation of generations, and the trend across the land plants

The ideaAn animal is diploid and makes its sex cells by halving. A plant does something stranger: after halving, it grows a whole separate multicellular individual out of the halved cell, and that individual makes the sex cells — by ordinary cell division. So a plant's life cycle has two organisms in it, one with two sets of chromosomes and one with one set, and evolution has spent four hundred million years shrinking the second one and hiding it inside the first.

Why the sporophyte won

The trend is not arbitrary and a question may ask you to justify it. Three arguments, in rough order of importance. Diploidy masks deleterious recessives, so a diploid body can carry a larger mutational load and a bigger genome. A single fertilisation can be amplified indefinitely: a diploid sporophyte grows large, branches, and makes millions of spores from one zygote, whereas a gametophyte can only make gametes. And a reduced gametophyte can be protected — once the haploid generation is small enough to live inside the sporophyte's tissue, sexual reproduction no longer requires a wet surface for swimming sperm, which is the single biggest constraint on where a plant can live. Seeds and pollen are the end point of that logic, and they are why seed plants, not ferns, dominate dry land.

The error that costs more marks than any other in this topic

"Meiosis in a fern produces sperm and eggs." It does not, and it cannot: a fern's gametophyte is already haploid, and a haploid cell has no homologous pairs to segregate. Meiosis happens once per cycle, in the sporophyte, and its products are spores. Track it mechanically: sporophyte 2n → meiosis → spore n → mitosis → gametophyte n → mitosis → gamete n. The same trap appears dressed up as "the moss capsule contains gametes" (it contains spores), "the pollen grain is a gamete" (it is a three-celled male gametophyte that contains two gametes) and "the embryo sac is formed by meiosis" (meiosis makes the megaspore; three mitoses make the sac).

Ploidy in one line

SPOROphyte makes SPOREs by meiosis; GAMETOphyte makes GAMETEs by mitosis. The names tell you the products; you only have to remember which division goes with which — and it is the 2n one that halves.

§ 09

Flowers, pollination and double fertilisation

The ideaA flower is a short shoot whose leaves have been repurposed: two whorls to advertise and protect, two to make and receive the two kinds of spore. The delivery of pollen and the fusion of nuclei are separate events, often days apart, and confusing them is the standard error. The genuinely unusual step is that angiosperms fertilise twice — once to make an embryo, once to make its packed lunch — which means no food is stored unless an embryo actually exists.

From landing to fusion

A compatible grain on a receptive stigma hydrates and germinates a pollen tube, which grows down through the style — a tip-growing cell that can extend for tens of centimetres in maize. It is guided chemotropically, in the final stretch by LURE peptides secreted by the two synergids. The tube enters the ovule, usually through the micropyle, one synergid degenerates, and the tube discharges its two sperm. Note that the sperm are not motile in angiosperms: they have no flagella and are delivered like cargo. That, not the seed, is the innovation that freed seed plants from standing water.

Now the point of doing it twice. In a gymnosperm, the food store in the seed is the haploid female gametophyte, and it is laid down before fertilisation — so a pine that is pollinated but not fertilised has already spent the resources. In an angiosperm, the endosperm forms only when the second sperm fuses, so provisioning is strictly conditional on a successful fertilisation. No embryo, no packed lunch. That coupling, together with the 2 : 1 maternal : paternal dosage, is a large part of why angiosperm seeds are so efficient and why angiosperms out-competed gymnosperms across most of the world.

Where the standard summary is too tidy

"Endosperm is always triploid." In the Polygonum-type sac — around 70 % of angiosperms, and the one every textbook draws — yes. But other embryo-sac types exist: the Oenothera type has a 4-nucleate sac with a single polar nucleus and therefore diploid endosperm, and some types give 5n or higher. Say "triploid in the usual Polygonum-type sac" and you are right in every case.

"Double fertilisation is unique to angiosperms." Nearly. Ephedra and Gnetum (Gnetophyta) also fuse both sperm — but the second fusion yields a supernumerary embryo, not a nutritive triploid tissue. The safe phrasing is that endosperm-forming double fertilisation is unique to angiosperms.

"Pollination and fertilisation are the same event." They are separated by hours in Arabidopsis, by weeks in some orchids, and by roughly 13 months in pines. A plant can be pollinated and never fertilised — which is exactly what self-incompatibility exploits.

What is inside the embryo sac, and why the count comes out 7 and 8

E-SAC · 1, 2, 3, then one big cell holding 2

Seven cells, eight nuclei is the standard shorthand you will meet everywhere; E-SAC is ours, and it has two virtues — it counts itself, and it all but spells the structure it describes. Egg: 1. Synergids: 2, flanking the egg at the micropylar end. Antipodals: 3, at the far chalazal end. Central cell: 1 large cell filling the space between the two ends, carrying 2 polar nuclei. Cells: 1 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 7. Nuclei: 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 8. The 1-2-3 climb is the self-check — if your version does not climb, you have dropped a cell. Two things it deliberately leaves out. It is the Polygonum type only (≈70 % of angiosperms, and the one every diagram draws); other sac types give diploid or 5n endosperm, so the safe phrasing in an answer is "triploid in the usual Polygonum-type sac". And it says nothing about the male side: a pollen grain is 3 cells — one tube cell plus two sperm — and because the two sperm are mitotic sisters they are genetically identical, which is the fact the maize-cob question in § 10 turns on.

Go deeper · outbreeding devices and the two flavours of self-incompatibility

Selfing is safe and reliable but produces no new genotypes and exposes every deleterious recessive. Plants therefore stack mechanisms against it: dichogamy (anthers and stigma mature at different times — protandry in carrot, protogyny in avocado), herkogamy (spatial separation, taken to an extreme in heterostylous primroses with pin and thrum morphs), monoecy, dioecy (the ultimate solution, but it halves the pollen producers) and, most precisely, self-incompatibility — a genetic recognition system that rejects the plant's own pollen.

Gametophytic SI (Solanaceae, Rosaceae, Papaveraceae). The behaviour of a pollen grain is determined by its own haploid S genotype. If the pollen's S allele matches either allele of the pistil, the tube is arrested in the style — in the Solanaceae by an S-RNase that the pistil secretes into the tube and that degrades its RNA unless a matching F-box protein detoxifies it. So an S₁S₂ pistil rejects S₁ and S₂ pollen and accepts S₃; a cross S₁S₂ × S₁S₃ gives half the pollen functional.

Sporophytic SI (Brassicaceae, Asteraceae). The behaviour of a pollen grain is determined by the diploid genotype of the plant that made it, because the recognition ligand (SCR/SP11) is deposited on the pollen coat by the anther's tapetum. Rejection happens at the stigma surface, before the tube even penetrates, and dominance relationships between S alleles mean a grain can be rejected for an allele it does not itself carry. This is the system that makes hybrid Brassica seed production possible.

The exam-relevant contrast in one line: gametophytic = pollen's own genotype, rejection in the style; sporophytic = pollen parent's genotype, rejection on the stigma. And note the general point — this is a self/non-self recognition system with enormous allelic diversity maintained by negative frequency-dependent selection, structurally analogous to MHC polymorphism in vertebrates.

Worked example 5 — reading the genetics of a maize cob

In maize the allele Su (starchy endosperm) is dominant to su (sugary, wrinkled endosperm). A homozygous sugary plant (su/su) is used as the female parent and is pollinated by a heterozygous plant (Su/su). (a) What fraction of the kernels on the resulting cob have starchy endosperm? (b) Give the ploidy and genotype of the pericarp, the seed coat, the endosperm and the embryo of a starchy kernel. (c) Would you see any change on the cob if the cross had been made the other way round?

  1. Set up the maternal contribution. The mother is su/su, so every haploid cell she makes — including both polar nuclei and the egg — carries su.
  2. Set up the paternal contribution. A Su/su plant makes microspores by meiosis, so half carry Su and half su. Within one grain the two sperm are produced by mitosis and are therefore identical — a grain is either a "Su grain" or an "su grain", never a mixture.
  3. (a) A Su grain gives endosperm Su/su/su — one dominant allele is enough — so the kernel is starchy. An su grain gives su/su/su, sugary. Hence 1/2 of the kernels are starchy, 1/2 sugary, on one cob.
  4. (b) Pericarp: from the ovary wall → 2n, su/su, maternal. Seed coat: from the integuments → 2n, su/su, maternal. Endosperm: 3n, Su/su/su. Embryo: 2n, Su/su.
  5. Why this matters. The starchy phenotype you see on the cob is a property of the 3n endosperm, i.e. of the next generation, displayed on the mother's body in the same season. This is xenia, and it is possible only because of double fertilisation.
  6. (c) Reciprocal cross. Mother Su/su × father su/su: the eggs and polar nuclei now segregate, giving endosperms Su/Su/su (starchy) and su/su/su (sugary) — again 1/2 and 1/2. But because kernel appearance is endosperm and endosperm is part-paternal either way, the cob still shows segregation. What would not change is the pericarp: any character expressed by the fruit wall or the seed coat shows the mother's phenotype only and never segregates on a single cob — which is how you tell the two classes of trait apart.

Exam technique · ploidy and parentage of any seed or fruit tissue, in four questions

  1. There is no mnemonic for the ploidy table and you do not need one — every row falls out of a single question: which part of the flower was this? Ovary wall → pericarp. Integuments → seed coat. Nucellus → perisperm. Zygote → embryo. Central cell + second sperm → endosperm. Answer that and the ploidy and the parentage are both forced. The containment even maps across: the ovule sat inside the ovary, so the seed sits inside the fruit; the integuments wrapped the ovule, so the coat wraps the seed.
  2. Sort the tissues into two piles before you read the options — the mother's own body (pericarp, seed coat, perisperm: 2n, maternal genotype only, and therefore identical in every seed of one plant) versus the new generation (embryo 2n, one allele from each parent; endosperm 3n, two maternal to one paternal). Almost every option set on this topic is built by swapping the piles.
  3. Two option shapes are dead on sight — a seed coat or pericarp carrying a paternal allele, and an endosperm genotype written with two alleles instead of three. Either kills the option before you do any genetics at all, and one kill is already worth answering under negative marking.
  4. The two sperm in one grain are mitotic sisters, hence identical, so a kernel is wholly Su or wholly su, never a mixture. A 3 : 1 ratio on a single cob therefore needs both parents heterozygous — if the stem hands you a homozygous mother, any 3/4 option is wrong without further thought.
  5. Check which structure the question actually asked about — "the kernel looks starchy" is a statement about the 3n endosperm (this is xenia), not about the embryo and not about the coat. The distractors here are true sentences about the wrong tissue, which is the hardest kind to catch when you are reading fast.
  6. If the stem says pine, spruce or cycad, change tables — a gymnosperm's food store is the haploid female gametophyte, maternal, and laid down before fertilisation. No endosperm, no 3n tissue, and no "wasted provisioning is impossible" argument.

Germination: three things must happen in order

First imbibition — dry seed tissue draws water in with enormous force, rehydrating enzymes and rupturing the coat. Then mobilisation of reserves: in a cereal grain the embryo releases gibberellin, which diffuses to the aleurone layer and induces synthesis of α-amylase and other hydrolases that digest the starchy endosperm to sugars, absorbed through the scutellum. (This is the assay that made GA famous, and it is also why barley is malted.) Then growth: the radicle emerges first — it anchors the seedling and reaches water before the shoot commits — followed by the shoot, protected by a hypocotyl hook in eudicots or a coleoptile in grasses.

Epigeal or hypogeal — and which axis does the lifting

Epi-geal lifts with the hypo-cotyl · the prefixes always cross

Ours, not a standard hook, but it fixes a pair whose prefixes are deliberately crossed. Epigeal = cotyledons carried above ground — and the organ that does the carrying is the hypocotyl, arching into a hook (bean, castor). Now apply the second half and the rest is forced: hypogeal = cotyledons stay below, so the organ that elongates must be the epicotyl (pea, broad bean, maize). Because the crossing is total, holding one pairing in your head hands you the other, and there is no third pattern to confuse either with. The -geal half needs no hook whatever: epi = upon, hypo = under, exactly as in epidermis and hypodermis, and -geal is the ground. Only the crossing is arbitrary. Note also what the line does not say — the radicle emerges first in both patterns, always, and that is never the discriminator.

Dormancy is a decision, not a failure

A dormant seed is viable but refuses to germinate, and the refusal is under hormonal control: ABA maintains dormancy, GA breaks it, and it is the ABA : GA ratio, not either alone, that decides. The point is timing — germinating in autumn, or inside the fruit on the parent plant, is usually fatal. Maize viviparous mutants defective in ABA synthesis or perception germinate on the cob, which is the cleanest demonstration of ABA's role. Dormancy is broken by conditions that predict a good season: prolonged cold (stratification) for temperate species, abrasion or digestion of a hard coat (scarification), leaching of inhibitors by rain in desert annuals, smoke chemicals after fire, or — in small, light-requiring seeds — a flash of red light telling them they are not buried (§ 11).

Reproduction without sex

A plant is modular and its cells are totipotent, so a fragment can regrow an entire organism. Natural vegetative propagation runs through modified stems and roots: rhizomes (ginger, bamboo), stolons/runners (strawberry), stem tubers (potato — the "eyes" are axillary buds, which is why a potato is a stem and not a root), bulbs (onion, tulip), corms (crocus), root suckers (aspen clones covering hectares), and plantlets budded from leaf margins (Kalanchoe). Apomixis is the odd one out: seeds produced without fertilisation, so the offspring are clones delivered in a sexual-looking package — dandelions, many blackberries, some citrus.

Horticulture exploits the same totipotency. Cuttings form adventitious roots, which is why rooting powders contain a synthetic auxin (IBA or NAA). Grafting joins a scion — the shoot whose fruit you want — to a rootstock chosen for vigour, dwarfing or disease resistance; note that the fruit has the scion's genotype, so a grafted tree does not produce hybrid apples. And in tissue culture the ratio of two hormones directs the outcome: high cytokinin : auxin gives shoots, high auxin : cytokinin gives roots, and a balanced ratio gives undifferentiated callus — Skoog and Miller's result, and the foundation of the entire micropropagation and transgenic-plant industry.

The price of a clone

Asexual propagation preserves a favourable genotype exactly, needs no pollinator, and is fast. It also produces a population with essentially zero genetic variation, so one adapted pathogen can take all of it. The Irish potato famine (1845, Phytophthora infestans on a narrow clonal base), the destruction of the 'Gros Michel' banana by Panama disease and the current threat of TR4 to its clonal replacement 'Cavendish' are the same story three times. When a question asks for a disadvantage of vegetative propagation, this — not "it is slower" or "it needs more energy", both false — is the answer.

Hormones, tropisms and photoperiodism: how a plant that cannot move responds

The ideaA plant answers its environment by growing differently on one side than the other, or by growing at a different time. Both are done with a small set of chemical messengers, and the striking thing is how few there are: five classical hormones produce most of what a plant does. The same molecule can promote or inhibit the same process depending on concentration, on tissue, and on what other hormone is present — which is precisely what exam questions probe.

Three hormone statements that are in the textbooks and are wrong

"Abscisic acid causes abscission." It was named in the 1960s by workers who thought so; in most species leaf and fruit drop are triggered by ethylene as auxin from the blade declines. ABA's real signature is stomatal closure and dormancy. The name is a fossil of a discarded hypothesis, and a distractor built on it looks completely respectable.

"Auxin promotes growth." Only within a window. The dose–response curve is a bell, and its peak sits about a thousand-fold lower in roots (~10⁻⁸ M) than in shoots (~10⁻⁵ M). A concentration that makes a stem elongate strongly inhibits a root. Every gravitropism question turns on this.

"Cytokinin is made in the shoot tip." The shoot tip is the auxin source; cytokinin comes chiefly from root tips and travels up. That asymmetry is the basis of the shoot : root ratio — damage the roots and cytokinin supply falls, so shoot growth slows until the root system recovers.

Recognise all of them on the page; the paper you sit is set in English.

Auxin, in enough detail to answer the questions

Auxin's transport is unlike anything else in the plant: it is polar. IAA is a weak acid, so in the acidic apoplast a fraction is protonated and uncharged, and crosses the membrane freely; inside the neutral cytosol it dissociates and is trapped as the anion, and it can only leave through a PIN efflux carrier — and PIN proteins sit at the basal end of each cell. The result is a one-way, cell-to-cell stream moving away from the shoot tip at about 1 cm·h⁻¹. Relocate the PINs and you relocate the auxin, which is exactly what happens in a tropism.

What auxin then does to a cell is the acid growth mechanism, and it explains the timing. Auxin activates the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase; protons pumped into the wall drop its pH toward 4.5–5; that pH activates expansins, which let cellulose microfibrils slip past one another; and turgor pressure then does the actual work of expansion. Elongation begins within 10–15 minutes — far too fast for new gene expression, which is why a purely transcriptional model cannot be the whole story. A slower transcriptional arm exists as well: auxin binds the TIR1/AFB receptor, which is an F-box protein, and the auxin molecule acts as "molecular glue" that makes TIR1 grip the Aux/IAA repressors so they are ubiquitinated and destroyed, freeing ARF transcription factors. Note the shape of that mechanism — a hormone acting by triggering targeted protein destruction rather than by switching a receptor kinase on.

Phototropism, gravitropism, thigmotropism

Phototropism. The classical experiments are worth knowing as a chain of eliminations, because that is how they get examined. Darwin and his son showed that a covered tip abolished bending while a covered base did not — the tip perceives, the base responds. Boysen-Jensen showed that a permeable gelatin block inserted under the severed tip preserved the response while an impermeable mica sheet in the same place abolished it — so the signal is a diffusible chemical, not an electrical or mechanical one. He then located that chemical: a mica sheet slipped into the shaded flank blocked the bending, whereas the same sheet in the lit flank did not, so whatever moves down does so on the shaded side. Went collected the chemical into agar, applied the block off-centre in the dark, obtained bending without any light at all, and named it auxin. The Cholodny–Went hypothesis is the synthesis: light causes auxin to redistribute laterally to the shaded side, that side elongates faster, and the organ bends toward the light.

Two refinements matter. The photoreceptor is phototropin (phot1/phot2), a blue-light receptor with a flavin chromophore — so the action spectrum for phototropism peaks near 450 nm with a UV-A shoulder and red light does not work at all. If a question gives you an action spectrum with blue peaks and no red peak, it is telling you the receptor is phototropin or cryptochrome, not phytochrome. And the lateral gradient is now known to be produced largely by relocalisation of PIN3 to the shaded flank rather than by destruction of auxin on the lit side — mechanism, not mystery.

Gravitropism. The sensor is a sedimenting particle: statoliths are dense, starch-filled amyloplasts that fall to the physical bottom of specialised cells — the columella cells of the root cap, and the starch-sheath endodermis of the shoot. Their settling repositions PIN carriers, auxin accumulates on the lower flank, and growth becomes asymmetric. Starchless mutants respond poorly, which is the cleanest evidence that the amyloplasts really are the sensor.

One gradient, two opposite bends — the reasoning the examiner wants

Lay a seedling on its side. Auxin accumulates on the lower side of both organs — the redistribution is the same. In the shoot, the raised concentration is still below the shoot's optimum, so the lower side elongates faster and the shoot curves upward (negative gravitropism). In the root, whose optimum is roughly a thousand times lower, the same raised concentration is past the peak of the bell, so the lower side elongates more slowly and the root curves downward (positive gravitropism). One signal, one direction of movement, two outcomes, entirely because of sensitivity. Distractors that claim auxin "moves upward in roots", or that roots use a different hormone, are testing whether you know that a dose–response curve can have a maximum.

Thigmotropism is directional growth in response to touch: a pea tendril contacts a support, the contacted side grows more slowly and the free side faster, and the tendril coils. Distinguish it from thigmonasty, which is not growth at all — Mimosa pudica folds its leaflets in one to two seconds by driving K⁺ and water out of motor cells in the pulvinus, propagated by an action potential, and re-inflates over minutes. A Venus flytrap requires two trigger-hair stimuli within about 20–35 seconds before it snaps, which is a genuine short-term memory implemented as a decaying calcium signal, and which stops it from wasting a closure on a raindrop. And thigmomorphogenesis is the chronic version: repeatedly brushed or wind-shaken plants grow shorter and thicker, which is why a greenhouse seedling flops over when first put outside.

Photoperiodism: the plant is timing the night

Flowering, tuber formation, bud dormancy and leaf fall are all seasonal, and day length is the only environmental cue that is perfectly reliable year on year — temperature is not. The classic finding, and the one every question is built on, is that a plant does not measure the day. Interrupt the day with a period of darkness and nothing changes. Interrupt the night with a flash of light lasting a minute and the plant behaves as though the night were only as long as its longest unbroken stretch. So:

  • Short-day plants are really long-night plants: they flower when the uninterrupted night is longer than a critical value. Chrysanthemum, poinsettia, soybean, cocklebur, tobacco. They flower in late summer, autumn or winter.
  • Long-day plants are really short-night plants: they flower when the night is shorter than a critical value. Spinach, lettuce, radish, barley, iris, Arabidopsis. They flower in late spring and early summer.
  • Day-neutral plants flower on their own schedule: tomato, cucumber, dandelion, many rices.

The critical value is a threshold, and it differs between species — which is why a 14-hour night can be "long" for one plant and "short" for another, and why the labels short-day and long-day tell you nothing about the absolute number of hours. The light-flash experiment also identifies the receptor: a brief pulse of red light breaks the night, and an immediately following pulse of far-red cancels the break. That signature — red effect, reversed by far-red — is phytochrome, and nothing else.

Worked example 6 — five regimes, two plants, one rule

Plant A is a short-day plant with a critical night length of 9 h (it flowers when the uninterrupted night is at least 9 h). Plant B is a long-day plant with a critical night length of 11 h (it flowers when the uninterrupted night is under 11 h). Predict flowering under: (i) 16 L / 8 D; (ii) 8 L / 16 D; (iii) 8 L / 16 D with a 1-minute red flash 8 h into the dark; (iv) as (iii) but with far-red immediately after the red; (v) 14 L / 10 D. What single regime flowers both?

  1. State the rule first. Only the longest uninterrupted dark period counts. Compute it for each regime, then compare with each threshold.
  2. (i) Longest dark = 8 h. A needs ≥ 9 → no. B needs < 11 → flowers.
  3. (ii) Longest dark = 16 h. A → flowers. B → no.
  4. (iii) The flash splits 16 h into two stretches of about 8 h each, so the longest is 8 h — identical to regime (i). A → no; B → flowers. The plant's response has been reversed by sixty seconds of light in the middle of the night.
  5. (iv) Far-red immediately after red returns phytochrome from Pfr to Pr, so the plant never registers the break: effectively one unbroken 16 h night. Back to regime (ii) — A → flowers; B → no. (Reverse the order, giving far-red then red, and the break is restored: the last flash wins.)
  6. (v) Longest dark = 10 h. 10 ≥ 9 so A flowers; 10 < 11 so B flowers. Both.
  7. The general condition. Both flower whenever the night length t satisfies 9 ≤ t < 11 — a two-hour window. If A's threshold had exceeded B's there would be no such window at all, which is the point: the two categories are thresholds on the same axis, not opposites.

Exam technique · night-break regimes in twenty seconds

  1. Throw the light away first — rewrite every regime as one number, the longest uninterrupted dark stretch. Total darkness does not matter, total light does not matter, and the day length quoted in the stem is decoration. Nothing else in the question is doing any work.
  2. Apply the flash rules to that one number — a red flash splits the night, so take the longer half. Red immediately followed by far-red is no split at all. Far-red then red is a split. Only the last flash counts, because only the last flash decides how much Pfr is left.
  3. Then two comparisons, no biology — a short-day (really long-night) plant flowers when your number is ≥ its critical night length; a long-day (really short-night) plant flowers when it is < its critical night length. Day-neutral flowers regardless. Write the two inequalities and read the answer off.
  4. "Which single regime flowers both?" — you are looking for a night t with SDP critical ≤ t < LDP critical. The window exists only when the SDP's threshold is the smaller of the two, and any regime inside it works. This is a two-inequality question wearing a physiology costume.
  5. Kill on sight — any option whose reasoning invokes day length; any option that treats short-day and long-day as opposites (they are two thresholds on one axis, which is precisely why one regime can satisfy both); and any option in which far-red creates a night break rather than cancelling one.
  6. Recognition rule for the receptor — a red effect reversed by far-red is phytochrome and nothing else, so the stem is about flowering, germination of small seeds, de-etiolation or shade avoidance. An action spectrum peaking near 450 nm with no red peak is phototropin or cryptochrome instead, so the stem is about phototropism, chloroplast movement or blue-light stomatal opening — a different half of the syllabus, and you can stop reading the flowering options.

What phytochrome is actually for, most of the time

Flowering is the textbook use, but the everyday one is shade avoidance, and it follows directly from the physics. A leaf absorbs red strongly and transmits far-red, so light that has passed through a canopy is far-red enriched: R : FR falls from about 1.15 in the open to 0.1–0.2 under leaves. Low R : FR means little Pfr, and little Pfr means "you are being shaded". The response is a coherent syndrome — rapid internode elongation, upward-angled leaves, reduced branching, earlier flowering: an all-in gamble on outgrowing the competition. Sow a crop too densely and you get exactly this, tall thin stems that lodge, which is why plant density is an agronomic decision and not just an arithmetic one.

Phytochrome also controls de-etiolation. A seedling in darkness follows the etiolated programme: a long pale hypocotyl, an apical hook, tiny unexpanded leaves, no chlorophyll — everything invested in reaching light. The first red light converts Pr to Pfr and switches the programme: elongation stops, the hook opens, leaves expand, chlorophyll synthesis begins. And in small light-requiring seeds such as lettuce, a flash of red promotes germination and a following flash of far-red abolishes it, through as many alternations as you care to run — the definitive photoreversibility experiment, and a sensible adaptation, since a small seed with tiny reserves must not germinate under soil or under a canopy.

Which phytochrome form does the work, and which flash counts

Pfr = the Functional form · the last flash wins

The "f for functional" tag is the usual student hook rather than anything official, and it is worth the shelf space because which of two interconverting forms is the active one is the single genuinely arbitrary fact in this topic. Everything else derives: each form is named for the light it absorbs, and absorbing that light converts it into the other. So Pr absorbs red (660 nm) and becomes Pfr; Pfr absorbs far-red (730 nm) and becomes Pr. Run any sequence of flashes you like and only the final one decides the state — which is exactly why R then FR cancels a night break and FR then R restores it. Carry the two consequences alongside it. Daylight is red-rich and canopy shade is far-red-rich (R : FR falls from ≈1.15 in the open to ≈0.1–0.2 under leaves), so little Pfr means "I am being shaded" and triggers the shade-avoidance syndrome. And Pfr also reverts slowly in darkness and is degraded — which is part of what a long night gives a plant time to register.

Go deeper · from a measured night to a flower — florigen, the clock, and vernalization

Where it is measured and where it acts. The photoperiod is perceived by the leaf; the flower forms at the shoot apex. Graft a single induced leaf onto a non-induced plant and the recipient flowers — so a mobile signal must exist. It was named florigen in 1937 and remained hypothetical for seventy years. It is the FT protein, made in leaf phloem companion cells, moved through the phloem to the apex, where it binds the transcription factor FD and switches on the floral meristem identity genes AP1 and SOC1.

How day length is actually measured. Not with an hourglass but with a clock. The circadian oscillator makes CONSTANS (CO) mRNA peak in the late afternoon and evening. CO protein is unstable in darkness and stabilised by light acting through phytochrome A and cryptochrome. Under long days the light is still on when CO mRNA peaks, so CO protein accumulates and induces FT; under short days the peak falls after dusk and the protein is destroyed before it can act. This is the external coincidence model — the plant asks whether light coincides with a particular internal phase. It explains why the response is to the unbroken night (a light flash resets phase) and why absolute hours are not what matters.

Vernalization is a different thing. Some plants — winter wheat, biennials such as henbane — require weeks of cold before they will flower at all. Cold does not cause flowering; it confers competence to respond to the photoperiod later. Mechanistically it is epigenetic: prolonged cold recruits Polycomb complexes to the floral repressor FLC, depositing H3K27me3 and silencing it stably through subsequent mitoses — a genuine cellular memory of winter, reset in the next generation. Distinguish it cleanly from photoperiodism in an exam: vernalization is a cold requirement acting on the shoot apex over weeks; photoperiodism is a light measurement made in the leaf every single night.

One more distinction worth having: phytochrome is the red/far-red receptor; cryptochrome and phototropin are the blue-light receptors (clock entrainment and de-etiolation for the first; phototropism, chloroplast movement and blue-light stomatal opening for the second); and UVR8 perceives UV-B. If an action spectrum peaks at 660 nm and reverses at 730 nm it is phytochrome; if it peaks at 450 nm it is not.

Senescence, abscission and dormancy: shutting down on purpose

Autumn colour is a good test of whether you understand senescence, because it contains two different phenomena that a distractor will merge. Leaf senescence is an ordered, programmed process of nutrient recovery: chlorophyll is degraded first and its nitrogen exported, along with most of the leaf's protein, to stems and roots. Carotenoids — the yellows and oranges — were present all along and are simply unmasked as the green disappears. Anthocyanins — the reds and purples — are the opposite: they are newly synthesised in the vacuole during senescence itself, probably as a sunscreen protecting the recovery machinery from photo-oxidation on cold bright days. "Autumn reds are revealed when chlorophyll breaks down" is a sentence you will meet, and it is wrong about the reds.

Abscission then removes the emptied organ. An abscission zone at the petiole base contains a separation layer, whose cell walls are digested by cellulase and polygalacturonase, and a protective layer below it that is suberised to seal the scar. The control is a ratio: a healthy blade exports auxin across the zone and the leaf stays on; as the blade senesces, auxin flux falls, the zone becomes sensitive to ethylene, and the enzymes are induced. This is why spraying auxin delays fruit drop commercially and why ethylene or ethephon is used to make a whole orchard drop at once for mechanical harvest.

Dormancy is the winter version of the same decision, and it is set by the same two signals that set flowering: shortening days and falling temperature. ABA rises, GA falls, apical meristems stop dividing and are covered by bud scales, and the tissue undergoes cold acclimation — membrane lipids become more unsaturated to stay fluid, and compatible solutes and dehydrin proteins accumulate to protect against freeze-induced dehydration. Release requires a species-specific chilling requirement, a number of hours below about 7 °C, which is why fruit trees fail to break bud evenly in a warm winter and why the chilling requirement of an orchard variety is now a climate risk factor as much as an agronomic one.

Auxin and cytokinin as a pair

Auxin comes down from the shoot tip and suppresses the buds below it; cytokinin comes up from the roots and wakes them. Cut off the tip and auxin stops arriving, so the laterals break out — which is exactly what pruning a hedge does, and why it makes the hedge bushier.
