Momentum, Impulse and Collisions

This whole chapter is Newton's second law integrated once. Integrate F = dp/dt over time and you get impulse and momentum; integrate it over distance and you get work and energy. Add Newton's third law — which makes every internal force cancel against its partner — and conservation of momentum, the centre-of-mass theorem, the airbag and the ballistic pendulum all fall out as consequences rather than as separate facts to memorise.

§ 01

Momentum: the vector that survives

The ideaEverything that moves carries a certain amount of motion, and how much it carries depends on two things: how heavy it is and how fast it is going — plus, crucially, which way it is going. That directional quantity has a remarkable property: when things crash into each other, the total is the same afterwards as it was before, so you can work out what happened without knowing anything about the violence in between. It is not the same currency as energy, and confusing the two is the most expensive habit in this chapter: a rifle and its bullet carry equal and opposite amounts of motion, and only one of them is dangerous.

Definition & formula

Linear momentum
The product of a body's mass and its velocity — a vector quantity that measures "how much motion" the body carries, pointing along the velocity.
p = mv definition; valid always (non-relativistic)

Total momentum of a system

Notation on this page: bold symbols are vectors (p, v, F); plain symbols are magnitudes or signed one-dimensional components. In every 1-D problem, pick a positive direction on the page before you write anything down and never change it.

Go deeper · why Newton wrote F = dp/dt, and where F = ma stops working

Newton did not write F = ma. He wrote that the change of motion is proportional to the impressed force — that is, F = dp/dt. The two agree whenever the mass is constant, but the momentum form is the general one. It is what you reach for with a rocket burning fuel or a raindrop growing as it falls — provided you draw the system to include every piece of matter involved, exhaust and captured droplets alike, so that no mass crosses the boundary; naively differentiating mv for a body that is gaining or shedding mass is a classic way to get a wrong answer. And the same equation survives into relativity untouched, with p = γmv in place of mv. Treat F = ma as the special case, not the definition.

The practical reason momentum earns a chapter of its own is that it lets you skip the force entirely. A car crash lasts about 100 ms and the force during it is a complicated, unknowable function of time. You will never integrate it. But you can measure the velocities before and after, and momentum conservation ties them together without your ever knowing what happened in between. Momentum is the currency of "before and after".

Momentum and kinetic energy are not interchangeable

Both are made of the same two ingredients, yet they behave completely differently — this is the single most productive comparison in the chapter.

The comparison that gets examined

Two bodies with the same kinetic energy, one four times heavier: since p = √(2mK), the heavy one carries twice the momentum. Two bodies with the same momentum, one four times heavier: since K = p²/(2m), the light one carries four times the kinetic energy. This is exactly why a rifle recoils gently while its bullet is lethal — equal and opposite momenta, wildly unequal energies.

Sign trap — the one that costs the most marks

Momentum is a vector, so a reversal is a change of 2mv, not zero and not mv. A 0.15 kg ball arriving at 30 m·s⁻¹ and leaving at 30 m·s⁻¹ the other way has Δp = 0.15 × (30 − (−30)) = 9.0 kg·m·s⁻¹. Students who write "speed unchanged, so Δp = 0" lose the entire question. Write the velocities with signs before you subtract.

Why a medical reader should care: trauma severity does not track speed, it tracks the momentum you must shed and the time you are given to shed it. The rest of this chapter is the physics behind seat belts, helmets, fall injuries in the elderly, and the forensic reconstruction of the crash that put the patient in front of you.

§ 02

Impulse and the impulse–momentum theorem

The ideaA push only changes how something is moving if it lasts. A gentle shove kept up for a long time and a violent blow lasting a thousandth of a second can produce exactly the same change of motion, because what counts is the strength of the push multiplied by how long it goes on for. That product has its own name, and this whole section is one sentence: the total push-times-time delivered to a body equals the change in the amount of motion it carries.

Definition & formula

Impulse
The time-integral of a force — a vector whose direction is the direction of the (average) force, measuring "how much push, for how long".
Jnet = ∫Fnet dt = Δp = mvf − mvi always true; no restriction on how F varies (last step assumes constant m)
Full definition, symbols and units
Impulse–momentum theorem
The net impulse delivered to a body equals the change in its momentum. It is not a new law: it is Newton's second law integrated over time.
J = Favg Δtdefinition of the average force over the interval
∫Fnet·dr = ΔKthe companion theorem: net force through distance
Jimpulse (N·s = kg·m·s⁻¹)
Favgtime-averaged force over Δt (N)
Δtduration of the interaction (s)
vi, vfinitial and final velocity (m·s⁻¹)
Δpchange in momentum (kg·m·s⁻¹)

The derivation is one line, and it is worth doing once so the theorem stops feeling like a separate formula. Start from Fnet = dp/dt and integrate both sides over the interaction:

∫titf Fnet dt = ∫pipf dp = pf − piNewton II, integrated over time

That is the whole content of it. The two great "integrated Newton" theorems sit side by side, and choosing between them is most of what problem-solving in mechanics is:

Force through time → momentum. Force through distance → energy.

If the question gives you a duration, or is about a collision, use impulse and momentum. If it gives you a height, a spring compression or a distance, use work and energy.

Two consequences of the vector nature of impulse are worth stating explicitly. First, the units are identical to those of momentum: N·s = (kg·m·s⁻²)·s = kg·m·s⁻¹. If a unit reduces to N·s it is an impulse; if it reduces to N it is a force. Second, impulse points along Δp, not along the velocity. A ball that bounces straight back off a wall receives an impulse pointing away from the wall, even though it was moving toward it for half of the contact.

Worked example 1 — catching versus bouncing

A 0.145 kg baseball reaches a wall at 40 m·s⁻¹. Compare the impulse the wall (or a glove) delivers if (a) the ball is caught and stops, and (b) the ball rebounds elastically at 40 m·s⁻¹.

  1. Choose away from the wall as positive, so vi = −40 m·s⁻¹ in both cases.
  2. (a) Caught: vf = 0, so J = m(vf − vi) = 0.145 × (0 + 40) = 5.8 N·s.
  3. (b) Rebounds: vf = +40 m·s⁻¹, so J = 0.145 × (40 + 40) = 11.6 N·s.
  4. The impulse is exactly doubled: the wall must first destroy the incoming momentum and then create an equal amount in the opposite direction.

Jcatch = 5.8 N·s  ·  Jbounce = 11.6 N·s (2×)

This is why a bouncy projectile is more destructive than one that embeds, and why a rubber-headed mallet transmits a bigger kick to a chisel than a lump of putty of the same mass and speed.

Read the question for "net"

The theorem uses the net impulse. If a question asks for the impulse delivered by one particular force (the floor, the racket, the hand), you must either be told that the others are negligible, or subtract them. During a millisecond-scale collision, gravity's impulse mgΔt is typically 10⁻³–10⁻⁴ of the contact impulse and is dropped without comment — but say so when you drop it.

§ 03

Area under F–t: why stretching Δt saves you

The ideaIf you are moving and you are going to end up stopped, the total amount of push you have to absorb is settled in advance by how heavy you are and how fast you were going. No seat belt, airbag or helmet can make it smaller. What safety equipment changes is how long the stopping takes — and since the total is fixed, spreading it over more time means less force at any one instant. That matters because it is the hardest instant, not the total, that breaks ribs and tears blood vessels.

Definition & formula

Impulse as an area
On a graph of force against time, the impulse is the signed area between the curve and the time axis. Area below the axis counts as negative.
J = area under the F–t curve = Favg Δt = Δp always; the shape of the pulse does not matter
Full definition, symbols and units
Average force
The constant force that would enclose the same area over the same interval — a definition, not an approximation.
Favg = Δp/ΔtΔp is fixed by the situation; Δt is what engineering changes
Fpeak = 2 Favgtriangular pulse (rise then fall)
Fpeak = π2 Favg ≈ 1.57 Favghalf-sine pulse — a good model of a real crash
Jimpulse = area under F–t (N·s)
Fpeakmaximum instantaneous force (N)
Favgtime-averaged force (N)
Δtcontact / crash duration (s)
Δpmomentum that must be shed (kg·m·s⁻¹)

Here is the entire logic of crash protection in two sentences. The impulse is fixed. If you are travelling at 15 m·s⁻¹ and you are going to end up at rest, your momentum must change by m × 15 whatever happens; no device can alter that. Only the shape of the pulse is negotiable. Since the area is fixed, making the pulse wider necessarily makes it shorter — and it is the peak force, not the impulse, that tears aortas and fractures ribs.

Worked example 2 — peak force from a triangular pulse

A 0.058 kg tennis ball arrives at the racket at 25 m·s⁻¹ and is returned at 35 m·s⁻¹ along the same line. The contact force rises from zero to a peak and falls back to zero over a total of 5.0 ms; model it as a triangle. Find Favg and Fpeak.

  1. Positive = direction of the return. vi = −25 m·s⁻¹, vf = +35 m·s⁻¹.
  2. Δp = 0.058 × (35 + 25) = 0.058 × 60 = 3.48 kg·m·s⁻¹, so J = 3.48 N·s.
  3. Favg = J/Δt = 3.48 / 5.0×10⁻³ = 696 N.
  4. Triangle: J = ½ Δt Fpeak ⟹ Fpeak = 2J/Δt = 2 × 696 = 1392 N ≈ 1.4 kN.
  5. Sanity check on ignoring gravity: the ball's weight is 0.058 × 9.8 = 0.57 N, so gravity's impulse over 5.0 ms is 2.8×10⁻³ N·s — 0.08 % of the contact impulse. Safely dropped.

Favg = 6.96×10² N  ·  Fpeak ≈ 1.39×10³ N ≈ 2400 × the ball's weight

Worked example 3 — what the airbag actually buys you

A 70 kg driver in a car travelling at 15 m·s⁻¹ (54 km·h⁻¹) is brought to rest by a frontal impact. Compare (a) an unrestrained stop against the steering column, Δt ≈ 30 ms, with (b) a restrained stop with belt, airbag and crumple zone, Δt ≈ 200 ms.

  1. The momentum to be shed is the same in both cases: |Δp| = 70 × 15 = 1050 kg·m·s⁻¹.
  2. (a) Favg = 1050 / 0.030 = 3.5×10⁴ N = 35 kN. As an acceleration, a = F/m = 500 m·s⁻² = 51 g.
  3. (b) Favg = 1050 / 0.200 = 5.25×10³ N = 5.25 kN, i.e. a = 75 m·s⁻² = 7.7 g.
  4. The ratio is exactly the ratio of the durations, 200/30 = 6.7. Note what the restraint system did not change: the same Δp of 1050 kg·m·s⁻¹ and the same ½(70)(15²) = 7.9 kJ of kinetic energy have to be disposed of either way. Only the timing — and therefore the peak force — is negotiable.
  5. Real crash pulses are closer to half-sine than triangular, so the instantaneous peak is about 1.57 × these averages: roughly 55 kN unrestrained against 8 kN restrained.

Favg: 35 kN (≈51 g) → 5.25 kN (≈7.7 g) — a 6.7-fold cut in force for the same Δp

The airbag does not "absorb the momentum"

Δp is dictated by your speed and your mass and cannot be changed by any device inside the car. The airbag does two separate things, and questions often test whether you can name both: (1) it lengthens Δt, lowering the peak force; and (2) it spreads that force over a much larger contact area, lowering the peak pressure on the sternum and face. Longer time and larger area are independent mechanisms — a rigid padded plate would give you the second without the first.

The five standard applications — and one that runs backwards

Lengthening Δt to reduce force: airbags and seat belts; crumple zones; bending the knees on landing (raises Δt from ~50 ms to ~200 ms); catching an egg in a loose sheet; a boxer riding a punch by moving with it. Lengthening Δt to increase Δp: the follow-through in golf, tennis and baseball — here you are the one applying the force, and keeping contact longer means a larger ∫F dt delivered to the ball. Same equation, opposite intention. Examiners like to mix one follow-through item into a list of cushioning items.

Clinical footnote: injury criteria used in vehicle safety (head injury criterion, chest acceleration limits) are integrals of acceleration over time, not functions of impact speed alone — they are engineering formalisations of exactly this graph. The same reasoning explains why a fall onto carpet and a fall onto tile at the same speed produce completely different hip fractures in an elderly patient.

§ 04

Conservation of momentum, and what "isolated" requires

The ideaWhenever two things push on each other, each push is matched by an equal push the other way, lasting exactly as long. Between the two of them, then, nothing is gained and nothing is lost — the total amount of motion is the same after the interaction as before, however violent or messy it was. The whole catch is in the words "between the two of them": you have to decide first which objects you are counting, because only pushes arriving from outside that chosen group can change the total. Choosing that group well, so the force you could never calculate ends up inside it, is the real skill of this section.

Definition & formula

Conservation of linear momentum
If the net external force on a system is zero, the total momentum vector of that system does not change with time — no matter how violent, complicated or dissipative the internal interactions are.
ΣFext = 0 ⟹ P = Σ mivi = constant exact statement; a vector equation
Full definition, symbols and units
Isolated system
A collection of bodies chosen so that every force you care about acts between members of the collection; only forces from outside the boundary can change P.
Σ mivix (before) = Σ mivix (after)each axis independently
FAB = −FBA ⟹ JAB = −JBAwhy internal forces cancel: Newton III, integrated
Ptotal momentum of the system (kg·m·s⁻¹)
Fextforce from outside the system boundary (N)
JABimpulse of A on B (N·s)
vixx-component of body i's velocity, signed (m·s⁻¹)

The proof is Newton's third law with a time integral wrapped around it. Inside the system every force comes in a pair, FAB = −FBA, and the two members of the pair act for exactly the same duration. Their impulses therefore cancel exactly, however complicated the force–time profile is. Sum over all pairs and every internal contribution vanishes, leaving ΔP = Jext. Zero external impulse, zero change in P.

"Isolated" comes in three grades

① Strictly isolated

ΣFext = 0 exactly. Two carts on a frictionless air track; two spacecraft in deep space. Rare in practice.

② Isolated during Δt

External forces exist, but their impulse over the brief interaction is negligible next to the internal impulse. This is what actually justifies almost every collision problem you will solve.

③ Isolated along one axis

ΣFext = 0 is a vector statement. If the external forces all point along y, then px is conserved and py is not.

Go deeper · putting a number on "the collision is short"

Grade ② deserves a number, because "the collision is short" is usually asserted and never checked. Take a 1500 kg car in a 0.10 s impact. Tyre friction is external: its impulse is roughly μMgΔt ≈ 0.8 × 1500 × 9.8 × 0.10 ≈ 1.2×10³ N·s. The internal impulse in the crash is of order mΔv ≈ 1500 × 10 = 1.5×10⁴ N·s. Friction contributes under 10 %, so momentum conservation is good to roughly that accuracy — which is precisely why accident reconstruction from skid marks works at all.

Worked example 4 — recoil, and why equal momenta do not mean equal energies

A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.020 kg bullet at 700 m·s⁻¹. The system (rifle + bullet) starts at rest and the propellant force is internal. Find the recoil speed and compare the kinetic energies.

  1. Take the firing direction as positive. Before: P = 0. After: P must still be 0.
  2. 0 = mbvb + mrvr ⟹ vr = −(0.020 × 700)/4.0 = −14/4.0 = −3.5 m·s⁻¹ (backwards).
  3. Kbullet = ½ × 0.020 × 700² = 4900 J; Krifle = ½ × 4.0 × 3.5² = 24.5 J.
  4. Ratio = 200 = mrifle/mbullet, exactly as K = p²/(2m) predicts for equal momentum magnitudes.
  5. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is emphatically not — 4925 J appeared from the propellant's chemical energy.

vrecoil = 3.5 m·s⁻¹ backwards · Kbullet/Krifle = 200

"Momentum is always conserved" is false as stated

A ball falling toward the floor gains momentum every second — gravity is an external force on the ball. What is conserved is the momentum of ball + Earth. The sentence is only true once you have specified the system and the axis. When a marker asks "is momentum conserved here?", the expected answer always names the system boundary.

Choosing the system is the whole skill

Draw the boundary so that the force you cannot compute becomes internal. You do not know the gas pressure in a rifle barrel — so include the bullet and the rifle. You do not know the force between a person's feet and a boat — so include both. You do not know the crash force — so include both vehicles. If a force crosses your boundary, either it is negligible over Δt or you have drawn the boundary in the wrong place.

§ 05

Elastic, inelastic, perfectly inelastic

The ideaEvery collision keeps the total amount of motion of the bodies involved — that part never varies. What separates one collision from another is what happens to the energy. A few interactions hand all of it back, most turn some of it into heat, sound and bent metal, and the messiest kind leaves the bodies stuck together and travelling as one lump. That last kind wastes the most energy any collision can waste — but never all of it, because the wreckage still has to keep moving in the direction the motion originally pointed.

Definition & formula

Collision
A brief interaction in which the internal impulse greatly exceeds any external impulse, so the total momentum of the colliding bodies is (to excellent approximation) unchanged.
Σp conserved in every collision · ΣK conserved only if elastic the one sentence to carry into the exam
Full definition, symbols and units
Elastic collision
One in which the total kinetic energy after equals the total kinetic energy before.
Inelastic collision
One in which some kinetic energy is converted into internal energy — heat, sound, permanent deformation.
Perfectly inelastic collision
One in which the bodies move off with a common velocity; this is the maximum kinetic energy loss that momentum conservation permits.
v′ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂m₁ + m₂perfectly inelastic, 1-D
ΔK = −½ μ (v₁ − v₂)²,   μ = m₁m₂m₁ + m₂energy lost, perfectly inelastic, any masses
|ΔK|/Ki = m₂m₁ + m₂special case: target m₂ initially at rest
Ktotal = ½Mvcm² + Krelonly Krel can ever be destroyed
v′common final velocity (m·s⁻¹)
μreduced mass m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) (kg)
Mtotal mass m₁ + m₂ (kg)
vcmvelocity of the centre of mass (m·s⁻¹)
Krelkinetic energy of relative motion (J)
ΔKchange in kinetic energy, negative for a loss (J)

Every collision conserves momentum. The taxonomy is entirely about what happens to the kinetic energy, and it is worth understanding why "perfectly inelastic" is the floor rather than "zero energy left". Split the total kinetic energy into two pieces: the energy of the system's bulk motion, ½Mvcm², and the energy of the bodies moving relative to the centre of mass. Momentum conservation fixes vcm permanently, so ½Mvcm² is untouchable. Only the relative part can be dissipated — and when all of it is gone, the bodies are no longer moving relative to each other, which is exactly the statement that they travel together.

Worked example 5 — coupling railway wagons

A 12 000 kg wagon rolling at 3.0 m·s⁻¹ couples to an 8000 kg wagon at rest on the same track. Find the common speed and the energy dissipated.

  1. Momentum (perfectly inelastic): v′ = (12 000 × 3.0 + 0)/(20 000) = 36 000/20 000 = 1.8 m·s⁻¹.
  2. Ki = ½ × 12 000 × 3.0² = 54.0 kJ.
  3. Kf = ½ × 20 000 × 1.8² = ½ × 20 000 × 3.24 = 32.4 kJ.
  4. Dissipated: 54.0 − 32.4 = 21.6 kJ, i.e. 40 % of the initial kinetic energy.
  5. Cross-check with the reduced mass: μ = (12 000 × 8000)/20 000 = 4800 kg, so |ΔK| = ½ × 4800 × 3.0² = 21.6 kJ ✓. And 40 % = m₂/(m₁+m₂) = 8000/20 000 ✓.

v′ = 1.8 m·s⁻¹ · 21.6 kJ lost (40 % of Ki) to couplers, heat and sound

"Perfectly inelastic" ≠ "all the kinetic energy is lost"

Only ½μvrel² can go. The bulk term ½Mvcm² survives untouched, because destroying it would destroy the momentum. All the kinetic energy disappears only when the total momentum is zero — two identical cars in a perfectly symmetric head-on crash, or two equal lumps of clay meeting at equal speeds. A question that offers "100 % of K is lost" for a collision with a stationary target is offering you a trap.

Explosions are collisions run backwards

The same equation, the same conservation law, the opposite sign of ΔK. A stationary object that bursts into fragments must have Σpfragments = 0 — so with two fragments they fly apart along the same line with equal and opposite momenta, and the lighter one is faster and carries more of the released energy (K = p²/2m again). With three fragments, the three momentum vectors form a closed triangle.

§ 06

One-dimensional elastic collisions and their three limits

The ideaWhen two bodies meet head-on along a single line and none of the motion is wasted, the outcome is settled in advance — there is nothing left for the collision to decide. The tidiest way to see it is that the two separate exactly as fast as they closed on each other, which is a much friendlier statement than it sounds and does all the work. Everything after that is governed by one thing only, the ratio of the two masses: evenly matched, light meeting heavy, or heavy meeting light. Learn those three pictures and every intermediate case is somewhere between them.

Definition & formula

1-D elastic collision
A head-on collision along a single line in which both total momentum and total kinetic energy are unchanged. Two conservation equations, two unknowns — the outcome is fully determined.
v₁ − v₂ = −(v₁′ − v₂′) elastic, 1-D; approach speed = separation speed
Full definition, symbols and units
Relative-velocity rule
In an elastic collision the speed at which the bodies separate equals the speed at which they approached. This linear statement replaces the quadratic energy equation.
v₁′ = (m₁ − m₂)v₁ + 2m₂v₂m₁ + m₂general 1-D elastic result
v₂′ = (m₂ − m₁)v₂ + 2m₁v₁m₁ + m₂general 1-D elastic result
v₁′ = m₁ − m₂m₁ + m₂ v₁  ·  v₂′ = 2m₁m₁ + m₂ v₁target at rest: v₂ = 0
K₂′/K₁ = 4m₁m₂(m₁ + m₂)²fraction of energy transferred, target at rest
m₁, m₂masses of projectile and target (kg)
v₁, v₂signed velocities before (m·s⁻¹)
v₁′, v₂′signed velocities after (m·s⁻¹)
K₁projectile's initial kinetic energy (J)
K₂′target's final kinetic energy (J)

Do not memorise the two big fractions. Derive them the way that takes ten seconds and never goes wrong. Write momentum and energy conservation as differences:

m₁(v₁ − v₁′) = m₂(v₂′ − v₂)momentum, rearranged
m₁(v₁² − v₁′²) = m₂(v₂′² − v₂²)energy, rearranged (the ½ cancels)

Divide the second by the first. Each side is a difference of squares over a difference, so it collapses to a sum: v₁ + v₁′ = v₂ + v₂′, which rearranges to the relative-velocity rule. You now have two linear equations — momentum and the relative-velocity rule — and no quadratic to solve. In an exam, use that pair; it is faster than the memorised formula and it automatically gets the signs right.

Worked example 6 — a 3 : 1 head-on elastic collision

A 0.30 kg glider on a frictionless air track moves at 4.0 m·s⁻¹ and collides elastically with a stationary 0.10 kg glider. Find both final velocities and verify both conservation laws.

  1. Positive = the direction of the incoming glider. m₁ = 0.30, m₂ = 0.10, v₁ = 4.0, v₂ = 0.
  2. v₁′ = ((0.30 − 0.10)/0.40) × 4.0 = 0.50 × 4.0 = +2.0 m·s⁻¹ (still forwards).
  3. v₂′ = (2 × 0.30/0.40) × 4.0 = 1.50 × 4.0 = +6.0 m·s⁻¹.
  4. Momentum check: before 0.30 × 4.0 = 1.20; after 0.30 × 2.0 + 0.10 × 6.0 = 0.60 + 0.60 = 1.20 ✓
  5. Energy check: before ½ × 0.30 × 16 = 2.40 J; after ½ × 0.30 × 4 + ½ × 0.10 × 36 = 0.60 + 1.80 = 2.40 J ✓
  6. Relative-velocity check: approach 4.0 − 0 = 4.0; separation 6.0 − 2.0 = 4.0 ✓
  7. Energy transferred: 1.80/2.40 = 75 %, matching 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)² = 4(0.30)(0.10)/0.16 = 0.75 ✓

v₁′ = +2.0 m·s⁻¹, v₂′ = +6.0 m·s⁻¹ · 75 % of the kinetic energy handed over

"2v" is a limit, not an identity

v₂′ = (2m₁/(m₁+m₂))v₁ is always less than 2v₁ and only approaches it as m₁/m₂ → ∞. In the worked example the projectile is three times heavier and the target still only reaches 1.5v₁. And no, the light body leaving faster than the projectile does not violate energy conservation — it has less mass, and ½m₂(2v₁)² can still be smaller than ½m₁v₁².

Why reactors use water, not lead

The fraction of kinetic energy a neutron loses in one head-on elastic collision is 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)², which is maximal (100 %) at equal masses. Hydrogen (A = 1) → 100 %; deuterium (A = 2) → 89 %; carbon-12 → 28 %; uranium-238 → 1.7 %. Heavy nuclei are useless as moderators for exactly the reason a bowling ball is useless for stopping a ping-pong ball. This "equal masses transfer everything" result is the most transferable idea in the section — it also governs impedance matching in ultrasound and acoustic transmission across a tissue boundary.

§ 07

The coefficient of restitution

The ideaBounciness can be boiled down to a single number. Compare how fast two things come apart with how fast they came together, and that one ratio places the collision somewhere on the scale from a lump of putty that simply stays where it lands to a pair of ball bearings that give everything back. Two warnings come with it. It belongs to the pair of surfaces rather than to one ball — the liveliest ball in the world is dead on sand — and because it compares speeds and not heights, reading it straight off how high something rebounded is the slip almost everyone makes first.

Definition & formula

Coefficient of restitution, e
The ratio of the relative speed of separation to the relative speed of approach, measured along the line of impact. A dimensionless number that grades a collision on the scale from perfectly inelastic to elastic.
e = |v₂′ − v₁′||v₁ − v₂| along the common normal at contact
Full definition, symbols and units
e = 1 elastic · 0 < e < 1 inelastic · e = 0 perfectly inelastic · e > 1 explosivethe whole taxonomy in one parameter
e = vup/vdown = √(h′/h)drop test onto a massive fixed floor
hn = h₀ e2nheight after n bounces
K′/K = e²fraction of KE retained per bounce off a fixed floor (in general e² is the ratio for the relative motion)
ecoefficient of restitution (dimensionless)
h, h′drop and rebound height (m)
vdown, vupimpact and rebound speed (m·s⁻¹)
nnumber of bounces (dimensionless)

The coefficient of restitution is what turns the qualitative words of §5 into a number you can put into an equation. Give me e and the masses and I can solve any 1-D collision: momentum conservation supplies one equation, the definition of e supplies the second, and neither is quadratic.

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′momentum
v₂′ − v₁′ = −e(v₂ − v₁)restitution, written with signs — no absolute values

Setting e = 1 recovers the elastic relative-velocity rule of §6; setting e = 0 forces v₁′ = v₂′, the perfectly inelastic case. Everything in this chapter's collision taxonomy is one parameter sliding from 0 to 1.

Two properties of e are frequently misunderstood. First, e belongs to the pair of materials, not to one ball — a "bouncy" ball on sand has e near zero. Second, e is only approximately constant: it falls as the impact speed rises, because faster impacts push more of the material past its elastic limit. Standardised drop tests therefore always specify the drop height.

Worked example 7 — a bounce test

A ball released from rest at 1.20 m above a hard floor rebounds to 0.75 m. Find e, the fraction of kinetic energy retained, and the height of the second bounce.

  1. The floor is effectively infinitely massive and stationary, so the relative speeds are just the ball's speeds: e = vup/vdown.
  2. Free fall gives v = √(2gh), so e = √(2gh′)/√(2gh) = √(h′/h) = √(0.75/1.20) = √0.625 = 0.79.
  3. Kinetic energy retained per impact = e² = 0.625 = 62.5 %. (Note this equals h′/h — of course, since K at the floor is proportional to the height it came from.)
  4. Second bounce: h₂ = h₀e⁴ = 1.20 × 0.625² = 1.20 × 0.3906 = 0.47 m.
  5. Because heights fall geometrically, the ball never quite stops bouncing in this model — but it does so in a finite total time, which is a nice piece of series arithmetic if you like that sort of thing.

e = 0.79 · 62.5 % of K retained per bounce · second bounce ≈ 0.47 m

e is a speed ratio, e² is an energy ratio

The single most common slip in this section is quoting h′/h as e. It is e². Height is proportional to speed squared, so you must take a square root to get back to a velocity ratio. If a bounce test gives h′/h = 0.64, then e = 0.80, not 0.64.

Restitution acts along the normal only

For an oblique bounce off a smooth surface, e applies to the component perpendicular to the surface; the tangential component passes through unchanged (no friction). Measuring both angles from the normal, tan θ′ = tan θ / e, so whenever e < 1 the rebound leaves at a larger angle to the normal than it arrived — the bounce is flatter, closer to grazing. A question that quietly makes the surface rough is asking you to notice that the tangential component is no longer conserved either.

§ 08

Two-dimensional collisions by components

The ideaWhen things hit each other off-centre instead of squarely, no new physics arrives — the same conservation holds separately along each of two directions at right angles, so one awkward problem on a flat surface becomes two ordinary straight-line problems worked side by side. What does change is the bookkeeping. Two directions give you two statements, and a glancing collision leaves more unknowns than that, so the question has to hand you one further fact: usually an angle, or the news that the two ended up travelling as one. Split into directions first, deal with each direction entirely on its own, and only put the answer back together at the very end.

Definition & formula

Two-dimensional collision
A collision in which the velocities are not collinear. Because momentum is a vector equation, it splits into one independent scalar equation per axis — a 2-D collision is two 1-D momentum problems solved simultaneously.
Σpx(before) = Σpx(after)  and  Σpy(before) = Σpy(after) two independent equations; always true if isolated
Full definition, symbols and units
|P| = √(Px² + Py²),   tan θ = Py/Pxrecombine components at the end, never before
ΣK(before) = ΣK(after)one extra scalar equation — elastic only
v₁′ ⟂ v₂′equal masses, elastic, target at rest, glancing
Px, Pycomponents of total momentum (kg·m·s⁻¹)
θdirection of the total momentum, measured from +x (rad or °)
v₁′, v₂′final velocity vectors (m·s⁻¹)

Start by counting equations, because that tells you what a 2-D problem can and cannot ask. Two bodies leaving a collision in a plane have four unknowns: two speeds and two angles. Momentum conservation gives you two equations. Kinetic energy conservation, if the collision is elastic, gives you one more — energy is a scalar, so there is only ever one energy equation no matter how many dimensions you have. Even in the elastic case you are one equation short.

That deficit is not a flaw in the theory; it reflects real physics — the outcome depends on the impact parameter, how squarely the bodies hit. So an exam question must always supply the missing information: either the collision is perfectly inelastic (the two bodies share one velocity, killing two unknowns), or you are told one of the outgoing angles. If you find yourself with more unknowns than equations, re-read the question; the extra datum is there.

The 90° result — a two-line proof worth knowing

For an elastic collision between equal masses with the target at rest, momentum gives p₁ = p₁′ + p₂′, and energy (dividing out the common 1/2m) gives p₁² = p₁′² + p₂′². A vector triangle whose sides satisfy Pythagoras is right-angled, so v₁′ ⟂ v₂′ — the outgoing velocities are always 90° apart. Every pool player knows this without knowing why. Drop any one of the three conditions and the result fails; note also that it excludes the exactly head-on case, where one final velocity is zero and no angle is defined.

Worked example 8 — reconstructing an intersection collision

A 1200 kg car travelling due east at 20 m·s⁻¹ collides with an 1800 kg van travelling due north at 15 m·s⁻¹. The wreckage locks together. Find the velocity of the wreck immediately after impact and the energy dissipated.

  1. Take east = +x, north = +y. Because the impact lasts ~0.1 s, the friction impulse is under 10 % of the internal impulse (§4), so treat momentum as conserved.
  2. Px = 1200 × 20 = 2.40×10⁴ kg·m·s⁻¹ (the van contributes nothing to x).
  3. Py = 1800 × 15 = 2.70×10⁴ kg·m·s⁻¹ (the car contributes nothing to y).
  4. Total mass M = 3000 kg, so Vx = 8.0 m·s⁻¹ and Vy = 9.0 m·s⁻¹.
  5. Speed: V = √(8.0² + 9.0²) = √145 = 12.0 m·s⁻¹. Direction: θ = arctan(9.0/8.0) = 48° north of east.
  6. Ki = ½(1200)(20²) + ½(1800)(15²) = 240 kJ + 202.5 kJ = 442.5 kJ. Kf = ½(3000)(145) = 217.5 kJ.
  7. Dissipated: 225 kJ, i.e. 51 % — crushed metal, heat, sound.

V = 12.0 m·s⁻¹ at 48° N of E · 2.25×10⁵ J dissipated (51 % of Ki)

Investigators run this backwards: the direction of the post-impact skid marks fixes θ, and θ plus the two masses is enough to test a driver's claimed speed. That single 48° is the whole forensic content of the problem.

You cannot add speeds, and the angle is not the mass ratio

In the example above the two momenta happen to be 24 000 and 27 000 kg·m·s⁻¹, but the resultant is 36 100, not 51 000. Resolve, add component by component, then recombine — in that order, every time. And θ = 48° comes from the momentum components; it is not arctan of the mass ratio and not the average of the two directions.

§ 09

Centre of mass and the CM theorem

The ideaAny collection of matter, however sprawling and however many pieces it has been broken into, has one special point that stands in for the whole thing. Push the collection from outside and that point moves exactly as a single lump of the same total mass would. Do whatever you like from within — explode, wriggle, collide, fly apart — and the point ignores all of it completely, because every internal push comes with its equal and opposite twin. That is why the pieces of a burst firework spread out around a path that carries on as though nothing had happened, and why standing upright is really the business of keeping one invisible point above your feet.

Definition & formula

Centre of mass
The mass-weighted average position of a system of particles — the unique point that responds to external forces as though the entire mass were concentrated there.
rcm = (1/M) Σ miri,   M = Σ mi discrete bodies
Full definition, symbols and units
Centre-of-mass theorem
The centre of mass accelerates as if all the external forces acted on a single particle of mass M placed at that point. Internal forces can never move it.
rcm = (1/M) ∫ r dmcontinuous body; dm = ρ dV (or λ dx, σ dA)
P = Mvcmtotal momentum ≡ mass × CM velocity
Macm = ΣFextthe CM theorem — Newton II for a system
rcmposition of the centre of mass (m)
mi, rimass (kg) and position (m) of particle i
Mtotal mass of the system (kg)
dmmass element (kg); λ in kg·m⁻¹, σ in kg·m⁻², ρ in kg·m⁻³
vcmcentre-of-mass velocity (m·s⁻¹)
acmcentre-of-mass acceleration (m·s⁻²)

The theorem is the definition differentiated twice. From Mrcm = Σmiri we get Mvcm = Σmivi = P, and differentiating again gives Macm = Σmiai = ΣFi. Internal forces cancel in third-law pairs, so only external forces survive. Notice what this means: conservation of momentum and "the CM moves at constant velocity" are the same statement, written in two different alphabets.

For a continuous body the sum becomes an integral. A uniform rod of length L and linear density λ has M = λL and xcm = (1/M)∫₀L xλ dx = (λ/M)(L²/2) = L/2 — the geometric centre, as symmetry demands. Every uniform, symmetric body has its CM at its geometric centre; the standard non-obvious results worth remembering are a uniform triangular plate (one third of the height from the base) and a uniform solid cone (one quarter of the height from the base).

Worked example 9 — centre of mass of three particles

Masses of 2.0 kg, 3.0 kg and 5.0 kg sit at (0, 0), (4.0, 0) and (0, 6.0) metres. Locate the centre of mass.

  1. M = 2.0 + 3.0 + 5.0 = 10.0 kg.
  2. xcm = [2.0(0) + 3.0(4.0) + 5.0(0)]/10.0 = 12.0/10.0 = 1.2 m.
  3. ycm = [2.0(0) + 3.0(0) + 5.0(6.0)]/10.0 = 30.0/10.0 = 3.0 m.
  4. The CM is pulled toward the heaviest particle, and it need not lie on any of them — nor even inside the body, as a ring, a boomerang or a horseshoe shows.

rcm = (1.2, 3.0) m

The same calculation applied to the Earth–Moon system (mE = 5.97×10²⁴ kg, mM = 7.35×10²² kg, separation 3.84×10⁸ m) puts the barycentre at mMd/(mE+mM) = 4.67×10⁶ m from the Earth's centre — inside the Earth, whose radius is 6.37×10⁶ m. The Earth does not sit still while the Moon orbits; both orbit that buried point.

What the theorem buys you: explosions become trivial

Because internal forces cannot move the CM, an exploding projectile's centre of mass carries on along the original parabola as though nothing had happened — right up until the first fragment lands and the ground starts exerting an external force. If you know where the CM must be and where all but one fragment went, the missing fragment's position follows from one line of algebra.

Worked example 10 — the exploding projectile

A shell is fired at 50 m·s⁻¹ at 37° above the horizontal (take sin 37° = 0.60, cos 37° = 0.80). At the top of its flight it bursts into two equal fragments; one of them is momentarily at rest and drops vertically. Where does the other land? Ignore air resistance; g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Components: vx = 50 × 0.80 = 40.0 m·s⁻¹, vy0 = 50 × 0.60 = 30.0 m·s⁻¹.
  2. Time to the apex: t = vy0/g = 30.0/9.8 = 3.06 s; apex height 30.0²/(2×9.8) = 45.9 m; horizontal distance to the apex xa = 40.0 × 3.06 = 122 m.
  3. Undisturbed range would be 40.0 × 2(3.06) = 245 m.
  4. At the apex the velocity is purely horizontal, so total momentum = (2m)(40.0) = 80.0m. Fragment A has zero momentum, so fragment B must carry all of it: m·vB = 80.0m ⟹ vB = 80.0 m·s⁻¹.
  5. Both fragments fall from the same height, so both take 3.06 s to reach the ground. A lands at 122 m; B lands at 122 + 80.0 × 3.06 = 122 + 245 = 367 m.
  6. CM check: (122 + 367)/2 = 245 m — the undisturbed range, exactly as the theorem requires ✓
  7. Energy check: before the burst K = ½(2m)(40.0²) = 1600m; after, K = 0 + ½m(80.0²) = 3200m. The explosive added 1600m joules — momentum conserved, kinetic energy created.

Fragment B lands 367 m from the launch point (fragment A at 122 m, CM at 245 m)

Centre of mass vs centre of gravity

They are defined differently: the CM is the mass-weighted mean position; the centre of gravity is the point where the resultant gravitational force acts. They coincide only in a uniform gravitational field — which is an excellent approximation for anything smaller than a mountain, so for exam purposes treat them as the same point, but know the distinction if asked. Also note the CM theorem stops applying the moment the external force set changes: once the first fragment hits the ground, the ground pushes back and the CM stops following its parabola.

Where physics meets anatomy

In the anatomical position the whole-body centre of mass sits roughly at the level of the second sacral vertebra, just anterior to it — but it moves the instant you raise an arm, and it leaves the body entirely when you bend forward. Standing balance is the problem of keeping the vertical projection of the CM inside the base of support, which is why gait analysis, fall-risk assessment, prosthetic design and the Fosbury flop (in which the athlete's CM passes below the bar while the body passes above it) are all the same piece of physics.

§ 10

The ballistic pendulum: momentum first, energy second

The ideaSome problems have to be cut in two before they can be solved, because the physics changes character partway through. A bullet burying itself in a hanging block is the classic one: the burial is brief and violent and throws away almost all the energy, while the swing that follows is slow and smooth and throws away none. So you carry the first half across on the rule that survives a violent impact, then carry the second half across on the rule that survives a gentle glide, and the measured height at the end tells you how fast the bullet was going. Running one rule straight through from start to finish is the tempting shortcut, and it is wrong by a wide margin.

Definition & formula

Ballistic pendulum
A block of mass M hanging on a light string or rods, into which a projectile of mass m embeds; measuring how high the block subsequently swings gives the projectile's speed.
v = m + Mm √(2gh) the combined result; h measured from the block's initial level
Full definition, symbols and units
Why it needs two stages
The capture is a perfectly inelastic collision — momentum survives, kinetic energy does not. The swing is a smooth, non-dissipative motion under gravity — mechanical energy survives, momentum does not.
Stage 1: m v = (m + M) Vmomentum conserved (Δt ≈ 1 ms); KE is not

This is the canonical two-stage problem, and the reason it appears in every syllabus is that it forces you to choose the right conservation law twice in a row, for opposite reasons.

Spelling out the reasoning, because this is what an examiner is really testing:

  • Stage 1, why momentum: the embedding lasts of order 10⁻³ s. The external forces (gravity, string tension) deliver an impulse of order (m+M)g·Δt ≈ 0.02 N·s, utterly negligible against the internal impulse of several N·s. So Σp holds.
  • Stage 1, why not energy: the bullet ends up buried in the block. That is a perfectly inelastic collision by definition, and the missing kinetic energy is now heat and permanently deformed wood and lead.
  • Stage 2, why not momentum: gravity acts for ~0.5 s. Its impulse is not remotely negligible — indeed it is what brings the block to a stop at the top of the swing.
  • Stage 2, why energy: gravity is conservative, and the string tension is always perpendicular to the velocity so it does zero work. Mechanical energy is therefore conserved.
Worked example 11 — measuring a bullet's speed, and the error that ruins it

A 10.0 g bullet embeds in a 2.00 kg block hanging from long light strings. The block rises 0.150 m. Find the bullet's speed, then compute what you would have got by (wrongly) equating the bullet's kinetic energy to the final potential energy. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Stage 2 first (it contains the measured quantity): V = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.150) = √2.94 = 1.71 m·s⁻¹.
  2. Stage 1: m v = (m + M)V ⟹ v = (2.010/0.0100) × 1.71 = 201 × 1.7146 = 345 m·s⁻¹ (3 s.f.).
  3. Sanity check on the energy budget: the bullet arrived with ½(0.0100)(345²) ≈ 594 J; the block+bullet leaves the collision with ½(2.010)(1.71²) ≈ 2.95 J. Only m/(m+M) = 0.0100/2.010 = 0.50 % of the kinetic energy survives — 99.5 % became heat and deformation.
  4. The wrong method: setting ½mv² = (m+M)gh gives v = √(2(m+M)gh/m) = √(2 × 2.010 × 9.8 × 0.150 / 0.0100) = √591 = 24.3 m·s⁻¹.
  5. That is too small by a factor √((m+M)/m) = √201 = 14.2 — a bullet apparently slower than a club cricket delivery. The error is not a rounding slip; it is a wrong physical assumption.

v ≈ 3.45×10² m·s⁻¹  ·  wrong method gives 24.3 m·s⁻¹, low by ×14.2

The single most common error in this chapter

Writing ½mv² = (m+M)gh in one step. It silently assumes that the bullet's kinetic energy all became gravitational potential energy — that is, it assumes the collision was elastic, which directly contradicts "embeds". It always underestimates the projectile speed, by the factor √((m+M)/m). The mirror-image error, applying momentum conservation to the swing, is equally fatal: momentum is manifestly not conserved when the block starts moving and finishes at rest.

The general two-stage template

The ballistic pendulum is one instance of a pattern you will meet repeatedly. In every case, split the problem at the instant the physics changes character, and pick the law that survives in each half.

ProblemStage 1 → lawStage 2 → lawThe trap
Ballistic pendulum Bullet embeds → Σp Block swings to height h → ΣE Using energy in stage 1; answer low by √((m+M)/m)
Bullet into a block on a spring Embedding → Σp Spring compressed x → ΣE with ½kx² Same as above; also forgetting the bullet's mass in the moving block
Bob swings down, then strikes a block Descent through h → ΣE Impact → Σp Order reversed relative to the pendulum — read which comes first
Block slides down a frictionless ramp into another block Descent → ΣE Collision → Σp (+ e or "sticks") Using energy for the collision when it is not elastic
Compressed spring released between two carts Push apart → Σp (total is zero) — Here energy also works, because an ideal spring is non-dissipative
Collision, then the wreck skids to a stop Impact → Σp Skid → work–energy with friction Friction dissipates: use W = −μkMgd, not ΣE conservation

Bang → momentum. Swing → energy.

Short and violent means momentum. Smooth and frictionless means energy. Use both in the same stage only when you are told the collision is elastic.

The follow-up question you should expect

"What fraction of the bullet's kinetic energy is lost?" — answer M/(m + M), which is 99.5 % here and near 100 % for any realistic bullet-and-block. Sometimes the pendulum length L and the angle θ are given instead of h, in which case substitute h = L(1 − cos θ) and proceed identically; for small θ you may use 1 − cos θ ≈ θ²/2 with θ in radians.
