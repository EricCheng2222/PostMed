The Cell Cycle, Mitosis, Meiosis and Cancer

A dividing cell has exactly one hard problem: make a second complete copy of the genome, then hand the two copies to two daughters without losing, duplicating or scrambling a single chromosome. Everything on this page is either a mechanism for solving that problem — the ordered phases, the spindle, the cohesin rings, the checkpoints — or a description of what happens when the solution fails. Meiosis is the same machinery deliberately modified to halve the count and shuffle the deck; aneuploidy is the machinery slipping once; cancer is the machinery's brakes being dismantled one gene at a time over decades. Read it as one story about control, and the vocabulary stops needing to be memorised.

§ 01

The cycle: what happens when

The ideaA cell that is going to divide has to do two things, and the order is forced: copy everything first, then split. The copying is invisible from the outside and takes most of the day; the splitting is the dramatic part and is over in an hour. The stretches in between are not idle waiting. They are where the cell asks whether the outside world still wants another cell, whether the copy came out clean, and whether it is safe to go on — and where, if the answer is no, it stops. Most cells in your body are stopped there permanently.

Definition & key facts

Cell cycle
The ordered sequence a cell passes through between one division and the next: a long interphase (G1, S, G2) in which the cell grows and duplicates its DNA, followed by a short M phase in which the duplicated genome is separated and the cell divides.
Tc = G1 + S + G2 + M ≈ 11 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 24 h a reference mammalian cell in culture; real values vary enormously and G1 varies most
Full definition, symbols and units
Interphase
G1 + S + G2. Not a resting stage — it is when transcription, translation, organelle duplication and DNA replication all happen. Chromosomes are present throughout, decondensed as chromatin.
Restriction point (R)
A point in late G1 past which the cell no longer needs external growth factors to complete the cycle. Before R the decision is reversible and depends on the environment; after R it is autonomous. Called START in yeast.
G0
A distinct non-cycling state entered from G1, not a slow part of G1. Reversible (a hepatocyte, a resting lymphocyte) or permanent (a neuron, a cardiac myocyte, a mature skeletal muscle fibre).
Mitotic index (MI)
The fraction of cells in a randomly sampled, asynchronous population that are in mitosis. In a steady-state population the fraction in any phase is proportional to that phase's duration.

Why the order is not negotiable

Three constraints fix the sequence, and each is worth being able to state:

  1. Replication must finish before separation begins. Pulling apart a partly replicated chromosome tears it. This is enforced, not hoped for: unreplicated DNA leaves stretches of single-stranded DNA at stalled forks, which activate ATR and hold the cell in G2.
  2. Replication must happen once and only once. An origin that fires twice in one S phase would give a region three or four copies. The cell solves this by splitting licensing from firing in time: origins are licensed in late M and G1, when CDK activity is low, by loading the MCM helicase; they are fired in S, when CDK activity is high — and high CDK activity simultaneously blocks any further licensing. So there is no window in which an origin that has already fired can be licensed a second time: re-licensing has to wait for CDK activity to collapse at mitotic exit. This one design decision is the reason G1 exists as a separate phase at all.
  3. The decision to commit is taken before the expensive part. Replicating 6.2 × 109 base pairs is metabolically enormous. The cell therefore concentrates its social decision-making — is there growth factor? am I big enough? is my DNA intact? — into late G1, at the restriction point, and after that runs to completion.

Sentences that sound like a textbook and are wrong

"Interphase is the resting phase of the cell cycle." This is the single most persistent falsehood in the topic, and it survives because the nineteenth-century cytologists who named it could only see the chromosomes, which are invisible in interphase. Interphase is when essentially all transcription, translation, growth and DNA replication occur. Mitosis is the phase in which the cell shuts transcription down. If anything deserves the word "resting" it is G0, and even that is an actively maintained state.

"During prophase the chromosomes replicate." Replication is complete before prophase begins — it happened in S, several hours earlier. A cell entering prophase already has two chromatids per chromosome. A question that mentions chromatids appearing "at prophase" is describing when they become visible, not when they were made.

"G0 is a prolonged G1." The two are biochemically distinct states, not a slow and a fast version of the same one. G0 cells have dismantled their cyclin D, raised p27, and in many cases shrunk their ribosome complement; re-entry takes hours and requires new transcription. The clinical consequence is real: cells in G0 are largely refractory to cell-cycle-specific chemotherapy, which is a standard explanation for why slowly growing solid tumours respond poorly to agents that devastate marrow.

Worked example 1 — from a microscope count to phase durations

A cultured cell line is asynchronous and in steady exponential growth with a cycle time of 20 h. In a randomly chosen field, 1200 cells are scored and 48 show condensed chromosomes. A parallel dish is given a 30-minute pulse of BrdU and 30 % of nuclei label. Estimate the duration of mitosis, of interphase, of S phase, and of G1 + G2 combined.

  1. The mitotic index is the observed fraction: MI = 48 / 1200 = 0.040 = 4.0 %.
  2. In a steady-state asynchronous population the fraction of cells caught in a phase equals that phase's share of the cycle. So tM = 0.040 × 20 h = 0.80 h = 48 min.
  3. Interphase is everything else: (1 − 0.040) × 20 h = 19.2 h. Notice how easy it is to be asked for this rather than for mitosis.
  4. The labelling index is the S-phase fraction, because a short BrdU pulse marks exactly the cells replicating at that instant: tS = 0.30 × 20 h = 6.0 h.
  5. By subtraction G1 + G2 = 20 − 0.8 − 6.0 = 13.2 h. Separating those two needs a different experiment — a pulse-chase watching labelled cells arrive in mitosis, whose lag gives G2.
  6. Where the assumption bites: in a truly exponentially growing population, younger cells are over-represented (every cell that divides is replaced by two), so the strict relation carries a logarithmic correction. Fewer cells are caught in a late phase than the simple proportion predicts, so the proportional method slightly under-estimates the duration of late phases such as mitosis and over-estimates early ones such as G1. At the level this exam works, use the proportional relation, but know that it assumes a steady-state, asynchronous, uniformly cycling population with no G0 fraction and no cell death.
tM = 48 min · interphase 19.2 h · tS = 6.0 h · G1 + G2 = 13.2 h

What this section is actually asked for

Three things, reliably. The order and the content of the phases — especially that DNA replication is confined to S and that the chromosome number does not change there. The mitotic-index calculation, in either direction: count → duration, or duration → expected count. And the meaning of G0 as a distinct state entered from G1, with named examples of cells that occupy it permanently (neurons, cardiac and skeletal muscle, lens fibre cells), reversibly (hepatocytes, lymphocytes, fibroblasts) or never (intestinal crypt cells, basal keratinocytes, haematopoietic progenitors).

Exam technique · turning a count into a duration

  1. Name the fraction before you touch the arithmetic — "condensed chromosomes", "mitotic figures" or "cells in mitosis" is the mitotic index; a short BrdU or ³H-thymidine pulse is the S-phase labelling index. The fraction you were handed belongs to the phase it names and to no other.
  2. Then it is one multiplication. tphase = (fraction in that phase) × Tc. Work in hours, convert to minutes at the very end. If you find yourself needing a second step you have misread the stem, not met a harder question.
  3. Re-read what was actually asked before you pick. The signature distractor in this question type is a correct calculation of the wrong quantity: (1 − MI) × Tc is interphase, and it will be sitting in the option list looking authoritative. Mitosis, interphase, S, and G1 + G2 are four different answers built from the same two numbers.
  4. Sanity-check against the reference proportions. In the 24-hour reference cell G1 is about half the cycle (11/24 ≈ 46 %), S about a third (8/24 ≈ 33 %), G2 about a sixth, and M only about 4 % — one hour in twenty-four. An answer that makes mitosis a fifth of the cycle is almost certainly the labelling index misapplied.
  5. Kill options on shape, without solving. Any option longer than Tc is dead. Any option in which the phases quoted cannot sum to Tc is dead. With five options and −0.25 for a wrong answer, striking even one makes answering strictly better than leaving it blank — so never blank one of these.
  6. Do not memorise the phase order, and do not build a hook for it. G1 → S → G2 → M follows from the three constraints above: copy before you split, license only while CDK activity is low and fire only when it is high, and take the expensive decision before the expensive work. A mnemonic here would be a second thing to remember in place of a sentence you can reconstruct.
Go deeper · the cycles that break these rules

The 24-hour four-phase cycle is a mammalian somatic cell in culture. Several important cycles are not that, and each exception clarifies what the phases are for.

  • Early embryonic cleavage divisions in Xenopus or Drosophila alternate S and M with essentially no G1 or G2, cycling every 8–30 minutes. They can do this because the egg is enormous and pre-stocked with every protein and every dNTP the embryo will need — there is nothing to grow and nothing to sense. The gaps, in other words, exist for growth and surveillance, not for the mechanics of replication or segregation. These cycles also run without functional checkpoints until the mid-blastula transition, which is why they are so fast.
  • Endoreduplication — repeated S phases with no intervening mitosis — makes polyploid cells on purpose. Drosophila salivary gland cells reach 1024C or more (the polytene chromosomes on which the first gene maps were built); the plant endosperm, the mammalian trophoblast and hepatocytes all do versions of it. Megakaryocytes reach 64C. This is the licensing logic being deliberately overridden.
  • Budding yeast commits at START in late G1 and lacks a clear G2 in the mammalian sense; it also keeps its nuclear envelope intact through mitosis (closed mitosis), with the spindle organised by an embedded spindle pole body rather than a centrosome.
  • Prokaryotes have no cell cycle in this sense. There is no mitosis, no spindle and no nuclear envelope. Binary fission replicates a single circular chromosome from one origin bidirectionally while the two copies are actively partitioned, and the septum is built by a contractile ring of FtsZ — a tubulin homologue, which is a nice reminder that the cytoskeletal proteins are older than the eukaryotic cell. Fast-growing E. coli even starts a new round of replication before the previous one finishes, so a cell can contain more than two origins.
§ 02

Counting chromosomes, counting DNA

The ideaThere are two separate things you can count in a dividing cell, and they do not change at the same moment. One is how many chromosomes there are, which is really a count of how many centromeres there are. The other is how much DNA there is. Copying the DNA doubles the second number without touching the first — a copied chromosome is still one chromosome, just a thicker one. Almost every trick question on this topic is built by asking for one count at a moment when only the other one has just changed.

Definition & key facts

Chromosome
One DNA molecule with its associated proteins, defined operationally by having one centromere. A replicated chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at that single centromere — and is therefore still one chromosome.
chromosome number = number of centromeres · C = DNA of one unreplicated haploid set two independent counters; learn to update them separately
Full definition, symbols and units
Sister chromatids
The two identical copies produced by replication of one chromosome, held together along their length by cohesin rings loaded behind the fork in S phase. They become separate chromosomes the instant that cohesion is destroyed.
Homologous chromosomes (homologues)
The maternal and paternal members of a pair: same length, same centromere position, same gene loci in the same order — but not necessarily the same alleles. Sisters are copies; homologues are versions.
Ploidy (n, 2n)
The number of complete homologous sets present. Haploid (n) = one set; diploid (2n) = two. Ploidy is not a chromosome count — it is a statement about how many versions of the genome are present.
C-value
The DNA content of one unreplicated haploid genome. A human somatic cell in G1 is 2n and 2C; in G2 it is still 2n but 4C. Human 1C ≈ 3.1 × 109 bp ≈ 3.2 pg, so 2C ≈ 6.3 pg.

Where each counter moves

Set up the discipline once and it never fails you. Ask three questions of any stage, in this order:

  1. Has S phase happened? If yes, every chromosome has two chromatids and the DNA content is double what it was. If not, one chromatid each.
  2. Has cohesion at the centromere been destroyed? If yes — that is, at anaphase of mitosis or anaphase II — every former chromatid now has its own centromere and counts as its own chromosome. The number of chromosomes doubles at that instant. No DNA is created or destroyed.
  3. Has the cytoplasm divided? Only then does the DNA content per cell fall.

Notice what this implies: replication and cohesin cleavage each move one counter and leave the other untouched, so for those two events the counters never change together; and the DNA content only ever changes at two moments — it doubles in S, and it halves at each cytokinesis. Cytokinesis is the single event that moves both counters at once. That is the whole of the bookkeeping. Everything below is that rule applied.

Four ways this is set as a trap

"A cell at the end of meiosis I is haploid, so it must be 1C." No. It is n but 2C — haploid in chromosome sets, but every one of those 23 chromosomes still carries two chromatids. This is the single most examined line in the whole table. Ploidy counts sets of homologues; C counts DNA. A cell can be haploid and carry twice a haploid genome's worth of DNA, and for the whole of meiosis II it does.

"At anaphase II the cell becomes diploid again." It has 46 chromosomes, but they are 23 pairs of identical chromosomes, not 23 pairs of homologues. Diploidy means two versions of the genome, one from each parent. The count doubled; the ploidy did not. The same distinction is why calling a mitotic anaphase cell "tetraploid" is at best a statement about the count.

"Sister chromatids are genetically identical." True at the moment they are made, and false thereafter in two ways that matter. Crossing over in prophase I swaps segments between non-sister chromatids, so after a crossover the two sisters of a meiotic chromosome differ distal to the exchange — which is exactly why meiosis II is not a pointless repetition. And replication itself is not error-free. The textbook sentence is a simplification of a starting condition, not a permanent property.

"Each chromosome consists of two chromatids." Only between S phase and anaphase. In G1, in G0, and in a gamete, each chromosome is a single chromatid. A diagram of "a chromosome" drawn as an X is a diagram of a replicated, condensed chromosome, which is a state that exists for about an hour out of every twenty-four.

Worked example 2 — bookkeeping in an unfamiliar organism

A plant has 2n = 18 and a G1 nucleus containing 12.0 pg of DNA. Give the chromosome number, chromatid number and DNA mass for (a) a root-tip cell at metaphase of mitosis, (b) a cell at anaphase of mitosis, (c) a cell at metaphase I, (d) a cell at metaphase II, (e) a mature pollen nucleus. (f) How many chromosomes are in a triploid (3n) endosperm nucleus of this species?

  1. Fix the constants first. G1 is by definition 2n and 2C, so 2C = 12.0 pg, hence 1C = 6.0 pg and 4C = 24.0 pg. And n = 9.
  2. (a) Metaphase of mitosis. S has happened, cohesion is intact: 18 chromosomes, 36 chromatids, 24.0 pg (4C), 2n.
  3. (b) Anaphase of mitosis. Cohesin cleaved, cytoplasm not yet divided: 36 chromosomes, 36 chromatids, 24.0 pg (4C). The mass is unchanged from (a) — nothing was synthesised or lost, only a linkage was cut.
  4. (c) Metaphase I. Identical counts to (a): 18 chromosomes, 36 chromatids, 24.0 pg, 2n — but arranged as 9 bivalents rather than 18 single files. The counts cannot distinguish metaphase I from mitotic metaphase; only the arrangement can.
  5. (d) Metaphase II. One division has occurred: 9 chromosomes, 18 chromatids, 12.0 pg (2C), n. Note the collision of intuitions — 12.0 pg is the same mass as the original G1 cell, but this cell is haploid.
  6. (e) Mature pollen nucleus. A product of meiosis: 9 chromosomes, 9 chromatids, 6.0 pg (1C), n. (Strictly, a pollen grain contains a generative and a tube nucleus produced by a mitotic division of the microspore — each is still n and 1C.)
  7. (f) Endosperm. 3n = 3 × 9 = 27 chromosomes, formed in the angiosperm double fertilisation when one sperm nucleus fuses with the two polar nuclei.

The one-line rule

Replication changes C, never n. Cutting cohesin changes the chromosome count, never C. Cytokinesis changes both. If you can say which of those three has just happened, you can fill in any row of the table without having memorised it.

Exam technique · the "how many at stage X" question

  1. Run the three questions, in order, before you look at the options. Has S happened? Has cohesion at the centromere been destroyed? Has the cytoplasm divided? Three yes/no answers fix all four numbers. Recalling the row of a table is slower and fails on an unfamiliar organism; the three questions never do.
  2. Use the chromatid–C identity as a free filter. A chromatid is one DNA molecule, so chromatids = n × (the C number) at every stage of both divisions without exception. For a human cell that means 1C ↔ 23 chromatids, 2C ↔ 46, 4C ↔ 92. Any option whose chromatid count divided by n does not equal its stated C value is internally inconsistent — strike it without deciding what the stage is.
  3. Use the legal-values filter. In a human cell the chromosome count at any stage of mitosis or meiosis is 23, 46 or 92 and nothing else. An option offering 69, or 92 in a gamete, or 46 chromatids alongside 4C, is dead on arrival. Generate the three legal counts from the organism's 2n the moment you read the stem.
  4. Use the "counters move separately" filter. Cytokinesis is the only event that moves both counters at once. If an option has the chromosome number and the DNA content both changing at a stage that is not a cytokinesis, it is wrong on structure alone.
  5. Know the two stages that exist to be traps. A cell at the end of meiosis I is n but 2C — haploid and still two chromatids per chromosome. A cell at anaphase II has 46 chromosomes and is still n — the count doubled, the ploidy did not. Options that tidily "resolve" either of those paradoxes are the wrong answer, every time.
  6. This is where the time is worth spending. Give these the full 66 seconds if you need it: the same three questions answer counting items in mitosis, in meiosis, in an unfamiliar plant or insect, and the gamete accounting in § 08. Time banked on the recognition questions in § 07 is what pays for it.
§ 03

Mitosis, stage by stage

The ideaMitosis is a sorting problem solved with rope. The cell first glues each copy to its twin, then builds a scaffold of fibres from two opposite poles, catches every pair on the fibres so that the two halves of each pair are pulled in opposite directions, checks that every last one is caught properly, and only then cuts the glue. Cutting the glue is what actually separates them — the pulling was already happening and doing nothing. Once you see that segregation is triggered by a pair of scissors rather than by a tug, the stages fall into a sequence that has to be in that order.

Definition & key facts

Mitosis
Division of the nucleus into two genetically identical daughter nuclei. It is not the division of the cell — that is cytokinesis, a separate process that usually but not always follows.
prophase → prometaphase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase "PMAT", with prometaphase inserted — the stage in which the envelope breaks and kinetochores are captured
Full definition, symbols and units
Centromere / kinetochore
The centromere is a DNA region, epigenetically specified by the histone H3 variant CENP-A. The kinetochore is the protein plate built on it — roughly a hundred proteins including the Ndc80 complex — which is what microtubules actually attach to. One kinetochore per chromatid, so two per replicated chromosome.
Cohesin / condensin
Two ring-shaped SMC complexes with opposite jobs. Cohesin encircles the two sisters and holds them together from S phase until anaphase. Condensin compacts each chromatid into the rod visible at metaphase. Cohesion is not condensation.
Spindle
The bipolar array of microtubules that segregates chromosomes. Minus ends at the poles, plus ends outward, in continuous dynamic instability — growing and shrinking on a timescale of seconds.
Centrosome
The main animal microtubule-organising centre: a pair of centrioles in a γ-tubulin-rich pericentriolar matrix. It duplicates in S. Plants and higher fungi have no centrioles and no centrosome, and build perfectly good spindles without one.

What triggers anaphase — and why the usual phrasing is misleading

Older textbooks say "the centromeres divide" or "the chromosome splits" at anaphase, as though the chromosome were being pulled in two. Nothing splits. The spindle has been pulling on the kinetochores since prometaphase and could not separate the sisters, because cohesin rings were holding them. What happens at the metaphase-to-anaphase transition is a proteolytic cascade: the ubiquitin ligase APC/C, activated by Cdc20, destroys securin; securin was the inhibitor of the protease separase; free separase cleaves the Scc1 subunit of cohesin; the rings open and the pre-existing tension does the rest in seconds. If you are asked what causes anaphase, the answer is regulated protein degradation, and the same fact explains why a drug that blocks APC/C arrests cells in metaphase with the spindle fully assembled and under load.

Plausible sentences worth rejecting

"Plant cells divide without a spindle." They divide without centrioles, which is not the same thing. Plant cells build a completely normal bipolar spindle, nucleated by γ-tubulin distributed over the nuclear envelope and cortex rather than concentrated in a centrosome. The general principle worth carrying: the centrosome is a convenient organiser, not a requirement — even animal cells whose centrosomes are laser- ablated can assemble a functional spindle by a chromatin-directed pathway, and female meiosis in mice and humans is normally acentrosomal.

"Chromosomes line up at the metaphase plate because the spindle pushes them there." Alignment is an equilibrium: a chromosome nearer one pole feels a stronger poleward pull from the far side and a stronger polar-ejection force pushing it away from the near pole, so it drifts to the middle. It is a balance of forces, which is why metaphase chromosomes oscillate about the plate rather than sitting still.

"Colchicine and taxol have opposite effects on the cell." They have opposite effects on tubulin — colchicine and the vinca alkaloids prevent polymerisation, taxanes prevent depolymerisation — but the same effect on the cell, which is arrest in mitosis, because the spindle-assembly checkpoint monitors correct dynamic attachment and tension. A frozen spindle satisfies the checkpoint no better than an absent one. Both classes are therefore mitotic poisons, and both cause the peripheral neuropathy you would expect from drugs that attack microtubules in long axons.

Go deeper · how the spindle finds every kinetochore, and how errors are corrected

Search and capture on its own is too slow: random microtubule probing of the whole nuclear volume would take far longer than the ten or twenty minutes prometaphase actually lasts. Three accelerants close the gap. Chromatin itself generates a gradient of Ran-GTP around the chromosomes that locally stabilises microtubule nucleation, so the probability of a hit rises steeply near the target. Microtubules can be nucleated from existing microtubules by augmin, amplifying the array where it is already succeeding. And chromosome arms carry plus-end-directed chromokinesins that push chromosomes out of the polar region into the field of growing microtubules.

The more interesting problem is error correction, because capture is random and several wrong geometries are possible:

  • Monotelic — only one of the two kinetochores attached. Normal early intermediate.
  • Syntelic — both kinetochores attached to the same pole. Would send both sisters to one daughter.
  • Merotelic — one kinetochore attached to both poles. The dangerous one, because it generates tension and so is poorly detected by the checkpoint; it produces lagging chromosomes at anaphase and is a major source of aneuploidy in cancer cells.
  • Amphitelic — correct bi-orientation.

The correction machinery keys on tension. Aurora B kinase sits at the inner centromere; a kinetochore that is not under tension stays close to Aurora B and gets phosphorylated on the Ndc80 complex, which weakens the microtubule grip and releases the attachment for another try. Correct bi-orientation stretches the kinetochore away from the inner centromere, out of Aurora B's reach, and the attachment is stabilised. It is an elegant piece of engineering: the same physical signal that means "this is right" is what removes the enzyme that would undo it.

The checkpoint itself is separate but connected. An unattached kinetochore recruits Mad1–Mad2 and BubR1 and catalytically generates a diffusible mitotic checkpoint complex that sequesters Cdc20. One single unattached kinetochore in the whole cell is enough to hold the entire cell in metaphase — which tells you the signal must be catalytic and amplified, not stoichiometric.

§ 04

Cytokinesis: animal versus plant

The ideaOnce the two sets of chromosomes are apart, the cell still has to become two cells, and here plants and animals take opposite routes for a purely mechanical reason. An animal cell is a soft bag, so it can be squeezed in two from the outside like tying off a balloon. A plant cell is inside a rigid box, so squeezing is impossible — it has to build a new wall across the middle instead, starting at the centre and extending outwards until it meets the old wall. Same outcome, opposite direction of travel, and that direction is what examiners ask about.

Two confusions that cost marks

"Telophase and cytokinesis are the same thing." Telophase is nuclear: envelopes re-form, chromosomes decondense. Cytokinesis is cytoplasmic and is a separate, overlapping process — it usually begins during anaphase, before telophase. More importantly it can be omitted entirely, and when it is, the result is a multinucleate cell. This is not pathological: it is how the Drosophila syncytial blastoderm, the coenocytic hyphae of many fungi, the endosperm of many seeds and the normally binucleate hepatocytes of the mammalian liver are made. Mitosis without cytokinesis gives more nuclei; S phase without mitosis (endoreduplication) gives more DNA per nucleus. They are different routes to a bigger cell.

"Plant cells form a furrow that is slowed by the cell wall." There is no furrow at all. Turgor pressure inside a walled cell is of the order of several hundred kilopascals pushing outward; an actin belt could not indent that even in principle, and plants have accordingly never evolved the option. The exam version of this distractor is usually phrased as a plausible-sounding compromise — "in plants the furrow is assisted by the cell plate" — and it is false in both halves.

There is no mnemonic for this section, and you do not need one

The direction of travel is derivable from the wall, so do not carry a hook for it — a hook is a direction you can invert under pressure, whereas a mechanical constraint is not. An animal cell is a soft bag: the only way to halve it is to squeeze from outside, so the furrow runs periphery → centre, and squeezing needs a motor — hence actin, myosin II and ATP. A plant cell sits inside a rigid box at several hundred kilopascals of turgor, where squeezing is impossible even in principle, so the partition has to be built outward from the middle, and building needs a delivery system — hence Golgi vesicles running on phragmoplast microtubules. Every remaining row of the table above falls out of that one sentence: the machinery, the membrane source, why the plant daughters never truly separate, even why the plane is set in G2 in a plant (the wall must be told in advance where to grow) and only at anaphase in an animal. If you can say "bag versus box", you can regenerate the whole table; if you can only say "outside-in, centre-out", you have one bit of information and a fifty-fifty chance of having it backwards.

§ 05

Meiosis: two divisions, one replication

The ideaSexual reproduction has an arithmetic problem: if two cells fuse every generation, the chromosome number would double every generation unless something halves it first. Meiosis is that halving. The trick is simple to state — copy the DNA once, then divide twice — but making the first of those divisions work required the cell to invent three things it does not use in mitosis: a way to find and physically link each chromosome to its partner, a way to make the two copies of a chromosome face the same pole instead of opposite ones, and a way to keep them glued at the middle while cutting them loose along the arms.

Definition & key facts

Meiosis
Two consecutive nuclear divisions following a single round of DNA replication, converting one diploid cell into four haploid products and generating new allele combinations while doing so.
2n, 2C → (S) → 2n, 4C → (MI) → n, 2C → (MII) → n, 1C × 4 meiosis I is reductional (2n → n); meiosis II is equational (n → n)

Prophase I is the long one, and it has five named substages

Prophase I occupies over 90 % of the time of meiosis — in a human male about a fortnight; in a human female, decades. Everything that makes meiosis different from mitosis is set up here. The five names are not arbitrary labels: each is Greek for what the threads look like at that moment — lepto- thin, zygo- yoked, pachy- thick, diplo- double, diakinesis the movement through into metaphase I. So thin → yoked → thick → double → through is the sequence, and it is also a description of the synaptonemal complex assembling, holding the pair while the exchange is made, and dissolving to leave chiasmata behind. Decoded that way the order does not need a separate hook at all — and two of the five carry the facts most often asked outright, crossing over being completed at pachytene and the human oocyte arresting at diplotene, which is what the whole of maternal-age aneuploidy hangs on. (The standard Chinese names 細線期・偶線期・粗線期・雙線期・終變期 encode the same morphology, so if you meet them the two sets cross-check each other.)

The three switches that make meiosis I different

Meiosis I takes the mitotic apparatus and changes exactly three things. If you can name them you can answer almost any mechanistic question about meiosis, including why non-disjunction happens.

  1. Homologues are physically linked. In mitosis, a chromosome and its homologue ignore each other completely — they are captured independently and it does not matter which pole either goes to. In meiosis I they must go to opposite poles, and the spindle has no way of knowing which chromosome is whose partner. The solution is to tie them together: pairing, synapsis, and at least one crossover per bivalent, so that the pair behaves as one object on the spindle exactly the way sisters do in mitosis. A bivalent that fails to form a chiasma — an achiasmate bivalent — has no such linkage, and its two homologues are then captured independently and may well go to the same pole. This is a leading cause of human aneuploidy.
  2. Sister kinetochores co-orient. In mitosis the two kinetochores of a chromosome face opposite directions and attach to opposite poles. In meiosis I they are fused into a single functional unit (monopolin in yeast, meikin in mice) that faces one way, so both sisters go together. Without this the whole scheme collapses.
  3. Cohesion is released in two steps. Separase cleaves the meiotic cohesin Rec8 along the arms at anaphase I — which is precisely what releases the chiasmata and lets the homologues part — while cohesin at the centromere is protected by shugoshin, which recruits the phosphatase PP2A to keep Rec8 there dephosphorylated and therefore uncleavable. Shugoshin is removed at anaphase II, centromeric cohesin is cleaved, and the sisters finally separate. Two divisions off one replication requires exactly this: glue released in two stages.

"When does the chromosome number halve?"

The number of chromosomes per cell halves at cytokinesis after meiosis I; the event that makes that possible — the separation of homologues, and hence the loss of one member of each pair from each future cell — is anaphase I. Both answers appear in exam options and the distinction is usually resolved by how the stem is worded: if it asks when reduction occurs, the answer is anaphase I; if it asks when a cell first contains the haploid number, the answer is at the end of telophase I. What is never correct is meiosis II: a cell entering meiosis II is already haploid. Meiosis II changes the DNA content per cell, not the ploidy.

Which of the two divisions does what

I — arms and homologues halve the count · II — centromeres and sisters halve only the amount

Arms and homologues belong to meiosis I: separase cuts cohesin along the chromosome arms, which is precisely what releases the chiasmata, and the two homologues then part. Centromeres and sisters belong to meiosis II: centromeric cohesin has been shielded all along by shugoshin, it is cut only now, and the sisters finally separate — which is why anaphase II and a mitotic anaphase are mechanically the same event and anaphase I is the odd one out. Count versus amount is the reductional/equational distinction in this page's own two-counter language: meiosis I halves the chromosome count, 2n → n, and is therefore the reductional division, while meiosis II leaves the count alone and halves only the DNA amount, 2C → 1C, and is therefore equational. The word only is load-bearing and is where the trap is: both divisions halve C, but just the first halves n. My own, though reductional/equational is the standard pair of terms. Two things it deliberately does not encode. It does not say when the cell becomes haploid — the separation is at anaphase I but the cell first contains n only after cytokinesis I, and the callout above is where that distinction is decided. And it says nothing about co-orientation of sister kinetochores at meiosis I, which is the third of the three switches and the one that makes the homologue split possible at all.

Oocytes stop dividing before the woman is born and then sit in diplotene for decades, held together by cohesin complexes that were loaded during fetal life and are not replaced. Textbook sentences that are only locally true

"Meiosis produces gametes." True for animals. In plants and fungi meiosis produces spores, which then divide mitotically to build a multicellular haploid generation — the gametophyte — and it is that haploid organism which finally makes gametes, by mitosis. So in a flowering plant, the pollen grain's sperm nuclei are produced by mitosis, not meiosis. A question that asks "which process produces the gametes of a moss?" has the answer mitosis, and it is designed to catch exactly this. Note 08 and note 09 develop the alternation of generations properly.

"Meiosis II is just mitosis." Mechanically it is very similar, and that similarity is worth having. But three things differ and any of them can be the point of a question: the cell is haploid, so there are no homologues present at all; there was no S phase before it, so the chromatids being separated are decades old in an oocyte; and the two chromatids are not identical wherever a crossover occurred in prophase I, which is what stops meiosis II from being a redundant step.

"A secondary oocyte is haploid, so it has half the DNA of a body cell." It is haploid (n = 23) and it has 2C — the same DNA mass as a G1 somatic cell, because each of its 23 chromosomes still carries two chromatids. It only becomes 1C after the second polar body is extruded, which in humans happens only if a sperm arrives. An unfertilised secondary oocyte never completes meiosis at all.

§ 06

Where the variation comes from

The ideaTwo entirely separate mechanisms, acting at two different moments, make every gamete different. One shuffles within each chromosome, by physically swapping matching segments between the maternal and paternal copies. The other shuffles between chromosomes, because when the pairs line up to be pulled apart, each pair decides independently which way round it faces. The first creates chromosomes that never existed before; the second deals out existing chromosomes into new hands. Both happen in meiosis I, and neither happens in mitosis at all.

Definition & key facts

Crossing over
Reciprocal exchange of corresponding segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, completed in pachytene of prophase I. It produces recombinant chromatids carrying new combinations of alleles that were previously linked on one parental chromosome.
gamete types from assortment alone = 2n · zygote types = 2n × 2n = 22n human: 223 = 8 388 608 gametes · 246 ≈ 7.0 × 1013 zygotes
Full definition, symbols and units
Independent assortment
Each bivalent orients on the metaphase I plate independently of every other, so which pole a given maternal chromosome goes to is decided separately for each of the n pairs. With n pairs there are 2n equally likely combinations. This is the physical basis of Mendel's second law — and it holds only for genes on different chromosomes, or far apart on the same one.
Random fertilisation
Any one of the 2n possible sperm may fertilise any one of the 2n possible eggs, squaring the number of possible zygotes. This is not a property of meiosis, but it is the third source of variation exam questions expect you to list.
Chiasma frequency
Every bivalent gets at least one crossover — the obligate chiasma — and typically one to three. Genome-wide this is about 50 crossovers per male meiosis and 70–90 per female meiosis in humans, consistent with sex-averaged genetic map lengths of roughly 3500 cM.
Crossover interference
One crossover suppresses the formation of a second nearby, so crossovers are more evenly spaced along a chromosome than random. This is why observed double-crossover frequencies fall below the product of the two single-crossover frequencies.

Why 2n, and why crossing over makes the number meaningless

The 2n argument is a counting argument and worth doing slowly once. Each bivalent on the metaphase I plate can face either of two ways: maternal-left/paternal-right, or the reverse. That is two choices, made independently, for each of the n bivalents — so 2 × 2 × … × 2 = 2n arrangements of the plate, each giving a different pair of daughter cells. For humans, 223 = 8 388 608. Any two sperm from the same man have a 1 in 8.4 million chance of carrying the same set of whole parental chromosomes.

Then add crossing over, and the argument breaks down in an informative way. Once segments have been exchanged, the chromosome a gamete receives is not "the maternal chromosome 7" or "the paternal chromosome 7" but a mosaic of both, with the switch points falling at slightly different places every meiosis. The number of possible gametes stops being 223 and becomes, for practical purposes, unbounded — which is why 2n is the floor, not the answer. Examiners exploit this by asking for "the number of genetically different gametes" (unbounded, because of crossing over) versus "the number of different chromosomal combinations" or "assuming no crossing over" (2n). Read the stem for which one is being asked.

Worked example 3 — variation arithmetic in three organisms

(a) A fruit fly has 2n = 8. Ignoring crossing over, how many chromosomally distinct gametes can one fly produce, and how many distinct zygotes can a mating pair produce? (b) Repeat for a human. (c) A human bivalent averages about 2.3 chiasmata in male meiosis. Roughly how many crossovers occur in one primary spermatocyte, and how does that compare with the male genetic map length of about 2700 cM?

  1. (a) 2n = 8, so n = 4 pairs. Gametes: 24 = 16. Zygotes: 16 × 16 = 28 = 256.
  2. (b) n = 23. Gametes: 223 = 8 388 608. Zygotes: 223 × 223 = 246 ≈ 7.0 × 1013 — about ten thousand times the current human population, from one couple, before any crossing over is considered.
  3. (c) 23 bivalents × 2.3 ≈ 53 crossovers per meiosis.
  4. Check it against the map. A single chiasma involves only 2 of the 4 chromatids in a bivalent, so it makes half the products recombinant for that interval — it contributes 50 cM, i.e. 0.5 M. So total map length in Morgans ≈ (chiasmata)/2 = 53 / 2 ≈ 26.5 M = 2650 cM, against a measured 2700 cM. The two independent routes agree, which is the real point: the genetic map is a count of crossovers in disguise.
  5. The commonest error here is to write "1 chiasma = 100 cM". It does not, and the factor of two is exactly the four-strand geometry of the bivalent. Four statements that look right and are not

"Crossing over occurs between sister chromatids." It occurs between non-sister chromatids of a homologous pair. Exchange between identical sisters would be genetically invisible — it would swap a segment for a copy of itself. Sister-chromatid exchange does happen and is measurable, but it produces no variation, which is precisely why it is not the answer.

"Independent assortment occurs at metaphase II." It occurs at metaphase I, when whole bivalents orient. By metaphase II each cell already contains a fixed haploid set; there are no homologous pairs left to assort. (Chromatids that differ because of crossovers do get dealt out at meiosis II, but that is the consequence of an earlier crossover, not independent assortment.)

"Crossing over occurs in mitosis too, so mitosis also generates variation." Mitotic recombination exists, but it is rare and unprogrammed — an accident of repair rather than a mechanism. Where it matters is in cancer: mitotic recombination is one of the standard routes to loss of heterozygosity at a tumour-suppressor locus, which is § 10's second hit. It is a pathological curiosity in the context of variation and a central mechanism in the context of cancer.

"The recombination frequency between two genes can be anything from 0 to 100 %." The maximum observable is 50 %, which is also what unlinked genes on different chromosomes give. So a measured RF of 50 % cannot distinguish "on different chromosomes" from "on the same chromosome but far apart" — you need intermediate markers to link them. This is the standard limitation of genetic mapping and is picked up properly in note 03.

Exam technique · 2n questions in under thirty seconds

  1. Halve first, and write it down. The stem gives you 2n almost every time; the exponent is n, the number of pairs. So 2n = 12 means 26 = 64, not 212. Physically writing "n = 6" on the paper before you do anything else prevents the single commonest error in this question type.
  2. Gametes 2n; zygotes are that squared. Zygote types = 2n × 2n = 22n. Check the two candidate options against each other — if the zygote figure is not the exact square of the gamete figure, at least one of them is wrong and you may not need to compute anything.
  3. Carry the ladder, not a calculator. 24 = 16 · 26 = 64 · 28 = 256 · 210 = 1024 · 212 = 4096 · 220 ≈ 106 · 223 = 8 388 608 · 246 ≈ 7.0 × 1013. Every version of this question is answered from that list.
  4. Recognise the two distractor shapes on sight. An option equal to 2n or to n (12, or 6) is testing whether you know the relation is exponential at all. An option equal to n! or (n!)2 — 720 and 518 400 for n = 6 — is testing whether you think assortment is a permutation. It is not: each pair makes one independent binary choice, so it is 2n, never n!.
  5. One word in the stem decides the answer. "Chromosomally distinct", "different chromosome combinations" or "assuming no crossing over" → 2n. "Genetically distinct" → effectively unbounded, because crossing over makes each chromosome a fresh mosaic; 2n is the floor, not the answer. If you cannot see that word, the question is not asking what you think it is.
  6. If the stem names a source of variation and asks for a stage, answer from a two-item list. Crossing over → pachytene of prophase I. Independent assortment → metaphase I (realised at anaphase I). "Assortment at metaphase II" is a permanent fixture of these option sets and is impossible — by then there are no homologues left to assort.
Go deeper · the molecular route from a deliberate double-strand break to a crossover

Recombination is repair, redirected. The steps are worth knowing because several of the proteins reappear as tumour suppressors in § 10.

  1. Spo11, a topoisomerase-VI relative, cuts both strands and stays covalently attached to the 5′ ends. It is removed with a short oligonucleotide by the MRN complex plus CtIP.
  2. Resection chews back the 5′ ends, leaving 3′ single-stranded tails hundreds of nucleotides long.
  3. Strand invasion. The tails are coated by RAD51 and the meiosis-specific DMC1, which search the homologue for a matching sequence and invade it, forming a displacement loop. Note the deliberate choice of template: mitotic homologous recombination prefers the sister chromatid; meiotic recombination is biased towards the homologue, because using the sister would achieve nothing.
  4. Resolution. The resulting double Holliday junction can be resolved two ways. Cut one way and you get a crossover — flanking markers are exchanged. Dissolve it, or cut the other way, and you get a non-crossover gene conversion: a short patch of sequence is copied from the homologue without exchanging the flanks. In humans only about a fifth of the initial breaks become crossovers — of the order of 250 breaks and 50 crossovers in a male meiosis — and the MutLγ complex (MLH1–MLH3) designates which ones will; its foci can be counted in spread nuclei as a direct read-out of crossover number.

Two consequences are examinable. Gene conversion violates Mendel's first law locally — in a fungal tetrad you can recover a 3:1 rather than a 2:2 ratio at a single locus, which is how the phenomenon was discovered. And the whole system explains why BRCA1 and BRCA2 matter: they load RAD51 onto resected ends. A cell without them cannot repair double-strand breaks by homologous recombination and falls back on error-prone routes, accumulating rearrangements — the reason BRCA-mutant tumours are so sensitive to PARP inhibitors, which force cells to rely on a repair pathway they no longer have.

Mitosis versus meiosis, line by line

The ideaThese two processes use most of the same parts, so the temptation is to learn them as two lists. Do the opposite: learn mitosis properly, then learn the short list of things meiosis changes. There are really only two changes — the pairs are made to line up together instead of separately, and the glue is released in two stages instead of one — and everything else on the comparison table is downstream of those two. If you can draw the metaphase plate correctly for each, you can reconstruct the rest.

Exam technique · "occurs in meiosis but not mitosis", and its EXCEPT twin

  1. Convert each option into a picture of the metaphase plate. Chromosomes paired and straddling the plate = meiosis I only. Chromosomes in single file = mitosis and meiosis II both, therefore shared, therefore not the answer to a "meiosis only" stem. One image sorts most option lists.
  2. Hold the meiosis-I-only set as a closed list — synapsis and the synaptonemal complex, bivalents / tetrads, chiasmata, programmed crossing over, co-orientation of sister kinetochores, cleavage of arm cohesin alone, and reduction of ploidy. Anything not on that list is shared. Seven items is a cheaper thing to carry than two full descriptions.
  3. Everything mechanical is shared, and that is the whole trick. Spindle, kinetochores, cohesin, condensin, APC/C, securin, separase, prior DNA replication, cytokinesis — all present in both.   4. On a negatively-worded stem, never hold the negation in your head. Mark each option T or F against the positive question in the margin, then take the odd one out.   5. Distrust an option because it sounds familiar. Recognition is not evidence; check that the sentence answers this stem.
  6. Ten seconds, not sixty. The moment an option is classified as shared or meiosis-only the question is finished. This is the section to answer fast so that the counting items in § 02 and § 08 get their full time.
§ 08

Non-disjunction and aneuploidy

The ideaMeiosis is a mechanical process and mechanical processes slip. When two chromosomes that should have gone to opposite ends go to the same end instead, one product ends up with an extra copy and the other with none. Fuse either with a normal gamete and the resulting person has three copies or one copy of that chromosome in every cell of their body. Because genes are dosage-sensitive, that is usually fatal before birth — and the handful of chromosomes on which it is survivable are survivable for reasons you can predict.

Definition & key facts

Non-disjunction
Failure of chromosomes to separate to opposite poles at anaphase: homologues at anaphase I, or sister chromatids at anaphase II or at a mitotic anaphase. The result is one daughter with an extra chromosome and one lacking it.
meiosis I error → 4 aneuploid products (2 × n+1, 2 × n−1) · meiosis II error → 2 normal, 1 × n+1, 1 × n−1 the accounting question, asked almost every year in some form
Full definition, symbols and units
Aneuploidy
A chromosome number that is not an exact multiple of the haploid set: 2n+1 (trisomy), 2n−1 (monosomy), 2n+2, and so on. Contrast euploidy, an exact multiple — n, 2n, 3n (triploidy), 4n (tetraploidy).
Anaphase lag
A chromosome that fails to reach a pole in time and is excluded from the reforming nucleus. It causes monosomy without producing a matching trisomy, and in a post-zygotic mitosis it is a common route to mosaicism.
Mosaicism
Two or more cell lines of different karyotype in one individual, arising from a mitotic error after fertilisation. Generally a milder phenotype, in proportion to the fraction and distribution of abnormal cells.
Robertsonian translocation
Fusion of two acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) at their centromeres, with loss of the tiny short arms. A balanced carrier has 45 chromosomes and is phenotypically normal, but can transmit an effective extra copy — the mechanism of the ≈4 % of Down syndrome that is not free trisomy and that carries a recurrence risk.

Meiosis I versus meiosis II errors — and how to tell them apart

The two errors give different numbers and different content, and both facts are testable.

A meiosis I error sends a whole bivalent to one pole. Both secondary cells are therefore already wrong before meiosis II begins: one has 24 chromosomes, the other 22. Each then divides normally, giving four aneuploid products — two with n+1 and two with n−1, none normal. And because the extra copies are the two homologues, they are genetically distinguishable: a marker heterozygous in the parent will appear as two different alleles in the gamete.

A meiosis II error affects one secondary cell only. That cell's sisters fail to part, so it yields one n+1 and one n−1 product, while the untouched secondary cell yields two normal ones — two of four abnormal. The two extra copies here are sisters, so at a marker locus the gamete carries the same allele twice, at least near the centromere. (Crossovers further out mean the distinction is reliable only for centromere-proximal markers, which is exactly why studies of trisomy origin genotype pericentromeric markers.)

The fraction, and the division it came from

Same allele, second division · the earlier the error, the wider the damage — I ruins 4 of 4, II ruins 2 of 4

Same allele, second division. That is the half of the rule worth alliterating, because it is the half that slips: two identical alleles at a marker means two sister chromatids, and sisters travel together only if they failed to part at anaphase II. Everything else is the other case — two different alleles means two homologues, which can only be in one gamete if they failed to part at anaphase I. The earlier the error, the wider the damage gives you the fractions without memorising them. An error at meiosis I sends a whole bivalent to one pole and so commits both secondary cells before meiosis II has even begun: 4 of 4 products aneuploid, two n+1 and two n−1, none normal. An error at meiosis II touches one secondary cell only: 2 of 4, one n+1 and one n−1, with two normal products from the untouched cell. My own phrasing, not a circulating hook — but the two fractions and the two marker patterns are exactly what is asked, in both directions. Three things it does not cover. The marker rule holds only for centromere-proximal markers, because crossovers reshuffle everything distal. It says nothing about a mitotic non-disjunction after fertilisation, which gives mosaicism rather than a uniform aneuploidy. And it does not cover anaphase lag, which produces a monosomy with no matching trisomy and is a common route to 45,X.

Worked example 4 — reading a trisomy backwards to its origin

A child with Down syndrome is genotyped at three markers close to the centromere of chromosome 21. At each marker the child has three alleles: two distinct maternal alleles and one paternal allele. (a) Which parent contributed the extra chromosome? (b) At which meiotic division did the error occur? (c) What fraction of the products of that meiosis were aneuploid? (d) What is the child's karyotype? (e) If instead the child's two maternal alleles had been identical at all three markers, what would you conclude?

  1. (a) Three alleles means three chromosome 21s, and two of them are maternal. The extra chromosome came from the mother — as it does in roughly 90 % of free trisomy 21.
  2. (b) The two maternal copies are different alleles, so they are the mother's two homologues, not two copies of one. Homologues end up in the same gamete only if they failed to separate at anaphase I. So this is a meiosis I error — the commoner class, accounting for about three-quarters of maternal errors.
  3. (c) A meiosis I error commits both secondary oocytes: 4 of 4 products aneuploid. In female meiosis three of the four are polar bodies, so the practical statement is that that oocyte was always going to be abnormal whichever product survived.
  4. (d) 47,XX,+21 or 47,XY,+21 — 46 + 1 = 47. Written with the total count first, then the sex chromosomes, then the abnormality.
  5. (e) Two identical maternal alleles at pericentromeric markers means two sister chromatids, so the failure was at anaphase II: a meiosis II error, with 2 of 4 products aneuploid. Because crossovers reshuffle distal segments, this inference is only sound close to the centromere — which is why the question specified markers near it.
  6. Sanity check on the arithmetic of recurrence: this is free trisomy, not translocation, so it is a sporadic accident. Recurrence risk after one affected child is about 1 % or the maternal age-specific risk, whichever is greater. Had karyotyping shown 46 chromosomes with a der(14;21), the picture would be completely different — a balanced parental carrier, with an empirical recurrence risk of roughly 10–15 % if the mother carries it and 1–2 % if the father does.

Why only some aneuploidies are survivable

Aneuploidy is common at conception — perhaps a quarter of all human conceptions are chromosomally abnormal — and almost all of it is lost early; it is the leading identified cause of first-trimester miscarriage. The survivors follow a pattern with a mechanistic explanation rather than an arbitrary list:

  • Autosomal monosomy is always lethal. One copy of a whole chromosome halves the dose of hundreds of genes at once, and unmasks every recessive lethal allele on the remaining copy.
  • Trisomies survive only for gene-poor chromosomes. Chromosome 21 is the smallest human autosome and among the least gene-dense; 18 and 13 are next. The trisomies that reach term are precisely those with the least dosage imbalance to impose — and even these have severe phenotypes, with trisomy 18 and 13 rarely surviving the first year.
  • Sex chromosome aneuploidy is comparatively mild for two specific reasons. Extra X chromosomes are largely silenced by X-inactivation (though not completely — the pseudoautosomal regions and other escapees explain why 47,XXY is not phenotypically normal). And the Y carries very few genes, so an extra one matters little. This is why 47,XXY, 47,XYY and 47,XXX are all compatible with a normal lifespan, and 45,X is the only viable human monosomy — although even that is survivable only rarely, with about 99 % of 45,X conceptuses lost in the first trimester.

Why maternal age, and only maternal age

The risk of trisomy 21 rises from roughly 1 in 1500 at maternal age 20 to about 1 in 350 at 35, 1 in 100 at 40 and 1 in 30 at 45 — a nine-fold rise between 30 and 40 alone. The mechanism follows directly from § 05: a woman's oocytes entered prophase I before she was born and have sat in dictyate arrest ever since, held together by cohesin loaded in fetal life and never replenished. Over decades cohesin is lost, chiasmata slip towards the chromosome ends and off, and bivalents that were meant to behave as single units on the spindle come apart into independently-orienting univalents. A bivalent that formed no chiasma at all, or whose only chiasma was very distal, is at risk from the start — which is why susceptible exchange patterns established in utero and cohesion loss over subsequent decades are usually described as a two-hit model. Spermatogonia, by contrast, replenish everything at every division, so paternal age contributes new point mutations rather than aneuploidy. Note also that 45,X is the exception that proves the rule: it is not maternal-age-related, because it usually arises from loss of a paternal chromosome.

Traps in this section

"Non-disjunction in meiosis II produces four abnormal gametes." Two. The other secondary cell was never involved. Conversely, "a meiosis I error produces two normal and two abnormal" is the mirror-image error — a meiosis I error produces none that are normal.

"Down syndrome is inherited." In 95 % of cases it is a sporadic accident of one meiosis, with no recurrence risk beyond the age-specific background. Only the ≈4 % caused by a familial Robertsonian translocation is genuinely heritable — and there the parent is a balanced carrier with 45 chromosomes and no phenotype. Distinguishing the two is the whole clinical point of karyotyping a child with Down syndrome.

"Polyploidy is always lethal." In animals, near enough — and in humans certainly. In plants polyploidy is routine, frequently advantageous, and a major mechanism of speciation: bread wheat is a hexaploid, and a great many crop species are polyploid. The reason for the animal–plant difference is worth knowing: sex determination in animals often depends on the ratio of sex chromosomes to autosomes, which polyploidy disturbs, whereas most plants are hermaphrodite and have no such constraint. An allopolyploid formed by hybridisation followed by genome doubling is instantly reproductively isolated from both parents while being perfectly fertile with itself — sympatric speciation in a single generation.

§ 09

Cyclins, CDKs and checkpoints

The ideaThe cell drives itself round the cycle with one family of enzymes that are always present but usually switched off. What switches them on is a partner protein that is made and then destroyed on a schedule — so the enzyme's activity rises and falls even though the enzyme itself never does. Different partners point the same enzyme at different jobs, which is how one mechanism produces an ordered sequence. On top of that sits a surveillance layer: if something is wrong, a signal is generated that holds the switch off until it is fixed. Those are the checkpoints, and losing them is most of what cancer is.

Definition & key facts

Cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)
A serine/threonine protein kinase that is catalytically inert on its own and becomes active only when bound to a cyclin. CDK protein levels are essentially constant through the cycle; cyclin levels oscillate. The oscillation of the partner is the clock.
CDK (constant) + cyclin (oscillating) → active kinase → phosphorylate the substrates of that phase synthesis sets the timing; ubiquitin-mediated destruction makes each step irreversible

Checkpoint
A surveillance mechanism that detects a specific defect and generates a signal that halts the cycle. Distinguish it from the restriction point, which is a decision about whether to proceed at all rather than a response to damage. A checkpoint is not part of the normal running of the cycle: cells with the checkpoint deleted cycle happily until something goes wrong.
MPF
Maturation-promoting factor, the activity first purified from frog oocytes that drives entry into mitosis; molecularly it is cyclin B–CDK1. It phosphorylates the nuclear lamins, condensin, histone H1 and dozens of others, and its destruction is what ends mitosis.
APC/C
Anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome — a ubiquitin ligase. With the adaptor Cdc20 it destroys securin and cyclin B at the metaphase-to-anaphase transition; with Cdh1 it keeps CDK activity low through late mitosis and G1, which is the window in which origins can be licensed.

Cyclin A–CDK2 sees that all is copied, and copied once only: it sustains S phase and blocks re-licensing of origins that have already fired. Cyclin B–CDK1 — MPF — breaks the cell apart, driving envelope breakdown, condensation and the spindle. The four initials are the cyclin names themselves in the order they act, which is the first thing to get right because it is not alphabetical; and the CDK numbers count down as the cycle runs forward: 4/6, then 2, then 2 again, then 1. Invented, not a standard hook, and worth checking once against the table above rather than taking on trust — the descending 4/6 → 2 → 2 → 1 is the load-bearing half, because the letters are easy and the numbers are what get mispaired. It covers the four complexes only. It does not encode the inhibitors (INK4 including p16 blocks CDK4/6; CIP/KIP including p21 and p27 blocks CDK2), nor which ligase destroys which cyclin — SCF takes cyclin E, APC/C takes cyclins A and B.

The five statements to be able to reject on sight

"CDK levels oscillate through the cycle." Cyclin levels oscillate; CDK levels are constant. This is the most frequently examined single fact in the section, and the reason for the naming — the kinase is dependent on the cyclin, so the cyclin is the variable.

"Phosphorylation of a CDK activates it." Which phosphorylation? Thr161 by CAK is activating; Tyr15 by Wee1 is inhibiting and dominant, which is why cells accumulate large stocks of fully assembled but inactive cyclin B–CDK1 during G2 and enter mitosis abruptly when Cdc25 strips that phosphate off. A generic statement about "phosphorylation" is not answerable, and options that make one are usually wrong.

"The spindle-assembly checkpoint detects DNA damage." It detects unattached kinetochores and lack of tension. DNA damage is monitored by the G1/S and G2/M checkpoints via ATM and ATR. A cell with badly damaged but fully attached chromosomes will sail through the SAC.

"Checkpoints are required for the cell cycle to run." They are required for it to run accurately. Early embryonic cleavage divisions run without functional checkpoints, and many cancer cell lines have lost several and cycle perfectly well — while accumulating the aneuploidy that follows.

"The restriction point is the G1 checkpoint." They coincide in position and are often conflated, but they answer different questions: R asks about growth factors and cell size, the G1/S checkpoint asks about DNA damage. A cell can pass one and be stopped by the other.

Which phosphate switches a CDK on, and which switches it off

Big number ON, small numbers OFF — and Cdc25 rubs the small ones out

Thr161, the big residue number, is added by CAK and is activating. Thr14 and Tyr15, the small numbers, are added by Wee1 and Myt1 and are inhibiting — and while they are present they win, which is why a G2 cell can hold a large stock of fully assembled, fully CAK-activated, completely inactive cyclin B–CDK1. The phosphatase Cdc25 strips the small ones off, and that removal is the actual trigger for mitotic entry. The kinase's name carries its own reminder: lose wee1 and the cell divides too early and therefore ends up too wee. The wee1 story is the standard etymology from fission yeast and is worth knowing as history as well as a hook; "big number on, small numbers off" is my own framing. It fixes the direction of three phosphorylations and nothing else. It does not encode the fact examined most often in this section — that CDK protein levels are constant while cyclin levels oscillate — which has to be held separately.

Exam technique · options you can kill on sight in this section

  1. "CDK levels rise and fall through the cycle" → dead. Cyclins oscillate; CDKs do not. Any option that makes the kinase the variable is wrong however plausible the rest of the sentence sounds, and this appears in some form in most option sets built from this section.
  2. "Phosphorylation activates it", with no residue named → dead. Thr161 activates, Thr14/Tyr15 inhibit, and the inhibitory pair dominates. An unqualified claim about "phosphorylation" is not answerable as stated, which is exactly why it is not the answer.
  3. Sort the stem into one of two wiring diagrams before reading the options. If it mentions kinetochore, attachment or tension, you are in SAC → MCC → Cdc20 → APC/C. If it mentions double-strand break, stalled fork or damage, you are in ATM/ATR → Chk2/Chk1 → p53 and Cdc25. The two diagrams do not meet, so "the spindle-assembly checkpoint detects DNA damage" is always wrong, and so is any option that puts p53 in charge of anaphase.
  4. "What causes anaphase?" → answer proteolysis, never force. These names decode themselves and need no mnemonic: APC/C–Cdc20 destroys securin, which was securing the sisters; freed separase then separates them by cutting cohesin. Any option describing a pull, a tug, or the centromere "splitting" or "dividing" is wrong — the tension had been there since prometaphase and was achieving nothing.
  5. Two drug stems, two opposite answers — read which one is inhibited. Block APC/C and the cell arrests in metaphase with a fully assembled spindle under load. Block the SAC (as in Mad2 loss) and anaphase runs early with kinetochores unattached, producing aneuploidy rather than arrest. Both outcomes usually appear in the same option list.
  6. Do not spend 66 seconds on a kinase name. The checkpoint logic — what is being asked, what happens if the answer is no — carries almost all the marks here. If an item turns on telling Chk1 from Chk2, eliminate what you can, answer anyway because elimination has already made that profitable, and move on.
Go deeper · why mitotic entry is a switch and not a dimmer

A cell must not be half in mitosis. Partial envelope breakdown or partial condensation would be worse than either state, so the transition needs to be all-or-none and, once made, hard to reverse. The circuit that achieves this is a textbook example of biological bistability, and it is built from two feedback loops wrapped around one enzyme.

Through G2 the cell accumulates cyclin B–CDK1 in an inactive state: assembled, activated by CAK on Thr161, but held off by Wee1-mediated phosphorylation of Tyr15. The removal of that phosphate by Cdc25 is the trigger. The crucial detail is that active CDK1 phosphorylates and activates Cdc25, and phosphorylates and inhibits Wee1. So a small amount of active CDK1 creates conditions that produce more active CDK1 — double positive feedback. Below a threshold of cyclin B, the loop cannot start and the system sits stably off; above it, the loop runs to completion in a minute or two and the system sits stably on. In between there is no stable state, which is exactly what "switch" means. The hysteresis is measurable: the cyclin concentration required to enter mitosis is higher than the concentration required to stay in it.

Exit is enforced by the opposite trick. APC/C–Cdc20 destroys cyclin B; because destruction is irreversible, the cell cannot slip back into mitosis, and the phosphatases (chiefly PP1 and PP2A-B55, themselves held down by CDK1 during mitosis and released as it falls) reverse every mitotic phosphorylation in a coordinated wave. Note the design principle that recurs throughout the cycle: an ordered irreversible sequence is built from reversible chemistry by coupling each step to proteolysis. The same principle is why the restriction point is irreversible (SCF-mediated destruction of p27) and why anaphase is irreversible (destruction of securin).

One clinical pay-off. Cells whose G1/S checkpoint is gone — that is, p53-mutant cells, which is roughly half of all cancers — become abnormally dependent on the G2/M checkpoint to survive DNA damage, because it is the only one they have left. That is the rationale for Wee1 and Chk1 inhibitors: abolish the remaining checkpoint and the damaged cell is driven into a catastrophic mitosis. It is the same synthetic-lethal logic as PARP inhibition in BRCA-mutant tumours.

§ 10

Cancer: broken accelerators, missing brakes

The ideaA cell decides whether to divide by weighing signals that say go against signals that say stop. Cancer is what happens when that arithmetic is corrupted — and there are only two ways to corrupt it. Either a go-signal gets jammed on, which takes one damaged copy of a gene because a jammed accelerator is jammed whatever the other copy does; or a stop-signal is lost, which usually takes both copies because one working brake is still a brake. That asymmetry — one hit versus two — explains the entire genetics of cancer, including why some families inherit it and most people do not.

Definition & key facts

Proto-oncogene → oncogene
A normal gene promoting proliferation (growth factors, receptors, signal transducers, transcription factors, cyclins). A gain-of-function mutation converts it to an oncogene. One mutant allele suffices — dominant at the level of the cell.
oncogene: gain of function, 1 hit, dominant · tumour suppressor: loss of function, 2 hits, recessive "accelerator stuck down" versus "both brake cables cut" — at the level of the cell
Full definition, symbols and units
Tumour suppressor gene
A gene whose normal product restrains proliferation or maintains genome integrity. Cancer requires loss of function of both alleles. Both alleles of a tumour-suppressor gene must be inactivated. A sporadic case needs two independent somatic hits in the same cell — rare, hence late and usually unifocal. Loss of heterozygosity (LOH)
The usual mechanism of the second hit: not a second point mutation but loss of the whole normal allele, by deletion, by mitotic recombination, by chromosome loss, or by epigenetic silencing through promoter hypermethylation.

Rb and p53: one node each, doing quite different jobs

Rb is the switch at the restriction point, and the direction of its regulation is the thing to get right. Unphosphorylated (hypophosphorylated) Rb binds E2F and holds it inert on the promoters of S-phase genes — this is Rb working, brake on. Cyclin D–CDK4/6 and then cyclin E–CDK2 phosphorylate Rb; hyperphosphorylated Rb releases E2F; E2F transcribes the genes that commit the cell to S phase, including cyclin E itself, which is the positive feedback that makes the transition irreversible. So phosphorylation inactivates Rb. Losing RB1 altogether has the same effect as permanent phosphorylation: E2F is never restrained. Note that the p16–cyclin D–CDK4/6–Rb–E2F axis is disrupted in the overwhelming majority of human cancers, but almost always at only one node — because breaking any one of them breaks the chain.

p53 is the damage-response hub, "the guardian of the genome". Undamaged cells keep it at very low levels because MDM2 continuously ubiquitinates it for destruction; the protein's half-life is minutes. DNA damage activates ATM or ATR, which phosphorylate both p53 and MDM2, breaking their interaction — so p53 accumulates within minutes without any new transcription of TP53 itself. It then acts as a transcription factor with four possible outputs: arrest (via p21), repair, senescence, or apoptosis (via PUMA, NOXA, BAX). Which one is chosen depends on the extent of damage and the cell type, which is why "p53 causes apoptosis" is only a quarter right. Germline TP53 loss gives Li–Fraumeni syndrome: sarcomas, premenopausal breast cancer, brain tumours and adrenocortical carcinoma, often several in one person.

The exception that makes "tumour suppressors are recessive" a simplification

Two qualifications are genuinely examinable. First, p53 works as a tetramer, so many TP53 missense mutants in the DNA-binding domain are dominant-negative: the mutant subunit poisons the whole complex, and one mutant allele is enough to cripple the protein. Some of these mutants acquire additional gain-of-function activities of their own. This is why TP53 mutations in tumours are overwhelmingly missense, whereas RB1 and APC mutations are overwhelmingly truncating — the mutation spectrum tells you the mechanism. Second, some tumour suppressors are haploinsufficient: half the normal dose is not enough, so losing one allele already contributes to the phenotype (p27 is the standard example). The clean one-hit/two-hit dichotomy is a good first model and an incomplete one, and an option that states it as an exceptionless law should raise your suspicion.

Worked example 5 — how many hits, from an age–incidence curve

(a) The incidence of colorectal cancer rises roughly as the fifth power of age. By what factor does incidence rise between age 40 and age 80, and how many rate-limiting mutational events does this imply? (b) A child inherits one mutant RB1 allele. Why does this shift the tumour to early childhood and make it bilateral, when the sporadic form is later and unilateral?

  1. (a) The Armitage–Doll relation is I(t) ∝ tk−1, where k is the number of independent rate-limiting events. A log–log slope of 5 gives k − 1 = 5, so k = 6 events.
  2. The fold-rise from 40 to 80: (80/40)5 = 25 = 32. Incidence rises about thirty-two-fold across those forty years — which is why cancer is overwhelmingly a disease of later life, and why the age distribution alone was strong evidence for multistage carcinogenesis decades before any of the genes were known.
  3. Sanity check on the exponent: if only one mutation were required, k = 1 and incidence would be flat with age; each additional required event adds one power. A steeply rising incidence curve is direct evidence that several things must go wrong in the same cell.
  4. (b) Sporadic retinoblastoma requires two somatic hits at RB1 in the same retinoblast. If the per-cell probability of one hit is p, the probability of two is of order p2 — vanishingly small, so at most one focus arises, in one eye, and only after enough cell-years have passed.
  5. A carrier already has one hit in every retinoblast, so each cell needs only one further event, probability of order p. With millions of retinoblasts, several will succeed — hence multiple foci, in both eyes, and early (median about 15 months, against roughly 24–30 months for sporadic disease).
  6. The general form of the result is worth carrying: an inherited first hit converts a two-hit problem into a one-hit problem, and squaring a small probability is what separates "rare and late and single" from "common in that individual, early and multiple". The same reasoning explains the hundreds of synchronous polyps in familial adenomatous polyposis.

Plausible sentences about cancer that are false

"Cancer cells divide faster than normal cells." Very often they do not. Many solid tumours have cell-cycle times longer than those of normal intestinal crypt cells, basal keratinocytes or haematopoietic progenitors. What distinguishes a cancer cell is not speed but that it does not stop — it ignores growth-inhibitory signals, has escaped density-dependent (contact) inhibition and anchorage dependence, does not enter G0, and does not die. Tumour growth is governed by the balance of the growth fraction against the cell-loss fraction, not by cycle time. The clinical corollary is important: cytotoxic chemotherapy is selective for proliferating cells, not for malignant ones, which is precisely why marrow suppression, mucositis and alopecia are its dose-limiting toxicities — those normal tissues are dividing faster than the tumour.

"A single mutation causes cancer." Six or so rate-limiting events for a typical adult carcinoma, accumulated clonally over decades — which is what the age–incidence curve above measures. The exceptions are informative rather than contradictory: some childhood leukaemias and sarcomas need very few events, and a few are driven by a single translocation in a cell that is already primed.

"Most cancers are inherited." Roughly 5–10 % have a strong hereditary component. The rest are somatic, arising from replication errors, mutagen exposure and time. What is inherited in the 5–10 % is not the cancer but a head start — a first hit already present in every cell.

"A benign tumour is one that grows slowly." The distinction is invasion, not rate. A benign tumour stays encapsulated and localised, however fast it grows; a malignant one breaches the basement membrane, invades locally and metastasises. Carcinoma in situ is malignant in cytology and behaviour but has not yet crossed the basement membrane, which is exactly why it is worth detecting.

Exam technique · direction of change as a filter

  1. Classify the gene, then check the direction the option claims. Accelerators — RAS, MYC, ERBB2/HER2, CCND1, EGFR, BRAF — cause cancer by gain. Brakes — RB1, TP53, APC, PTEN, NF1, CDKN2A, BRCA1/2 — cause it by loss. An option pairing "loss of an oncogene" or "an activating mutation in a tumour suppressor" with a tumour is wrong before you have read the second half of the sentence. On a five-option item this usually kills one or two outright, which is all the negative marking requires to make answering worthwhile.
  2. Then check the allele count, which follows from the direction. Gain of function needs one allele and is dominant in the cell; loss of function needs two and is recessive in the cell. Strike any option that gives a tumour suppressor one hit or an oncogene two. There is no mnemonic worth carrying here — jammed accelerator versus both brake cables cut generates it every time.
  3. "Dominant in the family, recessive in the cell" answers every Knudson stem. Early, bilateral or multifocal disease → an inherited first hit in every cell. Late, unilateral, single focus → sporadic, two somatic hits in one cell. You need the direction of the comparison, not the p versus p² arithmetic, unless the stem asks for it explicitly.
  4. Get Rb's direction right, because the trap is unchanged year after year. Hypophosphorylated Rb is the brake working; phosphorylation by cyclin D–CDK4/6 then cyclin E–CDK2 takes the brake off. Any option in which phosphorylation activates Rb, or in which p16 inhibits E2F directly rather than acting upstream on CDK4/6, is the intended error.
  5. p53 has four outputs, not one. Arrest (via p21), repair, senescence, apoptosis. An option asserting that p53's role is apoptosis is a quarter right, which on this paper is wrong. Equally wrong: "p53 is induced by transcription of TP53" — it is stabilised post-translationally by escaping MDM2, within minutes and with no new TP53 message.
  6. Translocation stems: look at the second number. The lymphoma translocations all move an oncogene next to the immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus on chromosome 14 — t(8;14) MYC in Burkitt, t(11;14) cyclin D1 in mantle cell, t(14;18) BCL2 in follicular. The one with no 14 in it is the one that is not a lymphoma: t(9;22), BCR-ABL, the Philadelphia chromosome, CML.

§ 11

Apoptosis, telomeres and senescence

The ideaA multicellular body needs cells to be able to die on command, and it needs a limit on how many times any one cell can divide. It has both. Every cell carries a self-destruct programme it can run in minutes, tidily, without spilling its contents and alarming the immune system. And every chromosome end is a countdown timer that shortens a little at each division, so that a lineage which has divided too many times triggers a permanent arrest. Cancer has to defeat both of these, and the ways it does so are as diagnostic as the mutations in the last section.

Definition & key facts

Apoptosis
Programmed cell death: an active, ATP-requiring, genetically encoded process that dismantles a cell from within and packages the remains for phagocytosis without provoking inflammation. Executed by caspases, cysteine proteases that cleave after aspartate residues.
intrinsic: damage → BH3-only → BAX/BAK → cytochrome c → Apaf-1 → caspase-9 → caspase-3 extrinsic: FasL/TNF → death receptor → FADD → caspase-8 → caspase-3 — two routes, one executioner
Full definition, symbols and units
Telomere
The repetitive DNA–protein cap at a chromosome end. In humans, tandem TTAGGG repeats, roughly 10–15 kb at birth, ending in a single-stranded 3′ G-rich overhang that folds back into a t-loop and is bound by the six-protein shelterin complex. Its job is to stop the cell mistaking a chromosome end for a double-strand break.
End-replication problem
Removing the final RNA primer on the lagging strand leaves a gap that no polymerase can fill, because DNA polymerases cannot start a chain and cannot work 3′→5′. Add the deliberate 5′-end resection that creates the overhang, and every round of replication costs 50–200 bp of telomere.
Replicative senescence (the Hayflick limit)
Permanent, non-reversible exit from the cycle after a characteristic number of divisions — about 50 ± 10 population doublings for human fibroblasts. It is triggered when telomeres become too short to be capped, so the end is recognised as damaged DNA and mounts a p53/p21 and p16 response. Senescent cells remain metabolically active and secrete an inflammatory programme (the SASP).
Telomerase
A reverse transcriptase (TERT) carrying its own RNA template (TERC), which extends the G-rich strand and so replaces what replication removes. Active in germ cells, stem cells and activated lymphocytes; silent in most somatic cells; reactivated in 85–90 % of cancers.

The two apoptotic pathways, and why they converge

The intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway responds to damage from inside: irreparable DNA damage, oxidative stress, growth-factor withdrawal, misfolded protein load. The decision is taken at the mitochondrial outer membrane by the Bcl-2 family. BH3-only sensor proteins (PUMA and NOXA, both p53 targets; BIM; BID) are induced or activated, they neutralise the anti-apoptotic guards (Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, Mcl-1) and activate the effectors BAX and BAK, which oligomerise and punch pores in the outer membrane. Through those pores comes cytochrome c — which in the intermembrane space is a respiratory carrier and in the cytosol is a death signal. It binds Apaf-1, which heptamerises into the apoptosome and recruits and activates procaspase-9.

The extrinsic (death receptor) pathway responds to an instruction from outside: a cytotoxic T cell or an NK cell displaying Fas ligand, or TNF, or TRAIL. The ligand trimerises its receptor, whose cytoplasmic death domain recruits the adaptor FADD, forming the DISC and activating procaspase-8.

Both routes then converge on the executioner caspases 3, 6 and 7, which do the actual demolition: they cleave nuclear lamins, cytoskeletal proteins and, importantly, ICAD — the inhibitor of caspase-activated DNase. Freed, CAD enters the nucleus and cuts the linker DNA between nucleosomes, generating the fragments in multiples of about 180 bp that produce the characteristic ladder on a gel. In many cells caspase-8 also cleaves BID to tBID, which activates the intrinsic pathway — an amplification loop that lets a weak extrinsic signal recruit the mitochondrial machinery.

Which initiator caspase belongs to which pathway

Extrinsic is eight; the other one is nine

Extrinsic — the instruction arrives from external: a death ligand (FasL, TNF, TRAIL) trimerises its receptor, the death domain recruits FADD, and the DISC activates caspase-8. Intrinsic — the damage is internal: BH3-only proteins release BAX and BAK, cytochrome c leaves the mitochondrion, binds Apaf-1, and the apoptosome activates caspase-9. Both then hand over to the same executioners, caspases 3, 6 and 7, which do the demolition. The "extrinsic–eight" pun circulates widely in English study material, which is reason enough to adopt it. It fixes two numbers and nothing else. It does not give you the apoptosome, which is worth holding as one three-part object — cytochrome c + Apaf-1 + procaspase-9 — nor the crosstalk step in which caspase-8 cleaves BID to tBID and pulls the mitochondrial route in behind a weak extrinsic signal. And note it says nothing about direction of dependence: apoptosis requires ATP, which is the fact most often inverted in an option.

Worked example 6 — telomere attrition, the Hayflick limit and what telomerase changes

A human fibroblast line has telomeres averaging 10.0 kb and loses about 100 bp per division; senescence is triggered at about 5.0 kb. (a) How many population doublings does the line have left? (b) The same measurement in a second line gives a loss of 50 bp per division — how many doublings now? (c) A newborn's cells start at 10 kb and a 70-year-old's at about 6 kb; using 100 bp per division, how many divisions separate them? (d) The line is transfected with hTERT and continues dividing past 200 doublings with stable telomeres and a normal diploid karyotype. Has it been transformed into a cancer cell?

  1. (a) (10 000 − 5 000) / 100 = 50 doublings — which is exactly why the Hayflick limit for human fibroblasts is quoted as about 50 ± 10. The arithmetic and the historical number agree, and that agreement is the evidence that telomere length is the clock.
  2. (b) 5000 / 50 = 100 doublings. The limit is not a fixed number of divisions; it is a fixed length divided by a variable rate. Oxidative stress accelerates the loss, which is one mechanism by which lifestyle and inflammation feed into cellular ageing.
  3. (c) (10 000 − 6 000) / 100 = 40 divisions of accumulated replicative history over a lifetime — a number that is small enough to be worth pausing on, and a reminder that most somatic lineages are maintained by a small pool of infrequently dividing stem cells rather than by every cell dividing constantly.
  4. (d) No. hTERT-immortalised fibroblasts remain contact-inhibited, anchorage-dependent, karyotypically normal and non-tumorigenic in immunodeficient mice. Telomerase removes one of the barriers — the replicative limit — and confers none of the other hallmarks. This is the experimental basis for calling telomerase an enabler rather than an oncogene, and it is why "telomerase is an oncogene" is a wrong answer that sounds authoritative. (Reconstituting a transformed human cell in culture famously required hTERT plus SV40 large T plus oncogenic RAS — that is, immortality plus loss of Rb and p53 plus a jammed accelerator.)
(a) 50 · (b) 100 · (c) 40 divisions · (d) no — immortalised, not transformed

Senescence, crisis, and how a tumour gets past both

The sequence is worth having as a narrative, because it explains why immortalisation is a discrete step in carcinogenesis rather than a gradual drift. As telomeres shorten, shelterin can no longer cap the end; the exposed end is read as a double-strand break; ATM is activated; p53 and p16 impose a permanent arrest. That is senescence, sometimes called mortality stage 1 — and note that it is a tumour-suppressive mechanism, which is why p53 and Rb loss lets cells bypass it. A cell that bypasses senescence keeps dividing and keeps eroding its telomeres until ends become genuinely uncapped and start fusing with one another, producing dicentric chromosomes that are torn apart at the next anaphase and fuse again — the breakage–fusion–bridge cycle. This is crisis, and it is massively genomically destabilising and usually lethal. The rare cell that survives is one that has reactivated telomerase (85–90 % of cancers) or switched on the recombination-based ALT pathway (10–15 %) — and it emerges from crisis carrying the chromosomal rearrangements that crisis generated. Telomere dynamics therefore both limit tumour growth and, in the cells that escape, supply the genomic instability that drives it.

The confusions worth pre-empting

"The Hayflick limit is caused by the accumulation of mutations." It is caused by telomere attrition triggering a DNA-damage response at the chromosome ends. The evidence is decisive and worth knowing: expressing hTERT alone abolishes the limit without doing anything about mutation rates.

"Senescent cells are dead, or dying." They are alive, metabolically active and permanently arrested. They also secrete the SASP — inflammatory cytokines, proteases and growth factors — which remodels the tissue around them and, paradoxically, can promote the growth of neighbouring pre-malignant cells. This is the basis of the current interest in senolytic drugs.

"Apoptosis is what happens when a cell runs out of ATP." Exactly backwards: apoptosis requires ATP, for apoptosome assembly among other things. A cell that runs out of ATP dies by necrosis. A cell committed to apoptosis that is then starved of ATP switches to a necrotic death — which is clinically relevant in ischaemia, where the core of an infarct becomes necrotic and the better-perfused penumbra shows apoptosis.

"Bcl-2 is an oncogene that makes cells divide." BCL2 is an oncogene, and it does nothing to the rate of division — it blocks apoptosis. The t(14;18) translocation of follicular lymphoma places BCL2 under the immunoglobulin heavy-chain enhancer, and the resulting cells accumulate because they fail to die. It was the first demonstration that failure to die is an independent route to a tumour, and it is why resisting cell death is one of the hallmarks in its own right. The BH3-mimetic venetoclax reverses this pharmacologically.

"Telomerase deficiency would simply be protective against cancer." Germline telomerase mutations cause dyskeratosis congenita — nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, reticular skin pigmentation, and above all bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis, because the tissues that suffer first are those depending most on stem-cell proliferation. Patients also have an increased risk of certain cancers, from the genomic instability that short telomeres cause. Telomerase is not simply a cancer-enabling nuisance; it is required wherever a stem cell must divide many times.
