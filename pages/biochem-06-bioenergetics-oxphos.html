Biochemistry · Note 06

Bioenergetics, the TCA Cycle and Oxidative Phosphorylation

One quantity organises this entire note: free energy, and the fact that it can only be moved from one process to another through something the two processes share. Catabolism does not "make energy"; it strips electrons off fuel and lets them fall down a potential ladder to oxygen, and the fall is harnessed twice — first into a proton gradient across a membrane, then into ATP. Get that two-step conversion clear and almost everything else on this page is arithmetic: why the citric acid cycle contains only one direct phosphorylation and yet supplies most of the ATP, why an uncoupler makes a cell burn faster while making less ATP, why the same NADH is worth 2.5 ATP in a heart cell and its cytosolic twin only 1.5 in a muscle fibre, and why the numbers 36 and 38 that everyone memorised turned out to be wrong.

§ 01

Free energy, coupling and why ATP works

The ideaSome changes happen by themselves and some do not, and a cell is mostly made of the second kind. The only way to make an unwilling change happen is to bolt it onto a willing one so that the two become a single event with a single balance sheet. That bolt is always a shared molecule. Nearly every unwilling reaction in the body is bolted, through one shared molecule, to the one reaction the cell keeps permanently willing — and that molecule is . The useful thing about ATP turns out not to be that it holds a lot of energy, but that it holds a middling amount, so it can be filled by strong donors and emptied into weak acceptors.

Definition & formula

Gibbs free-energy change, ΔG
The energy available from a process to do work at constant temperature and pressure. Negative ΔG means the process runs forward as written (exergonic); positive means it runs backward (endergonic); zero means equilibrium. ΔG says nothing whatever about rate — that is the enzyme's business.
ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q the standard value plus a correction for how far the actual concentrations sit from standard
Full definition, symbols and units
Standard transformed free-energy change, ΔG°′
ΔG when every reactant and product is at 1 M, at 25 °C, 1 atm, pH 7 and 55.5 M water. The prime is what makes it biochemical: [H⁺] is fixed at 10⁻⁷ M rather than 1 M, and water is left out of Q. A tabulated constant, not a measurement of the cell.
Phosphorylation potential, ΔGp
The actual ΔG of ATP hydrolysis in a living cell, given the real [ATP], [ADP] and [Pi]. Roughly −50 to −65 kJ·mol⁻¹ — considerably more negative than −30.5, because cells hold ATP far above its equilibrium level.
Coupled reaction
Two reactions joined by a shared intermediate, so that they proceed as one. Their ΔG°′ values simply add. Without a genuinely shared chemical species there is no coupling, only wishful bookkeeping.

What this paper actually asks — and it is not what you expect

Most people revise the citric acid cycle hard and oxidative phosphorylation lightly. On this paper the emphasis runs the other way. The cycle gets asked as a small number of stable facts — which step makes GTP, which enzymes are regulated, where the CO₂ carbons came from — and once you have those, extra revision buys you very little. Oxidative phosphorylation gets asked mechanistically, and the question can come from many angles: what each complex contains, which ones pump, where each named inhibitor binds, what an uncoupler does to oxygen consumption, what the P/O ratio is and why it is not a whole number. That is why §§06–10 of this page are longer and harder than §04. Spend your time accordingly.

ΔG°′ tells you where equilibrium is; ΔG tells you which way the reaction is going right now

This distinction is worth more marks than any single fact on the page, because examiners build distractors out of it. A tabulated ΔG°′ is a statement about a hypothetical solution in which everything is at 1 M. No cell has ever looked like that. What decides the direction of a real reaction is ΔG, and ΔG contains the concentration term.

The cleanest illustration is the last step of the citric acid cycle. Malate dehydrogenase catalyses

malate + NAD⁺ → oxaloacetate + NADH + H⁺ ΔG°′ = +29.7 kJ·mol⁻¹strongly endergonic under standard conditions — K′eq ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁶

Under standard conditions almost none of it would proceed. In a mitochondrion it proceeds continuously, every second of your life, because citrate synthase consumes oxaloacetate the instant it appears and holds it at a few micromolar. Push [oxaloacetate] down by five orders of magnitude and the RT ln Q term goes about −30 kJ·mol⁻¹ negative, which is exactly enough. A large positive ΔG°′ is not a barrier; it is a statement about how far from equilibrium the cell must hold the products.

Coupling requires a shared intermediate, and ΔG°′ values add

Phosphorylating glucose is uphill:

glucose + Pi → glucose 6-phosphate + H₂O ΔG°′ = +13.8K′eq = 3.8 × 10⁻³ — essentially nothing at equilibrium
ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi ΔG°′ = −30.5the willing reaction
glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADP ΔG°′ = −16.7sum of the two; K′eq = 845, a shift of 2.2 × 10⁵

Notice what hexokinase does not do. It does not hydrolyse ATP and then use the released heat. It transfers the γ-phosphoryl group directly onto glucose in one active site. The shared intermediate is the phosphoryl group itself, and there is never any free . This is the general pattern: biological coupling is group transfer, not heat transfer.

Why ATP hydrolysis is exergonic — and why "high-energy bond" is a misnomer

Nothing is stored inside the P–O–P linkage. What is large is the free energy of the reaction, and three product-side effects account for it:

  1. Electrostatic relief. At pH 7 the triphosphate tail carries three to four negative charges crowded within a few ångströms. Splitting it lets them move apart.
  2. Resonance stabilisation of Pi. Free inorganic phosphate spreads its charge over four equivalent oxygens. The same phosphorus locked in an anhydride cannot; the two ends compete for the bridging oxygen's electrons.
  3. Solvation and ionisation. Two separate products are hydrated better than one joined molecule, and the proton released is mopped up at pH 7.

Add a fourth, purely kinetic point that matters biologically: the reaction is enormously favourable and also enormously slow without a catalyst. ATP is stable in a test tube for days. That combination — thermodynamically poised, kinetically inert — is precisely what a controllable energy currency requires.

Everything above ATP in that list has enough transfer potential to drive the phosphorylation of ADP; everything below it has to be phosphorylated by ATP. If ATP sat at the top it would be a superb store and a useless currency, because nothing could recharge it. ATP is a currency precisely because it is not the richest compound in the cell. Note also that two entries are not phosphates at all: the thioesters. A thioester is high-energy for a related reason — sulfur's 3p orbitals overlap poorly with carbon's 2p, so the C=O of a thioester gets far less resonance donation than an ordinary ester does, and hydrolysis therefore releases much more. That single fact is why acetyl-CoA can drive citrate synthase and why succinyl-CoA can pay for a GTP.

There is no 口訣 for that table and you do not need one. The whole ranking is recoverable from a single question asked about each compound — does the cell make ATP from it, or make it using ATP? Phosphoenolpyruvate and 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate hand a phosphoryl group to ADP in glycolysis, so they must sit above ATP. Phosphocreatine recharges ATP in muscle, so it sits above too. The two thioesters pay for citrate synthase and for a GTP, so they sit above. Glucose 6-phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate and glycerol 3-phosphate are all made by ATP, so they sit below. The only genuinely arbitrary fact left over is that phosphoenolpyruvate is the top of the table — carry that one as a bare fact and derive the rest in the three seconds it takes to ask the question.

Three phrasings that lose marks

"ATP stores energy in its high-energy bonds." Bonds cost energy to break, always. The large negative value belongs to the overall reaction, products included.
"ΔG°′ is negative, so the reaction goes forward in the cell." Not necessarily — check Q. And the converse trap is commoner: a positive ΔG°′ does not forbid a reaction.
"ATP is the cell's energy store." It is the currency; the store is glycogen and triacylglycerol. A resting adult holds only a few tens of grams of ATP at any instant and turns over something on the order of its own body mass per day.

Exam technique · any stem that hands you concentrations

  1. Recognise the type in five seconds — if the stem quotes real [ATP], [ADP], [Pi] or any millimolar figures at all, it is a ΔG question, not a ΔG°′ question. The option that repeats the tabulated value (−30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹, or "ΔG°′ is a thermodynamic constant") is placed there deliberately, and it is usually phrased as a perfectly true sentence lifted from textbook prose. True is not the same as answering the stem.
  2. Write Q before touching a calculator — products over reactants, concentrations in molar, water omitted because it is already inside the prime. For ATP hydrolysis Q = [ADP][Pi] / [ATP].
  3. Use the base-ten shortcut, not ln — at 37 °C, ΔG ≈ ΔG°′ + 5.94 × log₁₀ Q (5.71 at 25 °C). A Q of 10⁻⁴ is therefore worth about −24 kJ·mol⁻¹, which you can do in your head while reading the options.
  4. The sign check that kills options without solving anything — a living cell always holds ATP high and ADP·Pi low, so Q ≪ 1, so ln Q < 0, so the real ΔG is more negative than −30.5. Any option less negative than −30.5 is wrong on sight, and so is any positive one. That is typically two of the five gone, and with −0.5 marking, eliminating even one option already makes answering strictly better than a blank.
  5. Expect the answer near −50 — the physiological window for ΔGp is −50 to −65 kJ·mol⁻¹. Landing far outside it means you inverted Q; landing at exactly the mirror image means you added RT ln Q instead of subtracting.
Worked example 1 — what coupling actually buys you

For glucose + Pi → glucose 6-phosphate + H₂O, ΔG°′ = +13.8 kJ·mol⁻¹. (a) What is K′eq at 25 °C? (b) What is K′eq for the hexokinase reaction, in which the phosphoryl group comes from ATP? (c) By what factor has coupling shifted the equilibrium? Take R = 8.315 × 10⁻³ kJ·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, T = 298 K.

  1. (a) K′eq = e−ΔG°′/RT = e−13.8/(0.008315 × 298) = e−5.57.
  2. K′eq = 3.8 × 10⁻³ — at equilibrium fewer than four glucose molecules in a thousand carry a phosphate. Useless.
  3. (b) Add the two ΔG°′ values, because the reactions share Pi: +13.8 + (−30.5) = −16.7 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  4. K′eq = e+16.7/2.478 = e6.74 = 8.4 × 10².
  5. (c) 845 ÷ 3.8 × 10⁻³ = 2.2 × 10⁵.
  6. Sanity check on the shortcut: 5.71 kJ·mol⁻¹ buys one factor of ten at 25 °C, so 30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ should buy about 30.5/5.71 = 5.3 orders of magnitude. It does: 2.2 × 10⁵.

Worked example 2 — the real ΔG of ATP hydrolysis in a cell

In a human erythrocyte the measured concentrations are [ATP] = 2.25 mM, [ADP] = 0.25 mM, [Pi] = 1.65 mM. Calculate ΔG for ATP hydrolysis at 37 °C, given ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹.

  1. Write Q with concentrations in molar, water omitted (it is the solvent, already in the prime): Q = ([ADP][Pi]) / [ATP] = (2.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 1.65 × 10⁻³) / (2.25 × 10⁻³).
  2. Q = 1.83 × 10⁻⁴. It is far below 1 because the cell keeps ATP high and its products low — that is the whole point of a phosphorylation potential.
  3. RT = 0.008315 × 310 = 2.578 kJ·mol⁻¹; ln(1.83 × 10⁻⁴) = −8.60.
  4. RT ln Q = 2.578 × (−8.60) = −22.2 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  5. ΔG = −30.5 − 22.2 = −52.7 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  6. Read the meaning: the tabulated −30.5 understates the driving force by about 70 %. Every ATP-consuming process in the cell is working against a real ΔGp nearer −50 to −65 kJ·mol⁻¹, and that is the number to use in § 07 when asking whether four protons can pay for one ATP.

§ 02

Redox potentials: volts into kilojoules

The ideaMolecules differ in how tightly they hold their outermost electrons. Put a loose holder next to a greedy grabber and electrons move, and moving charge downhill releases energy — exactly as it does in a battery. Chemists measure that greed as a voltage, so a table of voltages is really a table of how much energy each electron transfer can pay out. One short equation converts the volts into kilojoules, and once you have it, the whole respiratory chain becomes a single calculation done in three instalments.

Definition & formula

Standard reduction potential, E°′
The voltage a half-reaction generates against the standard hydrogen electrode when every species is at 1 M, 25 °C, pH 7. Always written as a reduction — as electrons being accepted. A more positive E°′ means a greater appetite for electrons.
ΔG°′ = −nF ΔE°′ with ΔE°′ = E°′(acceptor) − E°′(donor) the single most useful equation on this page; get the subtraction the right way round
Full definition, symbols and units
Conjugate redox pair
The oxidised and reduced forms of one species, e.g. NAD⁺/NADH, written together because a potential belongs to the pair and never to one member alone.
Electron donor / acceptor
The donor is oxidised, the acceptor is reduced. Electrons flow spontaneously from the pair with the more negative E°′ to the pair with the more positive E°′ — which is why NADH (−0.320 V) reduces oxygen (+0.816 V) and never the reverse.
Actual versus standard potential
Like ΔG, E is concentration-dependent (Nernst). A matrix NADH/NAD⁺ ratio far from 1 shifts the real potential, which is why complex I can run backwards under some conditions.
E = E°′ + RTnF ln [oxidised][reduced]the Nernst equation, in the biochemists' sign convention

The sign convention is where marks are lost, so fix it with a sentence you can recite: acceptor minus donor. If electrons are flowing from NADH to ubiquinone, the acceptor is ubiquinone (+0.045) and the donor pair is NAD⁺/NADH (−0.320), so ΔE°′ = 0.045 − (−0.320) = +0.365 V, positive, and ΔG°′ comes out negative because of the minus sign in front of nF. A spontaneous electron transfer always has ΔE°′ positive and ΔG°′ negative. If your answer has both positive, you subtracted backwards.

Why NADH and FADH₂ are worth different amounts

Not because "FADH₂ carries less energy" in some intrinsic sense, but because of where each one is able to hand its electrons over. NADH is a freely diffusible, two-electron (hydride) carrier with E°′ = −0.320 V, comfortably above ubiquinone, so it can reduce complex I and its electrons traverse all three pumping sites: 10 H⁺. Succinate sits at +0.031 V, essentially level with Q, so its electrons can only be delivered to the Q pool and skip complex I entirely: 6 H⁺. The 2.5-versus-1.5 ATP difference is nothing more than 10 ÷ 4 versus 6 ÷ 4.

"FAD's E°′ is −0.219 V, so how can it accept electrons from succinate at +0.031 V?"

Because −0.219 V is the potential of free in solution, and no flavin in the respiratory chain is free. A flavin's potential is tuned by the protein around it across a range of about −0.4 V to +0.06 V. The FAD of succinate dehydrogenase is covalently attached to a histidine and its protein raises its potential to near 0 V, which is exactly what is needed to sit between succinate and Q. The same logic applies to iron–sulfur clusters, whose potentials span roughly −0.7 to +0.4 V despite being built from the same iron and sulfur, and to the hemes of the cytochromes, which run from +0.077 V (cyt b) to +0.35 V (cyt a₃) with chemically similar iron–porphyrins. A tabulated E°′ describes a cofactor in a beaker; the protein decides its real value.

NAD⁺ and NADP⁺: same chemistry, opposite jobs

Their potentials are almost identical (−0.320 and −0.324 V) and they differ by one phosphate on the 2′-OH of the adenosine ribose — a group that takes no part in the chemistry at all. It is a recognition handle: dehydrogenases select one or the other, and cells therefore maintain the two pools in opposite redox states. is kept oxidised (cytosolic NAD⁺/NADH ≈ 700) so it is always ready to accept electrons from catabolism; NADPH is kept reduced (NADP⁺/NADPH ≈ 0.01) so it is always ready to donate them to biosynthesis and to antioxidant defence. Same voltage, opposite direction of flow, enforced entirely by concentration. This is the Nernst equation doing physiology.

Exam technique · volts into kilojoules in twenty seconds

  1. Name the acceptor out loud first — it is the species being reduced. Write its E°′ on the left and the donor's on the right, then subtract: ΔE°′ = E°′(acceptor) − E°′(donor). Physically laying them out in that order prevents the only mistake this question type is built around.
  2. Multiply by 193, not by 96.5 — nearly every couple on this page (NAD⁺/NADH, FAD/FADH₂, fumarate/succinate, Q/QH₂, ½O₂/H₂O, pyruvate/lactate, OAA/malate) moves two electrons, and 2 × 96.5 = 193. So ΔG°′ in kJ·mol⁻¹ is simply −193 × ΔE°′. Cytochromes are the exception — one electron each, multiplier 96.5.
  3. Kill the internally inconsistent options before calculating — a spontaneous transfer has ΔE°′ positive and ΔG°′ negative, always. Any option that pairs them with the same sign contradicts itself and can go without arithmetic.
  4. Read the surviving distractors as one family. They are generated from the right answer, not independently: same magnitude with the opposite sign means "you subtracted backwards"; exactly half means "you used n = 1"; exactly double means "you used n = 4". Seeing +29.7, −14.9, −29.7 and −59.4 in one list tells you the intended answer is −29.7 before you have touched a number.
  5. Sanity-check the magnitude against the whole chain. NADH to O₂ is 1.14 V and −219 kJ·mol⁻¹ in total, split −70 / −40 / −109. No single step can exceed the total, and anything near −109 has to be complex IV.
Worked example 3 — the whole chain, and then in three instalments

Using E°′(NAD⁺/NADH) = −0.320 V, E°′(Q/QH₂) = +0.045 V, E°′(cyt c Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) = +0.254 V, E°′(½O₂/H₂O) = +0.816 V and F = 96.485 kJ·V⁻¹·mol⁻¹, find ΔG°′ for the complete oxidation of one NADH by oxygen, and for each of complexes I, III and IV separately. Then check whether the parts sum to the whole.

  1. Whole span. Acceptor − donor: ΔE°′ = 0.816 − (−0.320) = +1.136 V. Two electrons, so ΔG°′ = −2 × 96.485 × 1.136 = −219 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  2. Complex I (NADH → Q): ΔE°′ = 0.045 + 0.320 = 0.365 V; ΔG°′ = −2 × 96.485 × 0.365 = −70.4 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  3. Complex III (QH₂ → cyt c): ΔE°′ = 0.254 − 0.045 = 0.209 V; ΔG°′ = −40.3 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  4. Complex IV (cyt c → O₂): ΔE°′ = 0.816 − 0.254 = 0.562 V; ΔG°′ = −108.4 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  5. Check: 70.4 + 40.3 + 108.4 = 219.1 ✓ (the last digit is rounding in E°′). Free energy is a state function, so it had to add up — but doing the check catches sign errors instantly.
  6. Now complex II for contrast (succinate → Q): ΔE°′ = 0.045 − 0.031 = 0.014 V, so ΔG°′ = −2 × 96.485 × 0.014 = −2.7 kJ·mol⁻¹. Moving a single proton against a typical gradient costs about 19 kJ·mol⁻¹ (§ 07). Complex II is short by a factor of seven — which is why it pumps nothing. The answer to "why does complex II not pump?" is a number, not a fact to be memorised.

§ 03

Pyruvate dehydrogenase: the one-way gate

The ideaSugar arrives at the mitochondrion as a three-carbon molecule; the cycle that will burn it only accepts a two-carbon one. A single enormous assembly does the conversion: it snips off one carbon as carbon dioxide, oxidises what is left, and hands the product over on a carrier. Two things about it matter more than the mechanism. It is completely irreversible, which is why the carbons of an ordinary fatty acid can never come back as sugar. And it is switched on and off by phosphorylation, which makes it the point where hormones decide whether the body is burning carbohydrate or sparing it.

Definition & reaction

Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH, PDC)
A multienzyme assembly of three catalytic activities (E1, E2, E3) and five cofactors that performs oxidative decarboxylation — losing CO₂ and generating a thioester in one committed step. It is not part of the citric acid cycle; it is the reaction that feeds it.
pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺ ΔG°′ = −33.4 kJ·mol⁻¹ strongly exergonic and physiologically irreversible — the point of no return for carbohydrate
Full definition, symbols and units
E1 — pyruvate dehydrogenase (decarboxylase)
Uses thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP). Its thiazolium C2 loses a proton to give a carbanion (ylide) that attacks pyruvate's keto carbon; CO₂ leaves and the resulting carbanion is stabilised by the positively charged ring. Vitamin B1.
E2 — dihydrolipoyl transacetylase
Carries a lipoyl group amide-linked to a lysine, giving a ~14 Å swinging arm. The lipoamide disulfide oxidises the two-carbon unit to an acetyl group and then passes it to coenzyme A (vitamin B5, pantothenate). Forms the structural core: 60 copies in mammals, arranged as a pentagonal dodecahedron.

Reoxidises the dihydrolipoamide using tightly bound FAD (B2), then passes the electrons to NAD⁺ (B3, niacin). The same E3 protein is shared with α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, the branched-chain complex and the glycine cleavage system, so an E3 mutation disables four pathways at once.

The mechanism, in the order the cofactors appear

Five steps, five cofactors, in sequence — which is why the mnemonic works and why a question about "the third cofactor" has a defensible answer:

  1. attacks pyruvate; CO₂ leaves, giving hydroxyethyl-TPP.
  2. Lipoamide (oxidised, a cyclic disulfide) accepts the two-carbon unit and is reduced in the process — this is the actual oxidation step. Product: acetyl-dihydrolipoamide.
  3. takes the acetyl group by transthioesterification, releasing acetyl-CoA and leaving dihydrolipoamide.
  4. FAD on E3 reoxidises the dihydrolipoamide back to the disulfide.
  5. NAD⁺ reoxidises FADH₂ and leaves as NADH — the only product that goes to the chain.

The reason for building all this into one particle is substrate channelling: the lipoyl arm swings between the three active sites without ever letting the intermediate into free solution. Nothing is lost by diffusion, nothing side-reacts, and the whole sequence runs at the rate of the slowest step rather than at the rate of three separate diffusive encounters.

The five cofactors, in order

Tender Loving Care For Nancy → TPP, Lipoamide, CoA, FAD, NAD⁺. And the vitamins in the same order: B₁, (lipoate — not a vitamin), B₅, B₂, B₃.

Do not build a second hook for "prosthetic group versus cosubstrate" — it can be read straight off the overall equation. The two cofactors that come and go are exactly the two that appear in the balanced reaction: pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺. CoA and NAD⁺ are written there as a substrate and a product, so they must diffuse in and out; TPP, lipoamide and FAD appear nowhere in it, so they must stay bolted to the enzyme between turnovers. The same one-line test works for α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the branched-chain complex, which use the identical five.

α β γ on a phosphate chain

Counted outward from the sugar: α is the phosphate bonded to the 5′ carbon, then β, then γ at the far end. A kinase moves the γ phosphate; attack at α releases PPi instead. This is the sense in § 01, where hexokinase transfers the γ-phosphoryl group of ATP, and the sense drawn in the panel behind every phosphate symbol on this page.

α β on a ring sugar

Here the letter names the anomeric carbon — which face of the ring the C1 hydroxyl sits on, as in α- and β-D-glucose. Nothing on this page uses that sense, but it is the one most easily confused with the other two, because the letter on the page looks identical.

α β γ along a carbon chain

Counted away from the carboxyl carbon: α is the carbon next to it, then β, then γ. That is why an α-keto acid carries its keto group beside the carboxyl (pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate), why β-oxidation attacks the second carbon, and why isocitrate dehydrogenase performs a β-decarboxylation.

Regulation: two layers, both reading the same thing

PDH is regulated more tightly than almost any enzyme in metabolism, because switching it on commits carbohydrate irreversibly to oxidation.

  • Product inhibition. Acetyl-CoA and NADH inhibit E2 and E3 directly. Both accumulate when fatty acid oxidation is running hard — so β-oxidation switches PDH off, which is precisely the glucose-sparing behaviour you want during fasting.
  • Covalent modification. PDH kinase phosphorylates three serines on E1α and inactivates the complex. PDK is activated by high ratios of NADH/NAD⁺, acetyl-CoA/CoA and ATP/ADP — the same signals, one layer up. PDH phosphatase reverses it and is activated by Ca²⁺ and by insulin. Pyruvate itself inhibits the kinase, so plenty of substrate keeps the enzyme on.

Read the logic rather than the list: the complex asks one question — does this cell need to oxidise carbohydrate right now? — and every input is a proxy for the answer. Ca²⁺ is the most elegant of them. Muscle contraction releases Ca²⁺; Ca²⁺ simultaneously activates PDH phosphatase, isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (§ 04). The signal that says "start contracting" is the same signal that says "start making ATP", with no intervening messenger.

The clinical hooks that get written into vignettes

Thiamine deficiency. No TPP means E1 cannot work, and neither can α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, transketolase (pentose phosphate pathway) or the branched-chain complex. Pyruvate backs up and is reduced to lactate: lactic acidosis with a normal or high pyruvate. Clinically this is wet and dry beriberi, and Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia) in chronic alcohol use or hyperemesis. Giving intravenous glucose before thiamine can precipitate it, because the glucose load demands exactly the enzyme that has no cofactor — a favourite vignette.
Arsenite (As³⁺) chelates the two adjacent thiols of dihydrolipoamide, forming a stable ring and locking E2. It therefore knocks out every lipoamide-dependent complex at once. (Arsenate, As⁵⁺, is different: it substitutes for phosphate in glycolysis and uncouples substrate-level phosphorylation.)
Inherited PDH deficiency — usually X-linked, in the E1α subunit — presents as congenital lactic acidosis with neurological damage; a ketogenic diet helps because ketone bodies bypass PDH entirely. Dichloroacetate inhibits PDH kinase and so forces the complex on.

Arsenite versus arsenate — two poisons, two unrelated targets

arsenate imitates phosphate · arsenite bites the dithiol

Arsenate, As⁵⁺, is a tetrahedral oxyanion of the same shape and charge as Pi, so it slips into phosphate's place in glycolysis; the arsenate ester that results hydrolyses spontaneously, so the pathway keeps running but the substrate-level ATP never appears. Arsenite, As³⁺, does something completely different: it clamps the two adjacent thiols of dihydrolipoamide into a stable ring, disabling E2 of every lipoamide complex at once — PDH, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the branched-chain complex. My own wording, not a circulating 口訣. It fixes only which is which; for the consequences, read arsenite as "a thiamine-deficiency picture without the thiamine deficiency" — same three complexes down, same lactic acidosis — and arsenate as "glycolysis without its payoff".

PDH is not a step of the citric acid cycle

It is the reaction before it, and the distinction is examined. A question that asks "how many NADH are produced per turn of the citric acid cycle" wants three, not four. A question that asks "per pyruvate oxidised" wants four. Note also that the CO₂ released by PDH is the carboxyl carbon of pyruvate — which is why [1-¹⁴C]pyruvate delivers no label to the cycle at all, while [2-¹⁴C] and [3-¹⁴C]pyruvate do.

§ 04

The citric acid cycle

The ideaA two-carbon fragment is handed to a four-carbon acceptor, and over eight reactions the six-carbon product is stripped back down to the same four-carbon acceptor, releasing two carbons as carbon dioxide on the way. Because the acceptor is regenerated, it is never used up — a catalyst on a grand scale. Almost none of the energy comes out as ATP directly; what comes out is reduced electron carriers, and they are the real product. The cycle is a machine for turning carbon into carbon dioxide and, in the process, loading four carriers with electrons for §§06–08 to spend.

Definition & net reaction

Citric acid cycle (TCA cycle, Krebs cycle, 檸檬酸循環)
Eight enzymes in the mitochondrial matrix — with one exception embedded in the inner membrane — that completely oxidise the acetyl group of to two CO₂, regenerating oxaloacetate each turn. It is amphibolic（兩性代謝）: catabolic and biosynthetic at the same time (§ 05).

Full definition, symbols and units
Substrate-level phosphorylation
ATP or GTP made by direct transfer of a phosphoryl group from a high-energy substrate, without any involvement of a membrane or a gradient. The cycle contains exactly one, at succinyl-CoA synthetase.
Oxidative decarboxylation
Loss of CO₂ coupled to oxidation of the remaining fragment. The cycle contains two, at isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase — and they are the two CO₂.

Every NAD⁺ and FAD the cycle needs is regenerated by the respiratory chain. Remove O₂ and the carriers stay reduced, NAD⁺ runs out, and the cycle stops within seconds — even though no step uses O₂ as a reactant.

Where the energy actually comes out

One per turn is almost nothing — about 1 ATP out of the 10 that a turn eventually yields. Roughly 90 % of the yield is in the four reduced carriers, and none of it is realised until §§06–08. This is the reason the cycle is worth so little as a standalone exam topic and so much as the supplier for oxidative phosphorylation: the citric acid cycle is not where ATP is made; it is where electrons are collected.

Regulation: one question, asked at three points

Citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase are the control points, and every one of them responds to the same three families of signal:

Energy charge

High ATP/ADP inhibits; ADP activates isocitrate dehydrogenase directly. The cycle asks whether ATP is already plentiful.

Redox state

NADH inhibits all three. If the chain is not consuming NADH, there is no point making more. This is the feedback that carries respiratory control backwards into the cycle.

Ca²⁺ and product build-up

Ca²⁺ activates the last two (and PDH phosphatase): contraction and oxidation are switched on together. Succinyl-CoA inhibits both citrate synthase and α-KGDH.

Follow the causal chain to its root and it is startlingly simple. ADP availability controls ATP synthase; ATP synthase controls the proton gradient; the gradient controls electron flow; electron flow controls NADH/NAD⁺; NADH/NAD⁺ controls the cycle and PDH. Everything on this page is ultimately governed by whether there is ADP to phosphorylate. Nothing in metabolism is regulated by "the amount of energy" — it is regulated by ratios, and the ratio that matters most is ADP to ATP.

There is no 口訣 for "which three steps are regulated", because the three pick themselves. Regulation always sits on irreversible steps, and an irreversible step is one with a large negative ΔG°′. Run down the eight values and the three most exergonic reactions in the cycle are α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (−33.5), citrate synthase (−32.2) and isocitrate dehydrogenase (−8.4) — which are precisely the three ✦ enzymes. No other step reaches even −4 kJ·mol⁻¹, and two of them are frankly positive and run only because the next enzyme drags their product away. You cannot usefully control a reaction that is already sitting at equilibrium. Learn that rule once and it hands you the control points here, in glycolysis and in every pathway you meet afterwards.

The cycle details that are actually asked

GTP, not ATP, at succinyl-CoA synthetase — and it is the only substrate-level phosphorylation in the cycle. Succinate dehydrogenase is complex II, is the only membrane-bound enzyme of the cycle, and is the only one whose flavin is covalently attached. Three NADH, one FADH₂, one GTP, two CO₂ per turn — per acetyl-CoA, so double everything per glucose. No oxygen appears anywhere in the cycle, yet the cycle is strictly aerobic. Aconitase does no redox chemistry despite having an Fe–S cluster. And the two carbons entering are not the two leaving.

The carbon-tracing trap

"Acetyl-CoA is oxidised to two CO₂" is true of the net process and false of any single turn. Citrate is a symmetric-looking molecule, but aconitase grips it at three points and therefore acts only on the arm that came from oxaloacetate (Ogston's three-point attachment, 1948). The consequence is exact: both CO₂ released in the first turn come from oxaloacetate carbons, and the acetyl carbons survive into the regenerated four-carbon acid. Then succinate and fumarate are genuinely symmetric, so a label scrambles between the two ends before oxaloacetate re-forms. Work the next example rather than memorising a conclusion.

Exam technique · isotope-label questions on the cycle

  1. Recognise it instantly. Any stem containing ¹⁴C, "labelled", or "which carbon is released as CO₂" is this one question type, and it is solved by three rules — never by drawing structures.
  2. Rule 1: turn one is a decoy. Both CO₂ of the first turn come from the oxaloacetate carbons, never from the acetyl group that just arrived. Every option putting an acetyl-derived label into first-turn CO₂ is dead, and there are usually two of them.
  3. Rule 2: symmetry begins at succinate. Succinate and fumarate are symmetric, so from there a label splits 50 : 50 between the two ends. Malate and oxaloacetate are not symmetric, and citrate only looks it — aconitase grips it at three points, which is the whole content of Ogston.
  4. Rule 3: a carboxyl label leaves one turn earlier than an interior one. Label on the acetyl carbonyl carbon → becomes a carboxyl of succinate → 0 % in turn 1, 100 % in turn 2. Label on the acetyl methyl carbon → becomes an interior carbon → 0 %, 0 %, then 50 % in turn 3.
  5. Translate a pyruvate label before you start. Pyruvate C1 is the carboxyl PDH releases, so [1-¹⁴C]pyruvate puts no label into the cycle at all — that fact alone answers some stems outright. C2 becomes the acetyl carbonyl (Rule 3, first case) and C3 the acetyl methyl (Rule 3, second case).
  6. Budget the time honestly. With the rules this is a 20-second question; without them it is a five-minute one you will still get wrong. If the rules are not immediately to hand, mark it, answer whatever survives Rule 1, and move on — an eliminated option already makes the guess worth taking.
Worked example 4 — where does the labelled carbon go?

Acetyl-CoA labelled with ¹⁴C in its carbonyl (thioester) carbon condenses with unlabelled oxaloacetate. How much of the label is released as CO₂ during (a) the first turn and (b) the second turn? What would be different if the methyl carbon had been labelled instead?

  1. Write citrate with its origins marked. Citrate synthase makes the acetyl methyl carbon attack the oxaloacetate carbonyl, so citrate is HOOC(C*)–CH₂(Cm)–C(OH)(COOH from OAA-C1)–CH₂(OAA-C3)–COOH(OAA-C4), where C* is our label.
  2. Aconitase acts on the oxaloacetate-derived arm, so the hydroxyl ends up on OAA-C3.
  3. Isocitrate dehydrogenase removes the carboxyl attached to the old central carbon: that is OAA-C1. First CO₂ is unlabelled.
  4. α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase removes the carboxyl next to the keto group: that is OAA-C4. Second CO₂ is unlabelled. (a) = 0 %.
  5. The label C* is now a carboxyl carbon of succinate. Succinate is symmetric, so it is read from either end with equal probability — but both ends are carboxyls, so the label lands on oxaloacetate C1 or C4 either way.
  6. In the next turn, steps 3 and 4 release exactly OAA-C1 and OAA-C4. So (b) = 100 %: the carboxyl-derived label comes out completely during the second turn.
  7. Methyl label instead. Trace it the same way: it becomes a methylene carbon of succinate, and therefore OAA-C2 or OAA-C3. Neither is released in turn 2 either — so 0 % in the first and second turns. Half of it then moves to a carboxyl position and is released in turn 3 (50 %), with the rest dribbling out over later turns.

§ 05

Anaplerosis and the cycle as a biosynthetic hub

The ideaThe wheel is also a warehouse. Cells constantly take intermediates out of it to build fats, amino acids, haem, glucose and nucleotides — and the moment an intermediate leaves, the wheel has less material to turn with. So there must be reactions whose only job is to top the wheel back up. Once you see the cycle as something with a stock level rather than a fixed loop, several confusing facts resolve at once: why a four-carbon intermediate can be withdrawn but a two-carbon acetyl group cannot replace it, and therefore why fat cannot become sugar.

Why acetyl-CoA can never make net glucose

This is the most examined consequence in the whole of metabolism, and it has two independent reasons — quote both:

  1. PDH is irreversible. ΔG°′ = −33.4 kJ·mol⁻¹, and there is no enzyme in animals that runs it backwards. Acetyl-CoA cannot become pyruvate.
  2. The cycle has no net carbon gain. Two carbons enter as acetyl; two leave as CO₂. Every four-carbon intermediate withdrawn for gluconeogenesis must therefore have been supplied by an anaplerotic reaction, not by the acetyl group. Feeding the cycle acetyl-CoA raises its flux, never its stock.

The apparent exceptions prove the rule and are worth knowing precisely. Odd-chain fatty acids finish β-oxidation with a three-carbon propionyl-CoA, which is carboxylated (biotin) and rearranged (vitamin B₁₂) to succinyl-CoA — four carbons entering the cycle at the succinate level, genuinely anaplerotic and genuinely glucogenic. The glycerol backbone of a triacylglycerol is likewise glucogenic, entering as dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Neither is a route from an acetyl group. In plants, the glyoxylate cycle bypasses both decarboxylations and so does convert acetyl units into four-carbon acids — which is why seedlings can turn stored oil into sugar and we cannot.

Everything that enters the cycle as succinyl-CoA

VOMIT

Valine · Odd-chain fatty acids · Methionine · Isoleucine · Threonine. All five converge on propionyl-CoA, which is carboxylated by a biotin enzyme to D-methylmalonyl-CoA, epimerised to the L form, and rearranged by a B₁₂ enzyme (methylmalonyl-CoA mutase) into succinyl-CoA. This is the standard, widely circulating mnemonic, and it is worth having verbatim because one word answers three separate questions: which residues are glucogenic through the cycle, why B₁₂ deficiency raises methylmalonic acid while folate deficiency does not, and what the single genuine route from a fatty acid to glucose is. It covers only the entries at the succinyl-CoA level. The other anaplerotic doors are not in it and are separately examinable: pyruvate carboxylase into oxaloacetate (the dominant one by far), glutamate dehydrogenase and transamination into α-ketoglutarate, malic enzyme into malate, and Phe/Tyr catabolism plus the urea cycle into fumarate.

Pyruvate carboxylase and the two readings of one signal

Pyruvate carboxylase is allosterically activated by acetyl-CoA, and this is elegant rather than arbitrary. Acetyl-CoA piling up means one of two things. If the cycle is stalled for want of oxaloacetate, making oxaloacetate restarts it. If the cell is instead fuel-replete — fasting liver, plenty of fatty acid oxidation — the extra oxaloacetate is drawn off by PEPCK into gluconeogenesis. Same enzyme, same activator, two useful outcomes depending on demand. Note the trap in the wording, though: acetyl-CoA is the activator, never the substrate. Pyruvate carboxylase carboxylates pyruvate. Any option that has it carboxylating acetyl-CoA is wrong, and it is a distractor examiners reuse.

Go deeper · when cycle enzymes become tumour suppressors and oncometabolites

Three cycle enzymes are bona-fide cancer genes, and the mechanism is metabolic rather than mitogenic — which is why this material has migrated into clinical vignettes.

  • Succinate dehydrogenase (SDHA/B/C/D). Germline loss-of-function causes hereditary paraganglioma and phaeochromocytoma, and gastrointestinal stromal tumours. Succinate builds up, leaks to the cytosol and inhibits the α-ketoglutarate–dependent dioxygenases — including the prolyl hydroxylases that mark HIF-1α for destruction. HIF-1α therefore survives even in normoxia ("pseudohypoxia") and switches on angiogenesis and glycolysis. Note the inheritance point: complex II is the only respiratory complex encoded entirely by nuclear DNA, so these syndromes are autosomal, not maternal.
  • Fumarate hydratase (FH). Germline loss causes hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma. Fumarate accumulates and does the same thing to the dioxygenases, plus it succinates cysteine residues non-enzymatically.
  • IDH1 and IDH2. These are the NADP⁺-linked isocitrate dehydrogenases — cytosolic and mitochondrial respectively — and not the NAD⁺-linked IDH3 that runs step 3 of the cycle. The lesions are gain-of-function point mutations (classically IDH1 R132H) found in low-grade gliomas, secondary glioblastoma and about 15 % of acute myeloid leukaemia. The mutant enzyme stops making α-ketoglutarate and instead reduces it to (R)-2-hydroxyglutarate, an oncometabolite that competitively inhibits the same α-KG-dependent dioxygenases — here the TET DNA demethylases and the histone demethylases, producing a hypermethylated epigenome. Ivosidenib and enasidenib are licensed mutant-IDH inhibitors.

The unifying idea is worth more than the list: a family of dioxygenases uses α-ketoglutarate as a cosubstrate, and any cycle lesion that alters the α-KG / succinate / fumarate balance reprogrammes them. Metabolism and epigenetics are the same subject seen from two ends.

Two clinical confusions worth separating

B₁₂ deficiency blocks methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, so methylmalonic acid rises — and that is what distinguishes it from folate deficiency, which produces the same megaloblastic anaemia and the same raised homocysteine but a normal methylmalonate. Biotin deficiency (or biotinidase deficiency) hits four carboxylases at once, giving lactic acidosis, alopecia and a periorificial rash; raw egg white contains avidin, which binds biotin — the classic dietary cause.

§ 06

The electron-transport chain, complex by complex

The ideaHydrogen burning in oxygen releases a great deal of energy all at once, as heat, which a cell cannot use. So the cell takes the same electrons and lets them down a staircase of carriers instead, each one slightly greedier than the last. At three of the steps the drop is big enough to do mechanical work, and the work is always the same: shoving protons from one side of a membrane to the other. Nothing here makes ATP. This is the part of the machine that converts a chemical fall into a physical gradient.

What each complex is actually doing

Complex I is the largest and the least intuitive. Its L-shape has a hydrophilic arm sticking into the matrix, where hands its hydride to FMN and a chain of eight iron–sulfur clusters relays the electrons one at a time over about 95 Å to a quinone bound at the junction with the membrane arm. Nothing in that arm touches the protons. The pumping happens in the membrane arm, through three or four antiporter-like modules driven by a long horizontal helix acting as a mechanical coupling rod — electron transfer at one end, conformational changes propagating along the arm. That architecture is why complex I is reversible: push hard enough on the proton side and electrons run backwards from Q to NAD⁺, generating superoxide on the way.

Complex II is the citric acid cycle enzyme wearing a second hat. It contributes electrons but no protons, for the reason computed in § 02: the succinate → Q drop is worth 2.7 kJ·mol⁻¹ against the ~19 kJ·mol⁻¹ it costs to move one proton. Everything it does is at the Q pool.

Complex III solves the two-to-one problem. Ubiquinol arrives with two electrons; cytochrome c can only take one. So the complex splits them: one goes "up" the high-potential route (Rieske Fe–S → cyt c₁ → cyt c), and the other goes "down" the low-potential route (cyt bL → cyt bH) to reduce another quinone at a second site on the matrix face. That branching is the Q cycle, and it is the reason the complex's proton count is 4 rather than 2.

Complex IV performs the single most dangerous reaction in the cell: the four-electron reduction of O₂ to two water molecules. Partially reduced oxygen species — superoxide, peroxide, hydroxyl radical — are all far more reactive than O₂ itself, so the enzyme must accumulate four electrons before releasing anything. It does this at the binuclear haem a₃–CuB centre, which holds every intermediate covalently bound until the reduction is complete. That is why complex IV, despite handling all the oxygen, leaks almost no ROS — while complexes I and III, which handle none, produce most of it.

The order of the cytochromes needs no 口訣 either — it is written into the complexes' own names. Complex III is cytochrome bc₁. Complex IV is cytochrome aa₃. Cytochrome c is the mobile carrier that walks between them. Read those three left to right and you have b → c₁ → c → a → a₃, which is exactly the order the redox tower gives (+0.077, +0.22, +0.254, +0.29, +0.35 V). What makes people reach for a bad mnemonic here is that the alphabet appears to run backwards at the end; the complex names make it free.

Go deeper · the Q cycle, and where the "4 H⁺" of complex III really comes from

Complex III has two quinone-binding sites on opposite sides of the membrane: Qo facing the intermembrane space and Qi facing the matrix. One full cycle consumes two QH₂ at Qo and regenerates one at Qi, so the net is one QH₂ oxidised per two cytochrome c reduced.

  1. QH₂ binds Qo and releases 2 H⁺ into the intermembrane space. Its first electron goes to the Rieske centre → cyt c₁ → cyt c. It leaves behind an unstable semiquinone.
  2. The semiquinone's second electron takes the other route: cyt bL → cyt bH → a quinone waiting at Qi, making a semiquinone there.
  3. A second QH₂ repeats step 1, releasing 2 more H⁺ and sending another electron down the b chain. That electron completes the reduction at Qi, which now takes 2 H⁺ from the matrix to become QH₂ again.
QH₂ + 2 cyt cox + 2H⁺(N) → Q + 2 cyt cred + 4H⁺(P)net per QH₂ oxidised, after the two half-cycles are added

Now the honest bookkeeping. Four protons appear on the P side, but only two of them were taken from the matrix by complex III itself; the other two rode across on the QH₂, having been loaded onto Q down in the matrix by complex I or complex II. Ubiquinone is therefore not merely an electron carrier — it is a proton carrier, and the conventional "4 at complex III" includes its cargo. This is why the standard-state arithmetic looks tight: complex III's own redox span of −40 kJ·mol⁻¹ is paying for two protons, not four.

Two consequences worth carrying. First, the Qo semiquinone is long-lived enough to hand an electron directly to O₂, which makes it — alongside complex I — one of the two major sources of mitochondrial superoxide, and explains why antimycin A (which blocks Qi and so strands electrons on the b chain) massively increases ROS production. Second, the two sites are pharmacologically distinct: atovaquone blocks Qo of Plasmodium and Pneumocystis cytochrome b selectively enough to be a useful drug, because those parasites' cytochrome b differs from ours at the binding pocket.

The three things this section is asked about

Which complexes pump. I, III and IV — 4, 4 and 2 — giving 10 per NADH and 6 per FADH₂. Complex II never. What each complex contains. FMN and Fe–S in I; covalently bound FAD in II; cytochromes b and c₁ plus the Rieske centre in III; two haems and two coppers in IV. Where an inhibitor acts and what happens downstream. Carriers upstream of a block become fully reduced and carriers downstream become fully oxidised — the crossover observation, and historically the way the order of the chain was worked out. If a question tells you cyt b is reduced while cyt c is oxidised, the block is between them: complex III.

§ 07

The proton-motive force and chemiosmosis

The ideaFor thirty years people looked for a molecule that carried energy from the electron chain to the ATP-making enzyme, and never found one, because there is no such molecule. What connects them is not a substance but a situation: protons piled up on one side of a sealed membrane, which are both more concentrated and more positive over there than over here. Two separate forces therefore push them back, and the machine that makes ATP simply stands in the doorway and takes a toll. This is why an intact membrane is a chemical requirement, and why a hole anywhere in it wrecks the whole system.

Definition & formula

The total free energy stored per mole of protons displaced across the inner membrane, expressed either as a voltage (V) or, multiplied by F, as an energy (kJ·mol⁻¹). It has two components that add, because the matrix is both negative and alkaline.

The electrical term does most of the work — and that is not an accident

Of the ~19 kJ·mol⁻¹, roughly three-quarters comes from Δψ and only a quarter from ΔpH. The reason is physical. A membrane is a very thin capacitor, so displacing a tiny number of protons — far too few to register as a pH change — already builds a large voltage. To shift matrix pH by a whole unit you would have to move orders of magnitude more protons, against buffering. The mitochondrion therefore banks its energy mostly as charge.

The immediate consequence is diagnostic. Anything that dissipates the voltage uncouples just as effectively as something that carries protons. Valinomycin is a K⁺ ionophore that moves no protons at all, yet it collapses Δψ and abolishes ATP synthesis. That single observation is a favourite exam item, because it separates people who have understood Δp from people who have memorised "uncouplers carry protons".

The evidence that convinced everyone

Mitchell proposed chemiosmosis in 1961 and it was resisted for a decade, largely because it made a structural claim rather than a chemical one. Four lines of evidence settled it, and each of them is the kind of thing an examiner turns into "which observation supports…":

  1. No high-energy intermediate has ever been isolated, despite an enormous search. The chemical-coupling hypothesis predicted one; chemiosmosis predicts none.
  2. A sealed, intact inner membrane is required. Puncture the vesicle and electron transport continues while ATP synthesis stops. No purely chemical mechanism would care about a hole.
  3. The Jagendorf–Uribe acid-bath experiment (1966). Chloroplast thylakoids were soaked in pH 4 buffer until their interiors equilibrated, then jumped into pH 8 buffer containing ADP and Pi. They made ATP in the dark, with no electron transfer at all. A pH gradient alone was sufficient.
  4. The Racker–Stoeckenius reconstitution (1974). Artificial vesicles were built from just two proteins: bacteriorhodopsin, a light-driven proton pump from a halophilic archaeon, and mitochondrial ATP synthase from beef heart. Shine a light and the vesicles made ATP. Two proteins from utterly unrelated organisms, with no chemistry in common and no shared intermediate, coupled solely by a proton gradient. This is the decisive one.

Mitchell received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the hypothesis; Boyer and Walker shared the 1997 prize for the mechanism of the enzyme it predicted.

Respiratory control: why mitochondria only burn when asked

Isolated mitochondria given substrate and oxygen but no ADP consume oxygen very slowly (state 4). Add ADP and consumption jumps several-fold (state 3) until the ADP is used up, then falls back. The ratio of the two rates is the respiratory control ratio, and a high one is the standard assay of intact, well-coupled mitochondria.

The mechanism is pure back-pressure, and it is worth stating precisely because it explains half of § 09. Without ADP, ATP synthase cannot turn; protons cannot return; Δp rises until the free energy of pumping one more proton exceeds what the redox drops can supply. At that point the pumps stall, and because the pumps have stalled, electron flow stops, and because electron flow has stopped, oxygen is not consumed. The gradient is not just a store — it is also the signal that tells the chain to slow down. Nothing measures ATP demand; the demand measures itself.

Exam technique · proton-motive force arithmetic

  1. Convert the pH term to volts before anything else — 0.0615 V per pH unit at 37 °C (0.059 at 25 °C). A ΔpH of 0.75 is worth 0.046 V, not 0.75 of anything. Getting this into the same units as Δψ is the entire difficulty of the question.
  2. Add the two terms. Never subtract them. The matrix is negative and alkaline, so both push a proton inwards: Δp = 0.15 + 0.05 ≈ 0.20 V. An option quoting ≈0.10 V is the subtraction trap and dies on sight; so does anything above ≈0.25 V, which is outside the physiological range for a mitochondrion.
  3. One multiplication turns it into energy — ΔG = FΔp = 96.5 × 0.196 ≈ 19 kJ per mole of H⁺. Have 19 memorised outright; every "is that enough?" question turns on it.
  4. Compare against the right cost. One ATP costs ≈50 kJ·mol⁻¹ in a cell, not 30.5. So a single proton buys about two-fifths of an ATP, three is the bare thermodynamic minimum, and the real figure is about four. Use 30.5 and you will conclude the mitochondrion is wasteful — a wrong conclusion that gets written into option lists as a plausible-sounding sentence.
  5. Recognition rule. If a stem mentions valinomycin, K⁺, or "collapses the membrane potential without transporting any protons", the examiner is testing whether you know Δψ supplies about three-quarters of Δp. The answer is that ATP synthesis stops anyway; any option reasoning that an uncoupler must carry protons is wrong.

§ 08

ATP synthase and rotational catalysis

The ideaThe enzyme that makes ATP is not a conventional catalyst at all — it is a rotary motor, the smallest one known. Protons flowing back through a channel spin a ring embedded in the membrane, the ring turns a bent shaft, and the shaft pushes in turn against three catalytic sites so that each one is squeezed and released once per revolution. The surprising part is what the energy is spent on. Making the bond between ADP and phosphate costs almost nothing once both are held tightly enough; what costs energy is prising the finished ATP off the enzyme. The motor is there to open the hand, not to close it.

The energy is spent on release, not on synthesis

This is the counter-intuitive core of the mechanism and it is examined precisely because it is counter-intuitive. Boyer's isotope-exchange experiments showed that when and Pi are bound at the tight site, ATP forms and reverses many times over before anything is released — the equilibrium constant at the enzyme surface is close to 1, so ΔG for the chemical step is nearly zero. Enzyme-bound ATP is not a high-energy species; it is stabilised by the protein far more than ADP and Pi are. What that stabilisation costs is the ability to let go. So the proton-motive force is spent turning γ, which forces the tight site open and evicts an ATP that would otherwise never leave.

Two experiments nailed it. Walker's 1994 crystal structure of F₁ found the three β subunits in three different conformations with three different ligand states in a single crystal — exactly what the binding-change model demanded and what a conventional enzyme would never show. Noji and Kinosita (1997) stuck an F₁ head to a glass slide, attached a fluorescent actin filament to the γ subunit, added ATP, and filmed the filament rotating in discrete 120° steps, always anticlockwise viewed from the membrane. The motor had been watched running backwards.

O, L and T need no mnemonic — the letters are the mechanism. A site binds ADP and Pi Loosely, holds them Tightly while they condense, then Opens and lets the ATP go: L → T → O is simply bind, make, release, and one 120° step of γ moves every site one place along. The same is true of the rotor and the stator — whatever is physically glued to the spinning c-ring is rotor (c-ring, γ, ε) and whatever has to be held still is stator (a, b₂, δ and the α₃β₃ head). The only thing here that is not derivable is the direction of causation, and it is the counter-intuitive one above: the proton flux is spent on T→O, not on L→T. Any option saying the energy is spent forming the phosphoanhydride bond is wrong however reasonable it sounds — it is the exact belief the binding-change model was proposed to overturn.

Why the ATP yield is not a whole number

Every old textbook said 3 ATP per NADH because it assumed the coupling had to be integral — that some fixed number of ATP was produced per pair of electrons. Chemiosmosis removes that assumption entirely. Electrons and ATP are connected only through a common pool of protons, and there is no reason whatever for the proton cost of an ATP and the proton yield of an NADH to share a common factor. In mammals the c-ring has 8 subunits, so 3 ATP cost 8 protons; a further ~1 proton pays to import Pi and swap ATP for ADP (§ 10); 10 protons per NADH divided by ~4 per exported ATP gives 2.5. If a question asks why the modern figures are non-integral, the answer is one sentence: because the intermediate is a gradient rather than a molecule.

Oligomycin blocks F₀, and that is why it stops respiration too

Oligomycin does not touch the electron-transport chain. It plugs the proton channel at the a–c interface (DCCD does the same by covalently modifying the c-subunit carboxylate). Yet adding oligomycin to respiring mitochondria makes oxygen consumption fall. Students often mark this wrong. The reason is respiratory control from § 07: with no return path the gradient rises until the pumps cannot push against it, so electron flow stalls. Blocking the exit blocks the entrance. The distinguishing observation is the gradient itself — an oligomycin-treated mitochondrion is hyperpolarised, whereas an inhibitor of complex III or IV leaves it depolarised.

§ 09

Inhibitors, uncouplers and what goes wrong

The ideaThere are exactly three ways to break this machine, and they look completely different from the outside. You can block the electron path, in which case nothing moves and nothing is made. You can block the ATP exit, in which case pressure builds up and, because of the back-pressure, the electron path stops too. Or you can poke a hole in the container, in which case the electrons rush through faster than ever, the pressure never builds, no ATP appears and all of the energy comes out as heat. Learning to tell those three signatures apart is the single highest-yield thing on this page.

Definition & mechanism

Uncoupler（解偶聯劑）
An agent that dissipates the proton-motive force without passing through ATP synthase, so that electron transport and phosphorylation become independent of each other. Classically a lipophilic weak acid: it picks up a proton on the acidic side, crosses the bilayer in its neutral form, and releases the proton in the alkaline matrix — a protonophore.
uncoupler: O₂ consumption ↑↑ · ATP ↓ · Δp ↓ · heat ↑ the only class that makes respiration go faster while making less ATP
Full definition, symbols and units
Ionophore versus protonophore
A protonophore (DNP, FCCP, CCCP) carries H⁺ and collapses both components of Δp. An ionophore such as valinomycin carries K⁺ and collapses only Δψ — which, since Δψ supplies about three-quarters of Δp, is enough to uncouple on its own.

The oligomycin-then-uncoupler experiment

If one experiment appears on this topic, it is this one, usually as an oxygen-electrode trace. Mitochondria are respiring on succinate with ADP and Pi present, consuming oxygen briskly. Add oligomycin: consumption slows to a crawl, because the protons have nowhere to return to and the gradient stalls the pumps. Now add DNP: consumption resumes and in fact overshoots the original rate, because the protons now have a completely free path home and the pumps face no back-pressure at all. ATP synthesis, however, does not return — it stopped when oligomycin went in and the uncoupler cannot restore it, since the protons bypass the enzyme entirely.

The take-home is that an uncoupler relieves an ATP-synthase inhibitor's effect on respiration but not on phosphorylation. An uncoupler cannot, however, rescue a chain inhibitor: if the electrons cannot reach oxygen, opening a hole in the membrane changes nothing. That asymmetry is the discriminator examiners build the question around.

Telling the three ways of breaking oxidative phosphorylation apart

ATP always falls · up O₂ = uncoupler · up Δp = plugged exit

ATP always falls — in all three classes — so ATP is never the discriminator; read the other two columns instead. Up O₂ = uncoupler (the two u's travel together): O₂ consumption rises in exactly one situation, an uncoupler (DNP, FCCP, salicylate in overdose, UCP1, and valinomycin, which collapses only Δψ and still uncouples). Up Δp = plugged exit (the two p's travel together): Δp rises in exactly one situation, a block on the way out — oligomycin and DCCD plugging F₀, atractyloside and bongkrekic acid plugging the translocase. Neither arrow up, everything down, is the third class: anything else — rotenone, malonate, antimycin A, cyanide, CO, H₂S, azide — is a chain inhibitor, and all three columns fall together. My own wording. Note what it is: a rule for sorting an agent into one of three classes. It does not tell you where within the chain a chain inhibitor sits — for that you need the crossover observation or the substrate test in the technique block below.

Exam technique · agent questions, the most reliably examined thing on this page

  1. Sort the agent into a box before you read the options. Chain inhibitor (Δp↓, O₂↓) · exit blocker (Δp↑, O₂↓) · uncoupler (Δp↓, O₂↑). Two of the five options will normally contradict the box you chose and die on the spot — and with −0.5 marking, one eliminated option is already enough to make answering strictly better than leaving it blank.
  2. If the stem names a substrate, it is asking about the entry point. Respiration continues on succinate but fails on pyruvate, malate or glutamate → the block is complex I (rotenone, amytal, MPP⁺). Fails on succinate only → complex II, and since malonate is competitive, more succinate reverses it. Fails on both → complex III or IV.
  3. If the stem gives you redox states, use crossover. Everything upstream of a block goes reduced, everything downstream oxidised. "Cytochrome b reduced, cytochrome c oxidised" places the block between them: complex III.
  4. For two-agent sequences, apply the asymmetry. An uncoupler restores respiration after oligomycin but never restores ATP, and it rescues nothing at all after a chain inhibitor. Any option in which ATP synthesis resumes once DNP is added is wrong by construction.
  5. For a clinical stem, read the venous saturation. Normal PaO₂ + severe lactic acidosis + high venous O₂ saturation = histotoxic, i.e. complex IV. An uncoupler does the opposite: it raises extraction and lowers venous saturation, which kills that distractor immediately. Cyanide binds ferric a₃, CO binds ferrous — and if you forget which, re-derive it from the antidote, since nitrite works by making methaemoglobin, whose Fe³⁺ is the decoy that pulls cyanide away.
  6. Do not be moved by a true sentence. "Complex II's FAD is covalently bound" and "citrate is a symmetric molecule" are both true and both appear as wrong options on this topic, because distractors are often lifted verbatim from textbook prose. The test is whether the option answers this stem, not whether it is a fact.

Cyanide, carbon monoxide and the arterialised venous blood

A smoke-inhalation victim with severe lactic acidosis, a normal PaO₂ and an abnormally high venous oxygen saturation has a histotoxic problem, not a delivery problem: oxygen is arriving but the tissues cannot reduce it, so it returns unused in the venous blood. That is cytochrome c oxidase inhibition. Cyanide binds the ferric haem a₃; treatment is hydroxocobalamin (which chelates CN⁻ to give cyanocobalamin), or sodium nitrite to generate methaemoglobin whose Fe³⁺ acts as a decoy sink, plus sodium thiosulfate as sulfur donor for rhodanese, which converts cyanide to thiocyanate. Carbon monoxide is different in an examinable way: it binds the ferrous form of both haemoglobin and a₃, so it impairs carriage and utilisation, and its pulse-oximetry reading is falsely normal. Hydrogen sulfide, from sewers and slurry pits, inhibits the same site and is the reason confined-space rescuers so often become the second casualty.

Go deeper · mitochondrial genetics, heteroplasmy and the OXPHOS diseases

Human mtDNA is a 16.6 kb circle carrying 13 polypeptides, 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs. Every one of the 13 is a subunit of an oxidative-phosphorylation complex: 7 of complex I (ND1–6, ND4L), 1 of complex III (cytochrome b), 3 of complex IV (COI–III) and 2 of ATP synthase (ATP6, ATP8). Complex II is the exception — entirely nuclear-encoded, which is why SDH disease is Mendelian while the rest can be maternal.

Three genetic ideas do most of the work in a vignette:

  • Maternal inheritance. Sperm mitochondria are eliminated after fertilisation, so an affected mother transmits to all her children and an affected father to none.
  • Heteroplasmy and the threshold effect. A cell contains thousands of mtDNA copies; mutant and wild-type coexist. Symptoms appear only above a mutant load threshold, which differs by tissue — highest demand tissues (CNS, retina, cardiac and skeletal muscle, cochlea, β cells) fail first. This explains the variable expressivity that looks so unlike Mendelian disease.
  • The germline bottleneck. Only a small sample of mtDNAs is passed to each oocyte, so sibling mutant loads — and therefore severities — can differ wildly.

Reactive oxygen species: the unavoidable side reaction

Roughly 0.1–2 % of the oxygen consumed by mitochondria is reduced by only one electron, giving superoxide. The culprits are the sites where an electron sits on a carrier long enough to meet O₂: the flavin and the Q-binding site of complex I, especially during reverse electron transport, and the Qo semiquinone of complex III. Complex IV, counter-intuitively, contributes almost nothing, because it never lets a partially reduced oxygen species go.

The defence is a cascade: superoxide dismutase — MnSOD (SOD2) in the matrix, Cu/Zn-SOD (SOD1) in the cytosol and intermembrane space — converts O₂•− to H₂O₂; then catalase and glutathione peroxidase (a selenoenzyme) remove the peroxide, the latter at the cost of NADPH regenerated by glutathione reductase and ultimately by the pentose phosphate pathway. Note the connection this creates: a red cell with G6PD deficiency cannot make NADPH fast enough, so an oxidant challenge causes haemolysis. What looks like a haematology fact is a consequence of the chemistry on this page.

Malignant hyperthermia is not uncoupling

It is tempting to file every hyperthermic crisis under "uncoupler", but the mechanisms differ and examiners exploit that. Malignant hyperthermia is uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release through a mutant ryanodine receptor after volatile anaesthetics or suxamethonium; the heat comes from frantic ATP hydrolysis by the SERCA pump and by actomyosin, so ATP falls and rigidity is prominent — treated with dantrolene. DNP or salicylate uncouple genuinely: ATP falls because it is never made, and the tissue is not rigid. Thyroid hormone raises basal metabolic rate partly by increasing proton leak and partly by inducing more Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — physiological, gradual, and not a crisis. Same symptom, three different places in the machine.

§ 10

Shuttles, transport and the honest ATP ledger

The ideaGlycolysis happens outside the mitochondrion and loads two carriers with electrons there; the machinery that can spend them is inside, behind a membrane the carriers cannot cross. So the electrons are moved across without the carrier: something reducible is reduced on the outside, ferried in, and re-oxidised on the inside. There are two ways of doing it, they deposit the electrons at different points in the chain, and that is the entire reason the famous ATP totals differ between tissues. Add the cost of exporting the finished ATP, and the honest total for one glucose is 30 or 32 — not 36 or 38.

Definition & the two shuttles

Substrate shuttle
A pair of dehydrogenases on opposite sides of the inner membrane plus a transporter for the reduced metabolite. The electrons cross; the does not. There is no NADH transporter in the inner membrane, and there never was.
cytosolic NADH → 2.5 ATP (malate–aspartate) · → 1.5 ATP (glycerol 3-phosphate) the difference is only where the electrons enter: complex I versus the Q pool
Full definition, symbols and units
Malate–aspartate shuttle
Liver, kidney, heart. Cytosolic malate dehydrogenase reduces oxaloacetate to malate; the malate/α-ketoglutarate antiporter carries malate in; matrix malate dehydrogenase regenerates NADH inside. Oxaloacetate cannot cross, so it is transaminated to aspartate and exported on the electrogenic glutamate/aspartate carrier. Fully reversible — it can run backwards when the cytosol needs NAD⁺.
Glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle
Skeletal muscle, brain, brown fat, insect flight muscle. Cytosolic NAD-linked glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reduces DHAP; the mitochondrial isoform is a flavoprotein on the outer face of the inner membrane that hands electrons straight to ubiquinone. Effectively irreversible and fast, because it never needs the matrix NAD⁺/NADH ratio to cooperate.
Adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT)
Antiports ADP³⁻ in for ATP⁴⁻ out. Because the charges differ, the exchange is electrogenic and is driven by Δψ — it costs part of the gradient. With the H₂PO₄⁻/H⁺ symporter that brings phosphate in, the export of one ATP costs about one extra proton.

The reason for the difference is worth saying in one sentence, because it is the whole of the answer to a common question: the shuttles do not differ in how much energy they carry, only in where they put it down. Malate–aspartate rebuilds a genuine NADH inside the matrix, so its electrons traverse all three pumping sites (10 H⁺). The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle hands its electrons to a flavoprotein that reduces ubiquinone directly, so they skip complex I entirely (6 H⁺). Divide each by the ~4 H⁺ an exported ATP costs and you get 2.5 and 1.5.

Which tissue runs which shuttle — and therefore 30 or 32

HULK hits 32 · the three B's stop at 30

H·U·L·K — Heart, Liver, Kidney, the U there only to make it a word — run the malate–aspartate shuttle (蘋果酸–天門冬胺酸), rebuild a genuine NADH inside the matrix, and reach 32; the Hulk is the strong one, which is how you keep the bigger number on the right list. The three B's — Brain, Brown adipose, Biceps, i.e. skeletal muscle — run the glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle (甘油-3-磷酸), put their electrons straight onto ubiquinone, and reach 30. The hook exists only because the tissue lists are arbitrary; the two-ATP gap is not, and you should be able to produce it in one line — 2 cytosolic NADH × 2.5 = 5 against 2 × 1.5 = 3. Mine, not a circulating hook. Keep the underlying logic as a cross-check: the reversible, higher-yield shuttle serves organs that must also be able to regenerate cytosolic NAD⁺ in either direction, while the one-way, faster shuttle serves tissues that buy rate at a cost of two ATP per glucose. It says nothing about heart being fast — heart chooses yield.

Why 36 and 38 were retired

The old figures came from assuming 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH₂, and those in turn came from an era that assumed the coupling had to be a whole number. Four separate corrections turned them into 30 and 32:

  1. Measured proton stoichiometries are 10 per NADH and 6 per succinate, not the values that would give whole-number ATP.
  2. The c-ring has 8 subunits in mammals, so the synthase itself charges 8/3 = 2.67 H⁺ per ATP, not 3.
  3. Export is not free. ANT and the phosphate carrier together cost about one more proton per ATP delivered to the cytosol, bringing the total to ~4.
  4. There is a proton leak. Real membranes are not perfectly sealed, so the effective yield is lower still, and it is not a constant — it varies with tissue, with thyroid status and with how hard the mitochondrion is working.

Point 4 is the one to internalise. 2.5 and 1.5 are themselves approximations, not new constants. The honest statement is "about 2.5", and the honest total for glucose is "about 30–32". Write the modern number, and if a paper's options are built on 36/38, notice that it is testing recall of an old textbook and answer accordingly — but be able to explain why the newer figure is right.

Exam technique · ATP ledgers and P/O ratios

  1. Read the noun in the stem before anything else — per glucose 30 or 32, per pyruvate 12.5, per acetyl-CoA 10. The commonest lost mark on this whole topic is a correctly computed number answering the wrong question, and 10 is deliberately offered as a distractor when the stem says pyruvate.
  2. Build the glucose ledger as four terms, always in the same order — 4 substrate-level (2 from glycolysis + 2 GTP), then 8 matrix NADH × 2.5 = 20, then 2 FADH₂ × 1.5 = 3, then the shuttle term: 2 × 2.5 = 5 or 2 × 1.5 = 3. So 4 + 20 + 3 + 5 = 32, or 4 + 20 + 3 + 3 = 30. Only the last term ever changes, so build it once and switch one number.
  3. Count each NADH once. There are 10 NADH per glucose in total — 2 cytosolic, 2 from PDH, 6 from the cycle — and the 2 cytosolic ones are the shuttle term. Adding them again on top of "10 × 2.5" gives 37 or 35, and those two numbers appearing in your working are the signal that you double-counted.
  4. P/O is per oxygen atom. If the data come in µmol O₂, double before dividing. The built-in check is that no P/O can exceed 2.5, so a result of 5.0 or 3.0 means you divided by O₂ instead of O — halve it and move on.
  5. Read a P/O backwards to name the substrate. 2.5 → the electrons entered at complex I (pyruvate, malate, glutamate, β-hydroxybutyrate). 1.5 → they entered at the Q pool (succinate, and equally fatty-acyl-CoA via ETF or glycerol 3-phosphate). ≈0 → the preparation is uncoupled, because a P/O ratio is only defined in coupled mitochondria.
  6. If the option list is built on 36 and 38, the examiner is testing recall of an older textbook. Pick the classical figure only when 30 and 32 are both absent; if both pairs appear, the modern one is intended, and you should be able to justify it in a sentence — the intermediate is a gradient, so the coupling has no reason to be a whole number.
Worked example 6 — the full ledger, and how efficient the process really is

One glucose is completely oxidised in a hepatocyte. (a) Build the ATP ledger using P/O = 2.5 and 1.5. (b) Repeat for a skeletal muscle fibre. (c) Given that complete combustion of glucose has ΔG°′ = −2840 kJ·mol⁻¹, what fraction of the available energy is conserved — at standard state, and under cellular conditions where ATP synthesis costs ~50 kJ·mol⁻¹?

  1. (a) Substrate-level: 2 from glycolysis + 2 GTP from the cycle = 4.
  2. Matrix NADH: 2 (PDH) + 6 (cycle) = 8, so 8 × 2.5 = 20.
  3. FADH₂: 2 × 1.5 = 3.
  4. Cytosolic NADH via malate–aspartate: 2 × 2.5 = 5. Total 4 + 20 + 3 + 5 = 32.
  5. (b) Only the last line changes: 2 × 1.5 = 3, so 4 + 20 + 3 + 3 = 30.
  6. (c) Standard state: 32 × 30.5 = 976 kJ·mol⁻¹; 976 / 2840 = 34.4 %.
  7. Cellular: 32 × 50 = 1600 kJ·mol⁻¹; 1600 / 2840 = 56.3 %.
  8. Read the second figure carefully. Better than half the chemical energy of glucose ends up in ATP, which is far better than any heat engine you own. The remainder is not "lost" — it is the entropy production that makes the whole sequence run forward instead of sitting at equilibrium.

Worked example 7 — reading a P/O ratio off an oxygen-electrode experiment

A suspension of isolated mitochondria is given a substrate and excess Pi. A pulse of 25 µmol ADP is added, and the electrode records the consumption of 5 µmol O₂ before respiration returns to the slow resting rate. (a) What is the P/O ratio? (b) Which substrate was it? (c) What would the same experiment give with succinate, and what would happen if 2,4-dinitrophenol were present throughout?

  1. (a) P/O is defined per oxygen atom. 5 µmol O₂ = 10 µmol O.
  2. P/O = 25 / 10 = 2.5.
  3. (b) 2.5 is the NAD-linked value, so the substrate fed electrons in at complex I — malate, pyruvate, glutamate or β-hydroxybutyrate. (Had you divided by 5 instead of 10 you would have got 5.0, an impossible number, which is the built-in check.)
  4. (c) Succinate enters at the Q pool: P/O = 1.5, so the same 25 µmol ADP would require 25 / 1.5 = 16.7 µmol O = 8.3 µmol O₂ — two-thirds more oxygen for the same ATP.
  5. With DNP present, oxygen would be consumed rapidly and continuously, the ADP pulse would produce no jump at all because respiration was never controlled in the first place, and the measured P/O would fall towards zero. A P/O ratio is only meaningful in coupled mitochondria.

Three arithmetic slips that cost whole questions

Per glucose versus per pyruvate versus per acetyl-CoA. Read which the question asks; they are 30–32, 12.5 and 10. P/O₂ instead of P/O. Doubling errors here look like a factor-of-two misunderstanding of the whole chain. Counting the cytosolic NADH twice — once as 2 NADH from glycolysis and again as part of the "10 NADH per glucose": there are 10 NADH in total, of which 2 are cytosolic and therefore discounted by the shuttle.
