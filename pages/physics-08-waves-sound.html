Waves and Sound

One idea carries this entire chapter: the medium sets the speed, the source sets the frequency, and the wavelength is whatever those two force it to be. Every result below — why λ changes at a boundary but f does not, why a closed pipe drops an octave, why beats and the Doppler shift look so similar yet come from opposite causes, why a 12 MHz probe cannot see a kidney — is that one sentence applied under different constraints.

§ 01

What a wave is, and what actually travels

The ideaDrop a pebble into a pond and something is obviously moving outwards. It is not the water. Every part of the pond simply bobs about the spot it already occupied and nudges its neighbour into doing the same a moment later, so what actually crosses the pond is the bobbing itself — a disturbance, and the energy needed to keep it going. Once you accept that the stuff stays put, the only real question left is which way each bit of it moves: sideways to the direction the disturbance is heading, or back and forth along it.

Definition & formula

Mechanical wave
A self-propagating disturbance of a medium that carries energy and momentum through the medium while each piece of the medium only oscillates about its own equilibrium position.
y(x, t) = f(x − vt) any shape that keeps its form and slides at speed v in +x
Full definition, symbols and units
Transverse wave
A wave in which the displacement of the medium is perpendicular to the direction the wave travels.
Longitudinal wave
A wave in which the displacement of the medium is parallel to the direction the wave travels — a moving pattern of compressions and rarefactions.
y(x, t) = f(x + vt)same shape travelling in −x
v = restoring stiffnessinertiathe shape of every mechanical wave-speed formula
ydisplacement of the medium from equilibrium (m)
xposition along the direction of travel (m)
ttime (s)
vwave (phase) speed — speed of the pattern, not of the matter (m·s⁻¹)

Start with the negative statement, because it is where marks are lost: a wave does not transport matter. When you speak, no air molecule from your larynx reaches the listener. Each molecule jiggles a fraction of a micrometre about a fixed point and hands the disturbance on. What crosses the room is energy and phase — the "where in the cycle we are" information — at a speed set entirely by the air.

The reason f(x − vt) is the definition of "a wave" is worth one line of thought. Ask where the pattern has a particular value: it is wherever the argument x − vt is constant, i.e. wherever x = vt + const. That point moves at speed v. So any function of the combination (x − vt) is automatically a rigid shape gliding right at v. The sine wave of the next section is just the most useful special case.

Which type a medium supports is decided by what restoring force it can supply. A transverse wave requires the medium to resist shear — you have to be able to pull one layer sideways and have it pull back. A longitudinal wave only requires resistance to compression, which every material has. Hence:

  • Fluids (gases and liquids) carry longitudinal waves only. They have a bulk modulus but essentially no shear modulus. Sound in air, in water and in soft tissue is purely longitudinal.
  • Solids carry both. A steel rod transmits compression waves and shear waves, at different speeds.
  • Surface waves on water are a hybrid: the water parcels move in near-circles, part transverse and part longitudinal.

Exam point

The classic application is seismology: P-waves (primary, longitudinal) pass through the Earth's liquid outer core, S-waves (secondary, transverse) do not — which is how we know the outer core is liquid. Medically, the same rule means diagnostic ultrasound in soft tissue is a longitudinal pressure wave, and shear-wave elastography works precisely because tissue, unlike a true fluid, does have a small shear modulus.

§ 02

The vocabulary and the travelling-wave function

The ideaA repeating wave is the same shape happening over and over, so there are only two ways to measure it: how far apart the repeats are laid out in space, and how long apart they arrive in time. This section names those two repeats, plus the size of the swing, and then packs all three into a single expression that tells you where the medium is at any place and any moment. Keep one distinction alive while you read it: the pattern sweeps along fast, while any one speck of the medium only shuffles slowly to and fro on the spot.

Definition & formula

Amplitude A
The maximum displacement from equilibrium — a half-swing, not a peak-to-peak distance.
y(x, t) = A sin(kx − ωt + φ) sinusoidal wave travelling in +x
Full definition, symbols and units
Wavelength λ / period T
The repeat distance of the wave in space, and its repeat interval in time.

The same two repeats expressed as radians of phase: radians per metre and radians per second.

k and ω exist for one reason: the sine function eats radians, not metres or seconds. To advance one full wavelength you must advance the argument by 2π, so kλ = 2π. To advance one full period you must advance it by 2π again, so ωT = 2π. That is the whole derivation. Read k as "radians of phase per metre" and ω as "radians of phase per second", and the ratio ω/k — radians per second divided by radians per metre — is obviously metres per second.

The sign inside the bracket is the direction of travel, and it is worth deriving rather than memorising. A given phase is at kx − ωt = const, so x = (ω/k)t + const: as t increases, x increases. Minus → moves in +x. Plus → moves in −x.

Memory hook

Minus means "later means further right". (kx − ωt) → the wave chases +x; (kx + ωt) → it retreats to −x.

Trap · wave speed is not particle speed

v = ω/k is the speed of the pattern and is fixed by the medium. vy,max = ωA is the top speed of a particle and depends on amplitude. They are different quantities with the same units, and an exam will cheerfully offer you both as options. Turn the volume up on a loudspeaker and vy,max rises while v does not move at all — loud sound does not arrive early.

Worked example 1 · reading everything off a wave function

A transverse wave on a long cord is y(x, t) = 0.020 sin(6.0x − 300t), with x and y in metres, t in seconds. Find A, λ, f, T, the wave speed and direction, and the maximum transverse speed of a point on the cord.

  1. Match to A sin(kx − ωt): A = 0.020 m (2.0 cm), k = 6.0 rad·m⁻¹, ω = 300 rad·s⁻¹.
  2. λ = 2π/k = 6.2832 / 6.0 = 1.047 m ≈ 1.05 m.
  3. f = ω/2π = 300 / 6.2832 = 47.7 Hz; T = 1/f = 0.0209 s ≈ 20.9 ms.
  4. v = ω/k = 300 / 6.0 = 50 m·s⁻¹. The sign is minus, so it travels in +x. Check with v = fλ: 47.746 × 1.0472 = 50.0 m·s⁻¹ ✓
  5. vy,max = ωA = 300 × 0.020 = 6.0 m·s⁻¹ — more than eight times smaller than the wave speed. (ay,max = ω²A = 9.0×10⁴ × 0.020 = 1.8×10³ m·s⁻².)
A = 2.0 cm · λ = 1.05 m · f = 47.7 Hz · v = 50 m·s⁻¹ (+x) · vy,max = 6.0 m·s⁻¹
§ 03

v = fλ: who sets what

The ideaThree numbers describe any repeating wave — how fast it goes, how often it repeats, and how long one repeat is — but they are not equal partners. Whatever the wave is travelling through decides the speed, and the wave gets no say in it. Whatever is doing the shaking decides how often, and nothing further down the line can change that count. The length of one repeat is then simply whatever those two leave over. Getting that pecking order straight is the single most useful thing on this page: strings, pipes, echoes and scanners all follow from it.

Definition & formula

The universal wave relation
In one period the wave advances exactly one wavelength; dividing that distance by that time gives the speed. It holds for every wave — sound, light, water, a wave on a rope — because it is a statement of bookkeeping, not of physics.
v = f λ = λ / T all waves, all media, no conditions
Related formulas, symbols and units
v = v(medium)stiffness and inertia only
f = f(source)conserved across a boundary
λ = v / fthe dependent variable — it obeys the other two
vwave speed (m·s⁻¹)
ffrequency (Hz)
λwavelength (m)
Tperiod (s)

This is the load-bearing idea of the chapter. Speed is a property of the medium: it comes out of the stiffness and density of whatever the wave is moving through, and the wave has no say in the matter. Frequency is a property of the source: a 440 Hz tuning fork pushes the air 440 times a second, and nothing downstream can change that count.

Now put a boundary in the path. On the far side the speed is different. But the interface is driven by the incoming wave — the last layer of medium 1 is physically attached to the first layer of medium 2, so they move together, and they move at the driving frequency. You cannot create or destroy cycles at an interface: whatever arrives per second must leave per second. Frequency is therefore conserved, speed changes, and λ = v/f has no choice but to change in exact proportion to v.

Memory hook

The medium sets the speed, the source sets the frequency, and the wavelength does what it is told.

Worked example 2 · one ultrasound pulse, three wavelengths

A transducer emits a 2.5 MHz pulse. Speeds: fat 1450 m·s⁻¹, average soft tissue 1540 m·s⁻¹, cortical bone 4080 m·s⁻¹. Find the wavelength in each, and state the frequency in bone.

  1. λ = v/f with f = 2.5×10⁶ Hz throughout.
  2. Fat: 1450 / 2.5×10⁶ = 5.80×10⁻⁴ m = 0.580 mm.
  3. Soft tissue: 1540 / 2.5×10⁶ = 6.16×10⁻⁴ m = 0.616 mm.
  4. Bone: 4080 / 2.5×10⁶ = 1.632×10⁻³ m = 1.63 mm — nearly three times longer.
  5. The frequency in bone is still 2.5 MHz. The transducer set it; the bone cannot change it.
λ = 0.580 / 0.616 / 1.63 mm · f = 2.5 MHz everywhere

Trap · "the wave slows down, so the pitch drops"

No. Shout into a swimming pool and the sound in the water is the same note, with a wavelength about 4.3 times longer (1480/343). The only mechanism that changes an observed frequency is relative motion (the Doppler effect, §10) — never a change of medium.

§ 04

Wave speed comes from the medium

The ideaEvery speed formula in this section is one idea wearing different clothes: a disturbance travels quickly through something springy and slowly through something heavy. Tighten a guitar string and a flick races along it; hang weight on the string and the same flick crawls. That is also why the surprising cases are surprising — steel is thousands of times heavier than air, yet sound goes fifteen times faster in it, because steel is springier by far more than it is heavier. Heaviness only decides the contest between materials of similar springiness.

Definition & formula

Wave speed
The rate at which a medium can pass a disturbance along — always the square root of a restoring stiffness divided by an inertia. Stiffer returns faster; heavier responds slower.
v = FTμ small-amplitude transverse wave on a uniform, perfectly flexible string
Related formulas, symbols and units

v = Yρlongitudinal wave along a thin solid rod

Notation trap · T is not T

In v = √(T/μ) as written in most textbooks, T means tension — not period, and not temperature, both of which are also T on this page. This note writes tension as FT to keep them apart. Whenever you see a bare T in a wave formula, check the units: newtons, seconds or kelvin.

Every line above is the same statement. The numerator measures how hard the medium pushes back when you deform it; the denominator measures how much mass has to be accelerated. A tighter string snaps back harder, so v ∝ √FT. A heavier string has more inertia per metre, so v ∝ 1/√μ. The square root is not decoration — it means the returns diminish. Doubling the tension multiplies the speed by only √2 ≈ 1.41; to double the speed you need four times the tension.

The same logic dissolves an intuition that fails constantly: denser is not slower. Steel is about 6500 times denser than air (7800 kg·m⁻³ against 1.2), yet sound travels roughly 15 times faster in steel, because steel's stiffness is up by an enormous factor more than its density is. Density only wins when you compare materials of similar stiffness.

Exam point · why helium changes your voice

Breathing helium does not make your vocal folds vibrate faster — the fundamental pitch is set by the folds and barely moves. What changes is the speed of sound in the gas filling your vocal tract (≈ 1000 m·s⁻¹ instead of 353), so the tract's resonant frequencies fn = n v/4L all shift up by about a factor of three. Different formants, same fundamental — a timbre change, not a pitch change. Note also that v = √(γRT/M) contains no pressure: for an ideal gas, changing the pressure changes ρ in exactly compensating proportion, so altitude does not change the speed of sound except through temperature.

Worked example 3 · speed on a string, and how it scales

A string of length 0.640 m and mass 1.60 g is stretched to a tension of 80.0 N. (a) Find μ and the wave speed. (b) What tension would double the speed?

  1. μ = m/L = 1.60×10⁻³ kg / 0.640 m = 2.50×10⁻³ kg·m⁻¹.
  2. v = √(FT/μ) = √(80.0 / 2.50×10⁻³) = √(3.20×10⁴) = 178.9 m·s⁻¹.
  3. Since v ∝ √FT, doubling v requires four times the tension: FT′ = 4 × 80.0 = 320 N. Check: √(320 / 2.50×10⁻³) = √(1.28×10⁵) = 357.8 m·s⁻¹ = 2 × 178.9 ✓
μ = 2.50×10⁻³ kg·m⁻¹ · v = 178.9 m·s⁻¹ · 320 N to double v
§ 05

Energy, intensity and the inverse-square law

The ideaA wave carries energy, and what you actually notice — how loud, how bright — is how much of that energy lands each second on each patch of whatever is receiving it. Walk away from a small source and nothing has been lost or absorbed; the same energy per second is just being shared out over an ever larger sphere, so each patch is handed a smaller share. Two things make a wave carry more energy in the first place: bigger swings and faster swings, and each of them counts twice over, so doubling the size of the swing delivers four times as much.

Definition & formula

Intensity
The wave power crossing unit area held perpendicular to the direction of travel — how much energy per second lands on each square metre.
I = P4πr² isotropic point source, no absorption
Full definition, symbols and units
Inverse-square law
For a point source radiating equally in all directions into a lossless medium, intensity falls as 1/r² purely because the same power is spread over the growing surface of a sphere.
I₁ r₁² = I₂ r₂²ratio form — avoids ever needing P
I = 2π² ρ v f² A² ∝ A² f²sinusoidal wave; A is displacement amplitude
P = ½ μ v ω² A²average power of a sinusoidal wave on a string
A ∝ 1 / ramplitude of a spherical wave (since I ∝ A²)
Iintensity (W·m⁻²)
Ppower radiated or transported (W)
rdistance from the source (m)
Adisplacement amplitude (m)
ρdensity of the medium (kg·m⁻³)
μlinear mass density of the string (kg·m⁻¹)
ωangular frequency (rad·s⁻¹)

Two proportionalities do most of the work. First, intensity goes as the square of the amplitude, because each oscillating particle is a harmonic oscillator with energy ½mω²A². Halve the amplitude and you quarter the energy. Second, intensity goes as the square of the frequency at fixed amplitude, because a faster oscillation means larger particle speeds for the same excursion. Both squares come from the same place: energy is quadratic in speed.

The 1/r² fall-off is pure geometry, not absorption. Nothing is lost; the same power is smeared over a sphere whose area 4πr² grows quadratically. That is why it fails whenever the geometry is different — an ideal line source gives 1/r, a plane wave in a waveguide gives no fall-off at all, and a focused ultrasound beam actually increases in intensity towards its focus. Real absorption (§11) is an additional, exponential loss on top.

Worked example 4 · a point source in open air

A small loudspeaker radiates 0.80 W of acoustic power uniformly in all directions. (a) What is the intensity 2.0 m away? (b) At what distance has the intensity fallen to 1.0×10⁻³ W·m⁻²?

  1. I = P/(4πr²) = 0.80 / (4π × 2.0²) = 0.80 / 50.27 = 1.59×10⁻² W·m⁻².
  2. Rearrange: r = √(P / 4πI) = √(0.80 / (4π × 1.0×10⁻³)) = √(0.80 / 1.2566×10⁻²) = √63.66 = 7.98 m ≈ 8.0 m.
  3. Sanity check with the ratio form: going from 2.0 m to 8.0 m is ×4 in distance, so ×1/16 in intensity: 1.59×10⁻² / 16 = 9.9×10⁻⁴ W·m⁻² ✓
I(2.0 m) = 1.59×10⁻² W·m⁻² · r = 8.0 m for 1.0×10⁻³ W·m⁻²

Trap · four times the distance is not a quarter of the intensity

It is a sixteenth. The inverse-square law squares the distance ratio. The amplitude, however, falls only by a factor of four, because A ∝ 1/r. Read the question carefully: "intensity" and "amplitude" scale differently.

§ 06

Superposition and interference

The ideaWhen two waves reach the same spot at the same moment, the medium just does both things at once and adds them together — and afterwards each wave carries on as if nothing had happened, which is why two conversations can cross a room without turning to mush. The interesting case is two sources shaking in step but sitting at different distances from you. One wave then has further to travel, so it arrives a little behind the other, and everything depends on how far behind: land crest on crest and the sound is loud, land crest on trough and the two cancel and you hear almost nothing.

Definition & formula

Superposition principle
Where two waves overlap, the displacement of the medium is the algebraic sum of what each wave would produce alone; afterwards each wave continues unchanged.
ytotal = y₁ + y₂ valid while amplitudes are small enough for the medium to stay linear
Full definition, symbols and units
Interference
The steady pattern of reinforcement and cancellation produced when waves of the same frequency meet with a fixed phase relationship.
Δφ = 2π Δrλphase difference produced by a path difference
Δr = n λ  →  constructivesources in phase; n = 0, 1, 2, …
Δr = (n + ½) λ  →  destructivesources in phase; n = 0, 1, 2, …
Amax = A₁ + A₂  ·  Amin = |A₁ − A₂|amplitudes add, intensities do not
Δrpath difference from the two sources to the point (m)
Δφphase difference at that point (rad)
nan integer (dimensionless)
λwavelength in the medium (m)

Superposition is the reason two conversations can cross a room without garbling each other: waves pass through one another and emerge unchanged. It holds because the wave equation is linear — a good approximation until amplitudes get large (a shock wave, §10, is exactly the case where it fails).

Interference is superposition plus bookkeeping. Two identical sources start in step; by the time their waves reach a particular point they have travelled different distances. Every extra wavelength of path is one extra complete cycle, which puts them back in step. Hence: an integer number of wavelengths of path difference → crests meet crests → constructive; a half-integer number → crest meets trough → destructive. That is the whole rule, and it is the same rule that governs the double slit in optics.

Trap · the conditions swap if the sources are out of phase

Δr = nλ means "constructive" only if the two sources are emitting in phase. Reverse the wires on one loudspeaker and it emits antiphase; then Δr = nλ gives silence and Δr = (n + ½)λ gives maximum loudness. Always ask about the source phase before applying the condition. The general statement is the safe one: constructive when the total phase difference (path plus source) is a multiple of 2π.

Worked example 5 · two loudspeakers

Two speakers driven in phase from one amplifier emit a pure 686 Hz tone; v = 343 m·s⁻¹. A listener stands 4.00 m from one speaker and 5.25 m from the other. (a) What do they hear? (b) What is the lowest frequency that would instead give a maximum at that same spot?

  1. λ = v/f = 343 / 686 = 0.500 m.
  2. Δr = 5.25 − 4.00 = 1.25 m. In wavelengths: 1.25 / 0.500 = 2.5 λ.
  3. 2.5 is half-integer, i.e. (n + ½) with n = 2 → destructive interference. If the two speakers are equally loud at that point the tone very nearly vanishes.
  4. For a maximum, Δr must be a whole number of wavelengths: 1.25 = nλ → λ = 1.25/n → f = v/λ = 343n / 1.25 = 274.4 n Hz.
  5. The lowest non-zero case is n = 1: f = 274.4 Hz (λ = 1.25 m).
Δr = 2.5λ → destructive · lowest constructive frequency = 274.4 Hz

Exam point · intensities do not simply add

Two coherent sources each producing intensity I give 4I at a constructive point and 0 at a destructive one — because amplitudes add (A + A = 2A, and I ∝ A² → 4I). Energy is not created: it is redistributed from the dark fringes into the bright ones, and the average over the pattern is still 2I. If the sources are incoherent (independent noise), there is no stable pattern and the intensities really do add to 2I — which is the case assumed in the decibel arithmetic of §09.

§ 07

Reflection and standing waves on a string

The ideaTie a rope down at both ends, shake one end, and whatever you send along it comes straight back at you. The returning wave then overlaps the one still going out, and at most shaking rates the result is an incoherent mess. At a few special rates the two fit together exactly and the rope settles into a pattern that no longer travels anywhere: certain points sit perfectly still while the points between them swing as hard as they can. Those special rates are the only notes the rope can hold, and they are set by its length, its tightness and its weight — which is the entire physics of a guitar.

Definition & formula

Standing wave
The interference pattern of two identical waves travelling in opposite directions: the pattern no longer moves, and every point oscillates in place with a fixed amplitude that depends on where it is.
fn = n v2L,  n = 1, 2, 3, … string fixed at both ends — all harmonics present
Full definition, symbols and units
Node / antinode
A node is a point that never moves; an antinode is a point that swings with maximum amplitude. Adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart, and a node and its neighbouring antinode are λ/4 apart.
λn = 2Lnan integer number of half-wavelengths must fit
f₁ = 12LFTμfundamental, combining §04 and §07

Reflection: the phase flip is the key

Send a pulse down a rope to a fixed end. The rope pulls up on the post; by Newton's third law the post pulls down on the rope, and the reflected pulse comes back inverted — a phase change of π. Send the same pulse to a free end (a light ring sliding on a frictionless pole): there is no reaction force, the end overshoots, and the pulse returns upright, with no phase change.

That single rule determines everything downstream. An inverted reflection superposed on the incoming wave forces the displacement at the boundary to be permanently zero — a node at every fixed end. A non-inverted reflection doubles the displacement there — an antinode at every free end. Standing waves are then simply the wavelengths that fit those end conditions.

Exam point · what a standing wave is not

A standing wave transports no net energy — the two travelling waves carry equal power in opposite directions. It also has no single wavelength on display: in y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt) the position and time dependencies have separated, so nothing propagates. Note too that every point between two nodes moves in phase (they all reach maximum together), and points on opposite sides of a node move in antiphase.

§ 08

Air columns: open-open vs open-closed

The ideaA tube of air behaves exactly like the rope of the previous section: only a few patterns fit inside it, and those few are the notes it will sound. What decides which patterns fit is what the air is allowed to do at each end — free to slosh in and out where the tube is open, held still where it is stopped by a wall. Cap one end of a tube and you change the rules enough to drop its note by an octave and to silence half of the higher notes above it, which is most of the difference between a flute and a clarinet.

Definition & formula

Air column resonance
A longitudinal standing wave inside a tube. The allowed wavelengths are those that place a displacement antinode at every open end and a displacement node at every closed end. Idealised: a real open end radiates, so the antinode sits slightly outside it and L should carry an end correction of about 0.6 × the tube radius.
open-open: fn = n v2L,  n = 1, 2, 3, … both ends open — every harmonic
Related formulas, symbols and units

The boundary conditions come straight from physical common sense, provided you keep track of which quantity you are talking about.

  • Closed (rigid) end: air cannot move through a wall, so the displacement is pinned at zero — a displacement node. But the air piles up against the wall, so the pressure swings hardest there — a pressure antinode.
  • Open end: the air is free to move, giving a displacement antinode; and it is connected to the atmosphere, so the pressure is clamped to atmospheric — a pressure node.

Now count. An open-open pipe needs an antinode at each end, and the shortest pattern with antinodes at both ends is half a wavelength, so L = λ/2 and every L = nλ/2 works. An open-closed pipe needs an antinode at one end and a node at the other; antinode-to-node is a quarter of a wavelength, so L = λ/4, and the next patterns add a further half wavelength each time: L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, … — which is exactly the statement that only odd multiples of the fundamental exist. An even harmonic would require an antinode at both ends or a node at both ends, and neither is available.

Trap · displacement or pressure?

An open end is a displacement antinode and a pressure node. Students routinely state one and reason with the other, and every conclusion after that is wrong. The safe habit: always draw the displacement pattern (as in the figure above), because the boundary conditions are easiest to justify there, then remember that the pressure pattern is its mirror image — nodes where displacement has antinodes and vice versa (§09).

Worked example 7 · the same tube, two ways

A 0.250 m tube is filled with air at 20 °C (v = 343 m·s⁻¹). (a) List its three lowest resonances when one end is closed. (b) Repeat with both ends open. (c) You measure resonances at 343, 1029 and 1715 Hz — which configuration is it?

  1. Closed at one end: f₁ = v/4L = 343 / (4 × 0.250) = 343 / 1.000 = 343 Hz. Only odd n, so the next two are 3f₁ = 1029 Hz and 5f₁ = 1715 Hz.
  2. Open at both ends: f₁ = v/2L = 343 / 0.500 = 686 Hz, then 2f₁ = 1372 Hz and 3f₁ = 2058 Hz.
  3. The measured set 343, 1029, 1715 is in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 → open-closed. The open-open pipe would give 1 : 2 : 3 starting at twice the frequency. Note the open-closed pipe sounds exactly one octave lower than the open-open pipe of the same length.
closed: 343, 1029, 1715 Hz · open: 686, 1372, 2058 Hz · the data are open-closed

Exam point · your ear canal is a stopped pipe

The external auditory canal is roughly 2.5 cm long, open at the pinna and closed by the tympanic membrane. Treating it as an open-closed pipe gives f₁ = v/4L ≈ 343 / (4 × 0.025) ≈ 3.4 kHz (3.5 kHz if you use the 353 m·s⁻¹ of body-temperature air) — and human hearing is indeed most sensitive around 3–4 kHz, with a resonant gain of roughly 10–15 dB. The physics of a clarinet explains an audiogram. (The same quarter-wave logic, with the ~17 cm vocal tract, predicts the first vowel formant near 500 Hz.)

§ 09

Sound as a pressure wave; the decibel

The ideaThere are two equally good ways to describe a sound: as air shuffling back and forth, or as the squeeze at each point rising and falling. They are one and the same wave seen from different angles, and the surprise is that they do not line up — the places where the air moves most are the places where the squeeze changes least. The rest of the section deals with a practical nuisance. The quietest sound you can hear and the loudest you can bear differ by a factor of about a million million, so loudness is quoted on a squashed scale in which equal steps up the scale mean equal multiplications of the energy arriving.

Definition & formula

Sound
A longitudinal wave in a compressible medium, describable either as a displacement wave s(x,t) or as the pressure fluctuation Δp(x,t) about ambient — the two are 90° out of phase.
β = 10 log₁₀ (I / I₀),  I₀ = 1.0×10⁻¹² W·m⁻² I₀ = threshold of hearing at 1 kHz

Displacement and pressure are the same wave, a quarter wavelength apart

Pressure responds to the gradient of displacement, not to displacement itself: Δp = −B ∂s/∂x. Air is compressed where neighbouring parcels are converging, which happens where the displacement curve crosses zero most steeply. So wherever the displacement is zero, the pressure swing is maximal, and vice versa. The physical picture is satisfying: at the instant a region is most compressed, the molecules there have just finished converging and are momentarily at rest.

Speed of sound in air

From v = √(γRT/M), the speed depends on the absolute temperature as √T and on nothing else that varies much. Substituting γ = 1.40, R = 8.31 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, M = 0.0289 kg·mol⁻¹ and T = 293 K gives 343 m·s⁻¹ — which is why the convenient linear fit v ≈ 331 + 0.60 TC reproduces 343 m·s⁻¹ at 20 °C. Two consequences worth stating explicitly:

  • Pressure does not appear. For an ideal gas, raising the pressure raises B and ρ in the same proportion. Sound is not faster at sea level than on a mountain except through the temperature difference.
  • Molar mass and γ do appear. That is the helium effect of §04, and it is also why sound is slightly faster in humid air (H₂O, M = 18, displaces N₂ and O₂, M = 28 and 32).

Decibels

The ear spans about twelve orders of magnitude in intensity, from 10⁻¹² W·m⁻² to about 1 W·m⁻² where sound becomes painful. A logarithmic scale compresses that into a usable 0–120, and it also matches perception, which is roughly logarithmic. The reference I₀ is not a law of nature — it is a convention chosen to put the threshold of hearing at 1 kHz at exactly 0 dB. A pressure check: Δpmax = √(2ρvI₀) = √(2 × 411.6 × 10⁻¹²) ≈ 2.9×10⁻⁵ Pa. That is an amplitude; divide by √2 for the rms value, 2.0×10⁻⁵ Pa — the familiar 20 μPa reference pressure, which is quoted rms. Either way it is about 3×10⁻¹⁰ of atmospheric.

The three numbers to have automatic:

  • +3 dB = ×2 intensity. (10 log₁₀2 = 3.01.) Two identical incoherent machines.
  • +10 dB = ×10 intensity, ×√10 ≈ 3.2 in pressure amplitude, and roughly twice as loud subjectively. The gap between "ten times the energy" and "twice as loud" is the whole reason the decibel exists.
  • +20 dB = ×100 intensity, ×10 amplitude.

Trap · decibels never add arithmetically

Two 60 dB sources give 63 dB, not 120 dB. Convert to intensity, add the intensities, convert back — or use the shortcut +10 log₁₀N. And a corollary that surprises people: adding a 50 dB source to a 70 dB source changes essentially nothing (70.04 dB), because its intensity is a hundred times smaller. The loudest source dominates.

Worked example 8 · from watts to decibels

Use the 0.80 W point source of Worked example 4. (a) Find β at 2.0 m and at 8.0 m. (b) Four such sources are placed together, running independently. Find β at 2.0 m.

  1. At 2.0 m, I = 1.5915×10⁻² W·m⁻². I/I₀ = 1.5915×10⁻² / 10⁻¹² = 1.5915×10¹⁰.
  2. β = 10 log₁₀(1.5915×10¹⁰) = 10 × 10.2018 = 102.0 dB — genuinely damaging with prolonged exposure.
  3. At 8.0 m, I = 0.80/(4π × 64) = 9.947×10⁻⁴ W·m⁻²; β = 10 log₁₀(9.947×10⁸) = 10 × 8.9977 = 90.0 dB.
  4. Cross-check without recomputing: ×4 in distance is ×1/16 in intensity, and 10 log₁₀(1/16) = −12.0 dB. 102.0 − 12.0 = 90.0 ✓
  5. Four independent sources: β = 102.0 + 10 log₁₀4 = 102.0 + 6.0 = 108.0 dB. Four times the power buys 6 dB — a little under twice the perceived loudness.

§ 10

Beats, Doppler and the sonic boom

The ideaSound two notes that are almost, but not quite, the same and you do not hear two pitches — you hear one that swells and fades, over and over. That throbbing is the two waves taking turns to help and to cancel each other, and the nearer the notes are the slower it goes, which is how a piano is tuned by ear. The rest of the section is about motion: if the thing making the sound or the person hearing it is moving, the note that arrives is not the note that was sent, because the journey crowds the arriving waves together or strings them out. Push that crowding to its limit — travel faster than the sound itself — and there is nothing left to crowd: everything the source has ever emitted piles up into a single wall of pressure.

Definition & formula

Beats
The slow rise and fall in loudness heard when two waves of slightly different frequency superpose at the same place. The beat frequency is the difference of the two frequencies.
f′ = f · v + vOv − vS vO, vS counted positive towards the other party; still medium, motion along the line joining them, vS < v

Beats

Add two equal-amplitude cosines and the product formula gives 2A cos(2π·(f₁−f₂)t/2) · cos(2π·(f₁+f₂)t/2): a fast oscillation at the average frequency, inside an envelope oscillating at half the difference. The trap is right there. The envelope has frequency (f₁−f₂)/2, but loudness depends on the magnitude of the envelope, and a sinusoid reaches maximum magnitude twice per cycle. So the audible beat rate is twice the envelope frequency: fbeat = |f₁ − f₂|.

Trap · the factor of two, and the missing sign

440 Hz against 446 Hz gives 6 beats per second, not 3. Also note the absolute value: hearing 6 beats per second tells you the unknown fork is either 434 or 446 Hz. To resolve it you must perturb one fork — add a little mass to the unknown fork (lowering its frequency) and see whether the beating speeds up or slows down.

Doppler: one formula, one memorable rule

Do not memorise four cases. Memorise "approaching raises the pitch" and let it fix the signs. Write

f′ = f v + vOv − vS count both speeds positive when that party is closing the gap

Check it against intuition: if the observer moves towards the source, vO is positive, the numerator grows, f′ rises ✓. If the source moves towards the observer, vS is positive, the denominator shrinks, f′ rises ✓. Receding motions take negative values and the frequency drops. If you can never remember which sign goes where, you can always reconstruct it in two seconds from this test.

The two cases look symmetric but are physically different, and this is the favourite conceptual question of the topic:

  • Moving source: each new crest is emitted from a point further along, so the crests in the medium are genuinely bunched up. The wavelength itself changes: λ′ = (v − vS)/f ahead of the source. The wave speed in the medium does not change.
  • Moving observer: the wavelength in the medium is untouched. The observer simply runs into crests at a higher rate because their closing speed on the wave pattern is v + vO.

Because the air defines a preferred frame, the two are not equivalent — unlike the relativistic Doppler effect for light, where there is no medium and only relative velocity can matter. They agree to first order (f′ ≈ f(1 ± u/v)) and differ at order (u/v)², which Worked example 9 makes concrete.

Exam point · no shift for purely transverse motion

Only the component of velocity along the line joining source and observer produces a shift. A car passing you at its closest point is, at that instant, moving purely transversely — so the sound it emits at that instant reaches you unshifted (a moment later, since it still has to travel), and the pitch drop you notice is spread over the approach and departure, not concentrated at the moment of passing. The same fact is the central limitation of clinical Doppler: a beam at 90° to blood flow measures zero velocity (§11).

Worked example 9 · an ambulance, and why the two cases differ

A siren emits 800 Hz; v = 343 m·s⁻¹. (a) What does a stationary bystander hear as the ambulance approaches at 30.0 m·s⁻¹, and after it has passed? (b) Instead, the ambulance is parked and the bystander drives towards it at 30.0 m·s⁻¹. What now?

  1. Approaching source: vS = +30.0, vO = 0. f′ = 800 × 343 / (343 − 30.0) = 800 × 343/313 = 800 × 1.09585 = 876.7 Hz.
  2. Receding source: vS = −30.0. f′ = 800 × 343 / (343 + 30.0) = 800 × 343/373 = 800 × 0.91957 = 735.7 Hz.
  3. The pitch therefore drops by 876.7 − 735.7 = 141.0 Hz as it goes past — about three semitones, which is why the effect is so audible.
  4. Moving observer, stationary source: vO = +30.0, vS = 0. f′ = 800 × (343 + 30.0)/343 = 800 × 373/343 = 800 × 1.08746 = 870.0 Hz.
  5. 876.7 Hz versus 870.0 Hz. Same relative speed, different answers — because the air is not moving in either case, and it is motion relative to the air that matters. The discrepancy is 0.8 %, of order (30/343)² = 0.76 % ✓
approach 876.7 Hz · recede 735.7 Hz · moving observer 870.0 Hz

The sonic boom in one paragraph

When vS = v the denominator of the Doppler formula hits zero: every wavefront the source has ever emitted arrives at the same instant, and superposition piles them into a single enormous pressure pulse. Beyond that speed the source outruns its own sound and the wavefronts envelope a cone with sin θ = v/vS = 1/M, where M is the Mach number. The boom is not a one-off event at the moment of "breaking the sound barrier" — the cone trails the aircraft continuously, and you hear a boom whenever its surface sweeps over you. Note also that the amplitudes here are far too large for superposition to remain linear; a shock front is precisely where the linear wave theory of this chapter breaks down.

§ 11

Medical ultrasound: what every formula on this page is for

The ideaA scanner does nothing more exotic than shout into the body and listen. The sound goes in from the probe, travels until it reaches a boundary between two different tissues, and part of it bounces back; the machine times how long each echo took to return, and from that alone it knows how deep the boundary lies and how brightly to draw it. Everything clinical then follows from a single tension: short, rapid pulses show the finest detail but are swallowed up before they get far, so choosing a probe is really choosing how deep you are willing to see. And because an echo from something that is moving comes back at a shifted pitch, the same machine can measure flowing blood — but only the part of the flow heading towards or away from the probe ever shows up at all.

Every reflection you see on a B-mode image is the boundary-crossing physics of §03 and §05: at an interface, the fraction of intensity reflected is set by the mismatch in acoustic impedance Z = ρc. Soft tissues have very similar Z, so most of the beam continues and only a few per cent comes back — which is exactly what you want for imaging deep structures. Air (Z ≈ 400) and bone (Z ≈ 7.8×10⁶) are wildly mismatched with tissue (Z ≈ 1.6×10⁶), so nearly everything reflects at those interfaces. That single fact explains the coupling gel (to exclude a layer of air), the acoustic shadow behind a gallstone or a rib, and why lung and bowel gas are ultrasound's blind spots.

The resolution–penetration trade-off

Two consequences of frequency pull in opposite directions:

  • Resolution improves with frequency. Axial resolution is roughly half the spatial pulse length, i.e. a small multiple of λ = c/f₀. Doubling f₀ halves λ and halves the smallest separation you can resolve.
  • Penetration worsens with frequency. Absorption in soft tissue rises roughly linearly with frequency: about 0.5 dB per centimetre per MHz. Doubling f₀ doubles the loss in dB, which is a squaring of the intensity loss.

So the choice of probe is a decision about depth, made in advance and irreversibly. Put an imaging depth of 10 cm through the arithmetic: the pulse travels 20 cm round trip, so at 5 MHz the attenuation is 0.5 × 5 × 20 = 50 dB (a factor of 10⁵ in intensity), and at 10 MHz it is 100 dB (a factor of 10¹⁰). The second echo is simply not detectable.

Exam point · why the factor of 2 in the Doppler shift

The red cell first receives the beam while moving — a moving observer, one Doppler factor. It then re-radiates that received frequency as a scatterer while still moving — a moving source, a second Doppler factor. Two shifts compound, giving Δf = 2f₀v cos θ/c in the low-speed limit. Any clinical Doppler equation that lacks the 2 is being applied to a one-way geometry.

Trap · the cos θ, and why sonographers keep the angle below 60°

At θ = 90° the measured shift is zero however fast the blood is moving — the transverse-motion result of §10. To recover the true velocity the machine divides by cos θ, and the sensitivity of that division to an angle error grows sharply: at 60°, misjudging the angle by 5° changes the reported velocity by about 18 %; at 80° the same 5° error very nearly doubles it. Hence the clinical rule of insonating at θ ≤ 60°. Note this is the exact opposite of the rule for B-mode imaging, where you want the beam perpendicular to the interface to get the strongest specular echo.

Worked example 10 · a carotid Doppler study

A 5.0 MHz probe insonates the carotid at θ = 60° to the flow; c = 1540 m·s⁻¹. (a) Peak systolic blood speed is 0.50 m·s⁻¹ — find the Doppler shift. (b) An echo from a vessel wall returns 130 μs after transmission — how deep is it? (c) What is λ, and roughly what axial resolution does that imply?

  1. Δf = 2f₀ v cos θ / c = 2 × 5.0×10⁶ × 0.50 × cos 60° / 1540 = (5.0×10⁶ × 0.50) / 1540 = 2.5×10⁶ / 1540 = 1.62×10³ Hz ≈ 1.6 kHz.
  2. That lands squarely in the audible range — which is why Doppler machines simply play the shift through a loudspeaker and why an experienced ear can diagnose a stenosis from the sound.
  3. d = ct/2 = 1540 × 130×10⁻⁶ / 2 = 0.2002 / 2 = 0.100 m = 10.0 cm.
  4. λ = c/f₀ = 1540 / 5.0×10⁶ = 3.08×10⁻⁴ m = 0.308 mm. A realistic 2-cycle pulse is about 0.6 mm long, so axial resolution is roughly 0.3 mm — half the pulse length.
