§ 01

Grades and clades: what a classification claims

The ideaFor most of the history of biology, living things were sorted by how much they looked like each other. Since the middle of the twentieth century they have been sorted by who is descended from whom. Those two schemes give different answers, and the questions on this topic sit precisely where they disagree — on a group that feels completely obvious, like "fish" or "reptiles", which turns out not to be a single branch of the family tree. Once you expect that, the topic stops being a memory test and becomes one repeated piece of reasoning.

Definition & key facts

Phylogeny
The evolutionary history of a group — who split from whom, and in what order. A phylogeny is a hypothesis, inferred from evidence and revisable, not an observation.
a classification should recover clades, not grades a clade is a piece of history; a grade is a level of construction that may have been reached more than once
Full definition, symbols and units
Systematics
The study of the diversity of organisms and their evolutionary relationships. Taxonomy — naming and classifying — is the part of systematics that produces names.
Clade
An ancestor and every one of its descendants. Equivalently: a single complete branch cut off the tree. Clades are the only groups a phylogenetic classification recognises.
Grade
A level of organisation or a shared way of life, reached by one or more lineages. "Prokaryote", "invertebrate", "fish", "tree" and "algae" are grades. They are useful descriptions and terrible taxa.
Taxon
An actual named group of organisms (Mammalia, Poaceae, Homo). Distinguish it from a rank or category (class, family, genus), which is the level a taxon is filed at.
clade = ancestor + ALL descendants · grade = a stage, possibly reached repeatedlythe single distinction the whole note turns on

Three ideas, roughly a century apart

Linnaeus (1735–1758) gave us the two-word name and the nested hierarchy, and he meant neither of them historically — for him the ranks recorded a divine plan. Darwin (1859) supplied the reason the hierarchy is nested at all: groups sit inside groups because lineages split and never rejoin, so descent generates exactly the pattern Linnaeus had been filing. And Hennig (1950, translated 1966) turned that insight into a method — phylogenetic systematics, or cladistics — by insisting that only one kind of evidence counts. Not overall similarity, but shared derived characters: novelties that arose in a common ancestor and were inherited by everything descended from it (§ 05).

The consequence is uncomfortable and is exactly what gets examined. If only complete branches may be named, then a great many familiar groups are not taxa at all. Birds are descended from reptiles, so a "Reptilia" that excludes birds is an incomplete branch. Tetrapods are descended from lobe-finned fishes, so "fish" is an incomplete branch. Plants, animals and fungi all arose from single-celled eukaryotes, so "Protista" is what is left over after three branches were cut out of it.

The sentence pattern that is always false

Any stem of the form "X evolved from Y" where Y is a living group. Humans did not evolve from chimpanzees; birds did not evolve from crocodiles; land plants did not evolve from any modern green alga. Living species are all tips, and a tip is never the ancestor of another tip — they are cousins with a shared ancestor that is no longer alive. The only legitimate versions are "X evolved from within Y" said of a clade (birds arose within reptiles; tetrapods arose within sarcopterygians), or "X and Y share a more recent common ancestor with each other than either does with Z".

§ 02

Naming and ranking

The ideaA name has two jobs. It has to pick out one kind of organism unambiguously anywhere in the world, and it has to say something about where that organism sits among the rest. Linnaeus's two-word name does the first job beautifully and is still in universal use. The stack of ranks above it — family, order, class — does the second job only loosely, because a rank is a filing decision made by people, not a quantity anybody measured.

Definition & key facts

Binomial nomenclature
Every species carries a two-part Latinised name: the genus it belongs to, followed by the specific epithet. The two words together are the species name; the epithet alone is not a name and means nothing on its own.
Homo sapiens → H. sapiens after first use · never "sapiens" alone genus capitalised, epithet never; both italicised, or underlined when handwritten
Full definition, symbols and units
Taxonomic hierarchy
Domain, Kingdom, Phylum (called division in botany), Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species. Each rank is more inclusive than the one below it.
Type specimen
A single preserved individual that permanently anchors a name. A name is attached to a specimen, not to a definition — so if the species is later split, the name follows the type.
Priority
Where two names have been given to the same taxon, the earliest validly published one wins. Zoological nomenclature starts from Linnaeus's Systema Naturae (1758), botanical from Species Plantarum (1753).

The order of the ranks

Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup. Read it downwards and each step is less inclusive; a question asking for "the most inclusive taxon listed" wants the one nearest the top of that sentence.

Ranks are labels; clades are the thing being labelled

This is the point worth carrying out of the section. A "family" of beetles and a "family" of birds are not comparable units — nobody measured anything to make them both families. Aves and Insecta are both ranked as classes, and Insecta holds roughly a million described species against Aves' eleven thousand. The branching pattern is a claim about nature that can be right or wrong; the rank label is a bookkeeping decision that can only be conventional or inconvenient. This is why modern practice quietly drops ranks in the middle of the hierarchy and simply names clades — Amniota, Tetrapoda, Ecdysozoa, Archaeplastida — without saying what rank they hold.

Six naming errors that cost marks

1. Treating the epithet as the species name. The species is Homo sapiens. "Sapiens" on its own is not a name and is not unique — americana, vulgaris and officinalis appear in dozens of unrelated genera.

2. Capitalising the epithet or dropping the italics. Escherichia coli, never "Escherichia Coli" and never plain roman text. Higher taxa (Mammalia, Enterobacteriaceae) are capitalised and not italicised — except bacterial and viral higher taxa, which the current codes do italicise.

3. Assuming the abbreviation is unambiguous. E. coli is safe only after Escherichia has been written out; E. could equally be Entamoeba or Enterococcus.

4. Trusting a common name. They are unregulated and they lie. "Buffalo" means bison in America and Bubalus in Asia; "daddy longlegs" names three different arthropod orders; a starfish is not a fish, a jellyfish is not a fish, and a silverfish is an insect.

5. Comparing ranks across kingdoms. A plant division and an animal phylum are the same rank under two names; a "class" of insects and a "class" of mammals are not equivalent quantities of anything.

6. Believing the hierarchy stops at kingdom. Since 1990 the most inclusive rank in general use is the domain, and it sits above kingdom. A stem offering "kingdom" as the broadest category is testing exactly this.

Go deeper · what a species actually is, and why the rank above it is a hypothesis

Species is the only rank that many biologists think corresponds to something real, and even that is contested. The concepts you may meet:

  • Biological species concept (Mayr): groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations, reproductively isolated from other such groups. It explains why species stay distinct, which no other concept does. It cannot be applied to asexual organisms, to fossils, or to populations that never meet — and it is embarrassed by the amount of hybridisation that turns out to occur (grizzly × polar bear; Homo sapiens × Neanderthal, which is why non-African genomes carry roughly 1–2 % Neanderthal ancestry).
  • Morphological: distinguishable by structure. Practical, used for almost every specimen ever described, and blind to cryptic species.
  • Ecological: defined by the niche occupied.
  • Phylogenetic: the smallest group sharing a common ancestor and diagnosable by at least one derived character. Applies to asexuals and fossils; tends to recognise many more species than the biological concept does.

Bacteria strain the idea past breaking point, because horizontal gene transfer means two "species" can exchange genes freely; the working definition is operational (≥70 % DNA–DNA hybridisation, or ≥95 % average nucleotide identity, or ≥97 % 16S rRNA identity), which is a threshold, not a concept.

Everything above species is explicitly a hypothesis. Placing two species in one genus is a claim that they are each other's close relatives, and that claim is regularly overturned — which is why species get renamed. Pneumocystis carinii became P. jirovecii; the chimpanzee has been Pan, Anthropopithecus and Troglodytes. A changed name is usually not a changed organism but a changed tree.

§ 03

How to read a phylogenetic tree

The ideaA tree is a picture of splits, and everything it tells you is in the pattern of the splits — which lineage last shared an ancestor with which. Nothing at all is told by the order the names happen to be printed down the side, by how far to the right a name sits, or by which name is at the top. Most marks lost on tree questions are lost by reading the printed layout instead of the branching order, and the fix is one habit: put your finger on the junctions, not on the names.

Definition & key facts

Node
A junction, representing the last common ancestor of everything descended from it — not of everything printed above it on the page. Relatedness is read from nodes and from nothing else: two taxa are more closely related the more recent the node that joins them.
relatedness is read from nodes, never from tip order any node may be rotated freely — the result is the same tree
Full definition, symbols and units
Branch (edge)
A lineage through time. Its length means nothing at all in a cladogram, amount of character change in a phylogram, and elapsed time in a chronogram — check the scale bar before you read anything into it.
Root
The node representing the common ancestor of every taxon on the tree. Rooting supplies the direction of time; an unrooted tree states relationships without saying which end is old.
Sister groups
Two lineages that arise from the same node — each other's closest relatives, by definition, and each other's only closest relative.
Polytomy
A node with three or more descendant branches. Almost always a statement of ignorance ("we cannot resolve the order"), occasionally a claim of near-simultaneous radiation.
rotate a node → the SAME tree; the printed order of tips carries no informationthe single most examined property of trees

The three questions to ask of any tree

  1. Where is the root? Without one there is no "earlier" and "later" and no group can be called ancestral to anything. A tree drawn without a root is a statement about relationships only.
  2. What do the branch lengths mean? Nothing, change, or time. If there is no scale bar, assume nothing.
  3. Which node joins the two taxa I am being asked about? Trace both back until the paths meet. The taxon that meets you at a more recent node is the closer relative — regardless of where the names sit on the page.

Rotation is worth doing once by hand, because it is the source of the classic trap. Take the tree (((mouse, human), lizard), shark). Swap the two branches at any junction — write (shark, (lizard, (human, mouse))) — and every relationship stated is identical. The printed order changed completely; the tree did not change at all. A tree is like a mobile hanging from a ceiling: you may spin any arm about its pivot and it is still the same mobile.

Four things a tree does not say

"This lineage is more advanced." A tree has no top and no direction of improvement. Every living tip has had exactly the same amount of time since the root — a bacterium alive today is precisely as evolved as you are, and has been through vastly more generations.

"This is a basal, primitive group." "Basal" describes a branch that attaches near the root of the diagram; it does not mean ancestral, unchanged, or primitive. A lamprey is not a fossil that forgot to evolve — it has been evolving for the same 500 million years you have, and it is loaded with derived features of its own.

"A is closer to B than to C because their labels are adjacent." Adjacency is an artefact of how the artist chose to rotate the nodes. Check the junctions.

"The longest branch has changed the most, so it is the most different species." Only on a phylogram, and even then "amount of change in the sequences analysed" is not "amount of change in the organism". On a chronogram a long branch means nothing more than a long time without a surviving split.

Worked example · how many trees is a program actually choosing between?

Counting the possibilities for n taxa

The number of distinct rooted binary trees for n labelled tips is the double factorial (2n − 3)!! = 1 × 3 × 5 × … × (2n − 3); the number of unrooted trees is (2n − 5)!!. Evaluate for n = 4, 5, 10 and 20.

  1. n = 4 → rooted (2·4 − 3)!! = 5!! = 1 × 3 × 5 = 15; unrooted 3!! = 1 × 3 = 3.
  2. n = 5 → rooted 7!! = 1 × 3 × 5 × 7 = 105; unrooted 5!! = 15.
  3. n = 10 → rooted 17!! = 34 459 425; unrooted 15!! = 2 027 025.
  4. n = 20 → rooted 37!! ≈ 8.2 × 10²¹.

Notice the identity falling out of the two formulae: rooted(n) = unrooted(n + 1). Rooting a tree is combinatorially the same operation as adding one extra taxon — which is exactly what an outgroup does.

n = 10 → 34 459 425 rooted trees · n = 20 → ≈ 8.2 × 10²¹

This is why "the computer tried all the trees" stops being true above about a dozen taxa. Real analyses use heuristic search — build a starting tree, then swap branches and keep improvements — which is a hill-climb and can stall on a local optimum. It is also why a published tree comes with bootstrap or posterior-probability values on its nodes: those numbers say how repeatable each split was, and a node below about 70 % bootstrap support should be read as unresolved.

What tree questions actually test

Overwhelmingly one skill: given a drawn tree, name the sister group of X, or say which of three taxa is most closely related to X. Do it by tracing to the shared node. The second most common item asks you to recognise that two differently drawn trees are the same tree — again, compare the sets of taxa descended from each node, not the pictures. Third, and worth a moment's care, is the "which of these statements about the tree is NOT supported" format, where the false option is almost always either a progress claim ("more advanced", "higher") or an ancestor claim ("X descended from Y", both being living tips).

Exam technique · a drawn tree in twenty seconds

  1. Cover the tip labels with your finger before you read the options — every trap on this item is built out of adjacency, so remove the temptation physically. Find the two taxa the stem names, trace each one back along its branch, and mark the junction where the two paths first meet.
  2. Compare junctions, not distances — of two candidate relatives, the closer one is whichever meets you at the node nearer the tips (the more recent split). Horizontal distance across the page and vertical distance down the list are both irrelevant unless there is a scale bar, and even then a scale bar tells you about change or time, never about kinship.
  3. "Are these two drawings the same tree?" — never compare the pictures — list, for each internal node, the set of tips descended from it. Two trees are identical if and only if those sets match. Ten seconds of set-writing beats a minute of staring, and rotation makes staring useless.
  4. Two free kills on any "which statement is NOT supported" item — strike, without looking at the tree at all, (a) any option containing more advanced, higher, more evolved, primitive or less evolved, and (b) any option of the form "X evolved from Y" where X and Y are both living tips. One of these two shapes is almost always the intended answer; if both survive, the progress claim is the more common one.
  5. Where the time goes — a tree item should cost twenty seconds, not sixty-six. Bank the surplus for § 06's arithmetic, which is the only part of this note that genuinely needs the full minute.
§ 04

Monophyletic, paraphyletic, polyphyletic

The ideaThere is one test for sorting any named group into these three boxes, and it takes about three seconds. Find the most recent common ancestor of everything in the group. Ask two questions in order: is that ancestor inside the group, and is everything descended from it also inside the group? Two yeses and you have a real branch. Yes then no, and you have a branch with a piece cut out. A no at the first question and the group was never a branch at all — its members were collected because they resemble each other, not because they are related.

Definition & key facts

Monophyletic group (clade)
An ancestor and all of its descendants — a complete branch. The only kind of group a phylogenetic classification formally recognises.
MRCA in the group? → all its descendants in the group? → mono / para / poly no → polyphyletic · yes then no → paraphyletic · yes then yes → monophyletic
Full definition, symbols and units
Paraphyletic group
Contains the MRCA but excludes at least one descendant lineage. Diagnosed by shared ancestral characters (symplesiomorphies) — which is precisely why it fails: an inherited old feature cannot tell you who split off from whom.
Polyphyletic group
Does not contain the MRCA of its members. Assembled from two or more separate branches on the strength of a character that arose independently in each — a homoplasy.
Synapomorphy
A shared derived character: a novelty that arose in one ancestor and was inherited by all its descendants. Clades are recognised by these and only these (§ 05).

Worked example · diagnose five groups on the tree above

Apply the two questions in order, every time

  1. Mammalia (monotremes + marsupials + eutherians). MRCA = the first mammal. Is it in the group? Yes. Is every descendant of it in the group? Yes — nothing has left Mammalia. → monophyletic.
  2. "Reptiles" (turtles, lizards, snakes, crocodilians; birds excluded). MRCA = the sauropsid ancestor. In the group? Yes. All its descendants in? No — birds descend from it and have been excluded. → paraphyletic.
  3. Archosauria (crocodilians + birds, plus the extinct dinosaurs and pterosaurs). MRCA = the archosaur ancestor. In? Yes. All descendants in? Yes. → monophyletic, even though a crocodile looks far more like a lizard than like a sparrow. This is the whole lesson in one line: appearance is not evidence.
  4. "Warm-blooded amniotes" (birds + mammals). MRCA = the root of this tree, a Carboniferous amniote that was not endothermic. In the group? No — the group is defined by a trait it lacked. → polyphyletic. Endothermy arose twice, independently, and so did the four-chambered heart.
  5. "Amniotes other than humans". MRCA = the root. In? Yes. All descendants in? No, one species removed. → paraphyletic. Removing any subclade from a clade always leaves a paraphyletic remainder — which is the general reason "fish", "invertebrates" and "protists" all fail.

Two things people get wrong about paraphyly

"Paraphyletic means the group is wrong." It means the group is not a branch. "Reptile", "fish" and "protist" remain perfectly good descriptions of a way of being built and are used constantly in biology and medicine. What they cannot do is appear in a phylogenetic classification, and what they must never do is license an inference — "sharks and tuna are both fish, so they are each other's closest relatives" is the inference that fails.

Confusing paraphyletic with polyphyletic. The discriminator is one question: is the common ancestor in the group? A paraphyletic group has the ancestor and is missing descendants; a polyphyletic group has the descendants and is missing the ancestor. If you can only remember one example of each, use "reptiles without birds" (para) and "birds plus mammals because both are warm-blooded" (poly).

Paraphyletic or polyphyletic — which one is missing what

PARA = PARTial family — parent in, some children out · POLY = PLURAL origins — children in, parent out

PARA is the PARTial family: the ancestor is in, so what the group is short of is its children — descendants left outside. Reptiles are short of birds; "fish" is short of tetrapods; Protista is short of plants, animals and fungi. POLY is PLURAL origins: the descendants are all there but the group is short of the parent — the common ancestor itself, which sat outside because it lacked the very trait the group is named for. Birds + mammals are short of the Carboniferous amniote that was not endothermic. One word decides it either way: parent. In, and you are looking at a paraphyletic grade; out, and it is a polyphyletic assemblage. My own phrasing, not a circulating hook; the parent-in / parent-out contrast is exactly the discriminator the callout above names, compressed to two words you can read straight off the group's own name. It says nothing about monophyletic, and it does not need to: monophyly is the case where nothing is missing at all.

How this is actually asked

Almost always as a single-best-answer with four or five named groups, asking which one is paraphyletic (or which is not monophyletic). The distractors are chosen so that the obviously wrong ones are real clades — Mammalia, Angiospermae, Insecta, Tetrapoda — and the discrimination is between one paraphyletic grade and one polyphyletic assemblage. Run the two questions; do not reason from how similar the organisms look, because the whole point of the item is that similarity points the wrong way.

Exam technique · "which of these is not a clade", attacked by the shape of the name

  1. Sort the five options by typography before you think about biology — a formally Latinised, capitalised, unquoted name (Mammalia, Insecta, Tetrapoda, Amniota, Archosauria, Angiospermae, Ecdysozoa, Chordata) is a published clade and is there to be eliminated. An English vernacular name, or a Latin name in scare quotes — "fish", "reptiles", "worms", "algae", "invertebrates", "prokaryotes", "warm-blooded animals", "trees" — is where the answer lives. On a typical item this kills three options in five seconds.
  2. Know the four Latin exceptions, because the examiner knows them too: Protista, Agnatha, Monera and Reptilia in its traditional bird-excluding sense are all Latin, all respectable-looking, and all paraphyletic. If one of those appears, step 1 does not apply to it and you go straight to step 3.
  3. Run the two questions on the survivors — is the MRCA in the group? are all of its descendants in the group? Yes/yes = monophyletic, yes/no = paraphyletic, no = polyphyletic. Say it out loud in one pass and do not reopen it.
  4. Split para from poly by how the group was defined, not by what is in it — a group defined by a subtraction ("all X except Y", "eukaryotes that are not plants, animals or fungi", "amniotes other than humans") is paraphyletic. A group defined by a shared job, habit or look (warm-blooded, photosynthetic, wormlike, tree-shaped, flying) is polyphyletic, because that trait was reinvented and the common ancestor did not have it. There is no mnemonic for the list of famous non-clades and you should not build one — this single rule regenerates every row of the table in § 01, including rows you have never seen.
  5. Answer even when steps 3 and 4 leave you with two — with five options and −0.25, a blind guess is exactly break-even, so killing two options makes answering worth +0.17 a question and killing three makes it +0.375. On this topic you will essentially always kill at least two. A blank here is a discarded mark.
§ 05

Homology, analogy and the characters that count

The ideaTwo organisms can end up looking the same for two entirely different reasons. Either they inherited the resemblance from a shared ancestor, or each of them arrived at it separately because the same job had to be done in the same world. Only the first kind of resemblance is evidence about relationship; the second kind is evidence about the job. Nearly every question in this block is a disguised version of "which kind is this?", and the disguise is usually a sentence that sounds exactly like a textbook.

Definition & key facts

Homology
Similarity that exists because both organisms inherited it from a common ancestor. Homologous structures need not look alike or do the same job — they need to correspond in position, in their parts, and in how they develop.
homology = same ancestry · analogy = same job divergent evolution produces the first; convergent evolution produces the second
Full definition, symbols and units
Analogy
Similarity of function and often of appearance, arrived at independently in lineages that did not inherit it. The product of convergent evolution under similar selection.
Homoplasy
The umbrella term for any similarity that is not inherited from a common ancestor. Three flavours: convergence (different starting points, same end point), parallelism (similar starting points, same change made twice), and reversal (a derived state changing back to the ancestral one). Every one of them is noise to a phylogeneticist.
Apomorphy / plesiomorphy
Derived versus ancestral state of a character. Shared derived = synapomorphy (builds clades); shared ancestral = symplesiomorphy (builds nothing); derived and unique to one terminal = autapomorphy (diagnoses that terminal, groups nothing).
only SYNAPOMORPHIES define cladesshared derived characters; everything else is either uninformative or misleading

polarity is set by outgroup comparison: the state seen in the outgroup is ancestralthis is what an outgroup is for, quite apart from rooting

The classic pair, and the nuance that decides the mark

The tetrapod forelimb is the standard demonstration of homology. A human arm, a whale flipper, a bat wing, a cat foreleg and a mole's digging spade all contain one proximal bone (humerus), two bones below it (radius and ulna), a cluster of wrist bones and five sets of digits, in that order and in that arrangement — and they row, fly, dig, walk and type. Function has diverged wildly; the parts correspond one for one. That is divergent evolution, and it is what homology looks like.

Now the nuance that actually decides exam questions. Compare a bat wing and a bird wing. As forelimbs they are homologous — the same bones, inherited from the same tetrapod ancestor. As wings they are analogous — a bird's aerofoil is feathers carried on a fused, reduced hand, a bat's is a sheet of skin stretched between four enormously elongated fingers, and the last common ancestor of the two had no flight surface of any kind. So the honest answer is not "homologous" and not "analogous" but both, at different levels. Homology is a claim about a specified character, not about two organisms. Any option that treats it as a property of the animals rather than of the structure is wrong, however plausible it reads.

Three tests for telling them apart

  1. Correspondence of parts and position, not of function. Ask what the structure is made of and where it attaches. An insect wing is an outgrowth of the body wall with no bones in it at all; a bat wing is a hand. That settles it before any evolutionary argument is needed.
  2. Embryonic origin. Structures that develop from the same tissue in the same place are candidates for homology. The mammalian malleus and incus develop from the first pharyngeal arch, exactly where the reptilian articular and quadrate jaw bones develop — which is why they are the same bones, repurposed, and why the fossil series showing the transition is one of the best in the record.
  3. Congruence with everything else. A genuine homology is corroborated by the rest of the data. If one character says annelids and arthropods are sisters and five others say otherwise, the one is the homoplasy. This is precisely the reasoning parsimony formalises in § 06.

Four textbook-sounding sentences to reject

"Homologous structures are similar structures." No — homologous structures are structures of shared origin, and they are frequently dissimilar. Your coccyx and a monkey's tail; the three bones in your middle ear and a lizard's jaw joint; a stamen and a leaf. Conversely the two most similar-looking eyes in the animal kingdom — yours and an octopus's — are not homologous as camera eyes, and the octopus's is arguably better engineered, with the photoreceptors facing the light and no blind spot.

"Analogous structures show the organisms are unrelated." Everything is related; the question is how closely. Bats and birds are both amniotes and share a great deal by descent, including the forelimb the wings are built from. "Analogous" restricts a claim to one character.

"Molecular data can't be fooled by convergence." They can. Antifreeze proteins in Antarctic notothenioids and in Arctic cod are functionally near-identical and evolved from completely different genes; the same amino-acid substitutions in prestin appear independently in echolocating bats and dolphins. Molecules are better because there are so many independent characters, not because each one is immune.

"Vestigial structures are useless leftovers." Vestigial means reduced from an ancestral function, not functionless. The human appendix carries gut-associated lymphoid tissue and appears to act as a microbial reservoir; flightless-bird wings are used in display and balance. What makes them evidence is not uselessness but the fact that they are unmistakably the same structure as a working one in a relative.

Exam technique · the homology-or-analogy item, in four moves

  1. Write down the character before you read the options — the stem is never really about two animals, it is about a wing, an eye, a fin, a spine, a leaf. Naming it stops you from answering the question "are bats and birds related?", which is not the question and whose answer is always yes.
  2. Two questions, in this order. Same parts, in the same relative positions, from the same embryonic tissue? → homologous, whatever they now do. Different parts doing the same job? → analogous. Structure decides before any evolutionary argument is needed: an insect wing has no bones in it, so it cannot be a modified forelimb, and you are done.
  3. Four free kills, available on nearly every item of this type. Strike any option that (a) calls two organisms homologous rather than two structures, (b) infers from analogy that the organisms are unrelated, (c) requires homologous structures to look alike or share a function, (d) claims molecular or sequence data cannot be fooled by convergence. Each of these is a sentence lifted from real textbook prose and each is false as written — which is exactly why it reads so well.
  4. When the character is a composite built on an inherited base — a wing, a flipper, a camera eye, a digging spade — expect the correct option to split the levels ("homologous as forelimbs, analogous as wings"). The flat single-word options are the distractor pair, and they are usually offered as a matched set so that neither looks safer than the other. Level-splitting is the tell.
  5. If instead the stem asks "does this character group these taxa?", use the one-level-down rule — a character is informative only one level below where it arose. If the character is also present in the outgroup or in the wider group named in the stem, it is symplesiomorphy and the answer is no. Vertebral column across lamprey, shark and human: no. Hair across mammals: yes.
Go deeper · deep homology, and why "convergent" and "homologous" are not always exclusive

The vertebrate eye and the cephalopod eye are the standard example of convergence: both are camera eyes with a lens, an iris and a retina, arrived at from ancestors that had nothing of the kind, and built differently enough that the vertebrate retina is inverted (photoreceptors facing away from the light, with an optic-nerve blind spot) while the cephalopod one is not. As camera eyes they are analogous, and that is the answer to the exam question.

But the genetic machinery that builds them is shared. Pax6 (eyeless in Drosophila) sits at the top of the eye-specification network in vertebrates, cephalopods and insects alike; the mouse gene, expressed in a fly's leg, induces a compound eye there. Opsins are one ancient family. So the eyes are convergent as organs while the developmental toolkit that makes them is homologous — a relationship called deep homology. The same story holds for animal appendages (Distal-less/Dlx in insect legs, vertebrate limbs, echinoderm tube feet) and for hearts (tinman/Nkx2-5).

The moral for an exam: state which level you are talking about. The structure can be analogous while the genes patterning it are homologous, and an option that asserts one without qualifying the level is incomplete rather than simply wrong. Convergence is also far more common than it feels — C₄ photosynthesis has evolved independently more than sixty times in flowering plants — which is exactly why a single shared character is never enough to build a group on.

The symplesiomorphy trap

This is the item that separates candidates. "A lamprey, a shark and a human all have a vertebral column. Does this show they form a natural group?" — the column is ancestral for all three, so it groups them only against animals outside Vertebrata; within the group it says nothing about who is closest to whom. The general rule: a character is only informative one level below where it arose. Having four limbs does not tell you a frog and a human are close relatives (both are tetrapods — ancestral at that level); having hair does, because hair arose on the branch leading to mammals and nowhere else. When a stem offers you a character, ask first where on the tree it appeared.

The four "-morphy" words, from four roots

syn/sym = shared · aut = alone · apo = new · plesio = old — and only shared + new builds a clade

Do not learn four long words; learn four short roots and screw them together on the spot. synapomorphy = shared + new = a novelty inherited by everything descended from the ancestor that acquired it — this and only this builds clades. symplesiomorphy = shared + old = an ancestral state, shared because it is older than the question you are asking, and therefore evidence for nothing. autapomorphy = alone + new = derived but unique to one terminal, so it diagnoses that terminal and groups nobody. Then the fourth word is not built from these at all: homoplasy (homo = same, plassein = to form) is similarity that was formed the same rather than inherited — convergence, parallelism and reversal. The roots are the standard Greek and appear in every textbook glossary; the four-root packaging is my own. Note what falls out for free once you hold it: "ancestral" and "derived" are relative to the level of comparison, so the same character can be apomorphic at one node and plesiomorphic one node down — the amniotic egg is derived for Amniota and ancestral within it. That is the symplesiomorphy trap above, and no separate hook is needed for it.

§ 06

Inferring the tree: parsimony and molecules

The ideaNobody watched the past, so the tree has to be inferred. The method is to lay the characters out in a table, try the possible trees, and keep whichever tree explains the table with the fewest accidents — the fewest times a feature has to be invented twice or lost again. Molecules made this vastly more powerful, because a gene supplies thousands of independent characters where a skeleton supplies a few dozen. But molecules are the same kind of evidence used in the same way, and they can be fooled in the same ways.

Definition & key facts

Maximum parsimony
Prefer the tree that requires the fewest character-state changes ("steps") to account for the observed data. It is a criterion for choosing, not a claim that evolution proceeds economically.
best tree = fewest steps summed over all characters every extra step is an extra assumed homoplasy — an independent gain, loss or reversal

Why parsimony, and what it is actually claiming

Parsimony does not assume evolution is efficient. It assumes that, other things being equal, an explanation requiring fewer unobserved coincidences is a better bet than one requiring more — the same reasoning a clinician uses in preferring one diagnosis that accounts for four findings over four unrelated diagnoses. Each extra "step" on a tree is a claim that a feature was independently invented, or lost, an extra time. If tree A needs you to believe segmentation arose twice and tree B needs you to believe a trochophore larva arose twice, you compare the totals across all the characters and take the smaller.

Two practical consequences. First, characters that fit all candidate trees equally well cannot discriminate between them — they are parsimony-uninformative, and this includes any character with the same state in every taxon, and any derived state unique to one terminal (an autapomorphy). Second, the winner may win by one step, which is no evidence at all; this is what bootstrap values are for.

Molecules: why they redrew the tree

Morphology runs out. You cannot compare the femur of a fungus with the femur of a bacterium, and above the level of the phylum there are almost no characters two organisms share that can be scored the same way. A gene, on the other hand, can be aligned across all of life, gives one character per site, and is far harder to talk yourself into. The trick is choosing a gene whose rate suits the depth of the question:

  • Deep splits (domains, kingdoms) — small-subunit ribosomal RNA (16S/18S). Universal, functionally constrained, and slow enough that a signal survives billions of years.
  • Phyla to families — nuclear protein-coding genes, or whole-genome data.
  • Species and populations — fast markers: mitochondrial COI (the animal DNA barcode), cytochrome b, the mitochondrial control region, microsatellites.

Mitochondrial DNA is popular for recent questions because it is maternally inherited, does not recombine, accumulates substitutions roughly ten times faster than nuclear DNA, and is present in hundreds of copies per cell — which is why it is what you get from a degraded forensic or ancient sample. It is also why a mitochondrial tree is a tree of mothers, and can differ from the tree of the organisms.

Worked example · a molecular clock, and the correction most people forget

From per cent difference to a date

A nuclear gene accumulates substitutions at r = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ per site per year in each lineage. Two species differ at 4.8 % of aligned sites. When did they last share a common ancestor?

  1. Since the split, both lineages have been changing independently. The observed difference is therefore the sum of two lineages' worth of change: D = 2rT, so T = D / (2r).
  2. T = 0.048 / (2 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹) = 0.048 / 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.2 × 10⁷ years.
  3. That is 12 million years. Dividing by r instead of 2r gives 24 Myr — the single commonest error on this calculation, and always available as a distractor.
  4. Now correct for multiple hits. A site that changed A→G→C counts once in the alignment but twice in history, and a site that changed A→G→A counts as no difference at all. Jukes–Cantor: d = −(3/4) ln(1 − (4/3)p). With p = 0.048: d = −0.75 × ln(1 − 0.064) = −0.75 × ln(0.936) = 0.0496, so 4.96 % rather than 4.8 % — a 3.3 % relative correction, giving 12.4 Myr.
  5. The correction only matters when divergence is large. At p = 0.45 it gives d = 0.687, a 53 % increase; and as p approaches 0.75 — the value expected for two random sequences — d goes to infinity. That ceiling is saturation, and it is why fast-evolving genes are useless for deep questions.
T ≈ 12 Myr uncorrected · ≈ 12.4 Myr with the Jukes–Cantor correction

Four confident-sounding statements about molecular phylogenetics that are wrong

"The molecular clock ticks at a constant rate." It is a stochastic average, not a metronome — substitutions are a Poisson process, so even a perfect clock gives scattered dates. Worse, the rate genuinely varies: with generation time (short-generation lineages accumulate substitutions faster per year), with metabolic rate, with DNA-repair efficiency, and between genes by orders of magnitude. Modern analyses use relaxed clocks that let the rate vary across branches, and every date needs a fossil calibration.

"The most parsimonious tree is the true tree." It is the tree requiring fewest homoplasies given these characters. Where evolutionary rates differ strongly between lineages, parsimony is not merely imprecise but positively misleading — it converges on the wrong answer as you add data (see the Go deeper line below).

"A gene tree is the species tree." Two species can share an ancestral polymorphism that sorts differently in different genes (incomplete lineage sorting); genes are duplicated and lost; and in bacteria, genes are transferred sideways wholesale. Roughly 15 % of the human genome gives a gene tree grouping us with gorillas rather than with chimpanzees (about 30 % of it conflicts with the species tree one way or the other), and this is expected, not an error. Robust conclusions come from many loci, not one.

"More data always give a better tree." More data shrink sampling error — the thing bootstrap measures — but do nothing about a wrong substitution model. An analysis with a misspecified model and a genome's worth of sites returns the wrong tree with 100 % bootstrap support. High support means repeatable, not correct.

Go deeper · long-branch attraction, and why more data can make things worse

Consider four taxa where two unrelated lineages, A and C, have been evolving very fast, while their respective relatives B and D have been slow. On the long branches leading to A and C, so many substitutions occur that some sites change to the same base in both purely by chance. With only four nucleotides to choose from, chance matches accumulate steadily. Parsimony reads each of these coincidences as a shared derived character and pulls A and C together into a spurious clade. The name is long-branch attraction and the region of parameter space where it happens is called the Felsenstein zone.

The disturbing part is what happens as you add sequence. The true signal is finite — it was written into a limited number of sites at the moment of divergence — while the chance matches keep accruing with sequence length. So the wrong answer gets more support the more data you collect. A method that behaves this way is called statistically inconsistent, and parsimony is the textbook example.

The remedies are all about admitting that multiple hits happen: model-based methods (maximum likelihood, Bayesian inference) that explicitly estimate how much unseen change a long branch has accumulated; breaking long branches by adding taxa that subdivide them; and removing the fastest-evolving sites. Historically this mattered a great deal — the early placement of microsporidia at the base of the eukaryote tree was a long-branch artefact, and they are in fact highly derived fungi. It is also the live issue in the ctenophore-versus-sponge argument about the base of the animal tree (§ 09).

What is examined here

Three things, all of them short. (1) Counting steps on two given trees and naming the more parsimonious — arithmetic, so do it rather than eyeballing it. (2) The clock calculation, where the factor of two is the entire question. (3) A conceptual item on what parsimony assumes, where the wrong option is nearly always "evolution takes the shortest path" or "the simplest organism is the ancestor". Parsimony is a rule for choosing between hypotheses, and it says nothing whatever about how evolution behaves.

Exam technique · the clock calculation, and the two options you can kill before doing it

  1. Recognise the type in five seconds. A rate quoted per site per year plus a percentage difference between two sequences = molecular clock, and the whole question is T = D / (2r). Two candidate trees plus a character table = step counting. Neither item is conceptual; every mark in both is in the arithmetic, so stop reading the physiology of it and start writing.
  2. Three moves, always in the same order. Percent → fraction (4.8 % becomes 0.048, and this is where most of the wreckage happens); double the rate (2 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁹); divide. 0.048 / (4.0 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.2 × 10⁷ = 12 Myr. Double the rate, never halve the answer at the end — halving at the end is what people forget to do.
  3. Kill two options without calculating anything. A clock item's option list nearly always contains some number and exactly twice that number: the larger is the divided-by-r-instead-of-2r distractor, so take the smaller. And any option 100 or 1 000 times away from its neighbours is the percent-left-as-4.8 slip (here, 1.2 × 10⁹ years). Two of five gone on inspection.
  4. Two-second sanity check that also gives you the answer. At r = 2 × 10⁻⁹ per lineage, one million years of separation buys 2rT = 0.4 % divergence. So % difference ÷ 0.4 = Myr: 4.8 ÷ 0.4 = 12. Build the same one-line conversion for whatever rate the stem gives you and use it to confirm the long calculation, never to replace it.
  5. The Jukes–Cantor correction only ever moves the date one way — older. Corrected d is always greater than observed p (here 4.96 % against 4.8 %, so 12.4 Myr against 12), because unseen multiple hits can only have been missed, never invented. Any option offering a "corrected" value below the uncorrected one is wrong on sign alone, no arithmetic required.
  6. Step counting: sum every character, including the ones that tie. Invariant characters cannot change which tree wins, but they do change the totals, and the totals are what the options quote. Never take the per-character differences and add them as absolute values — a character that favours tree I must be subtracted from the margin, not added to it, and that inflated margin is always on offer as a distractor.
  7. No mnemonic exists for the factor of two and you must not invent one. It falls out of one sentence: after the split, both lineages have been changing, so the difference you observe is two lineages' worth of time. Anybody who holds that sentence reconstructs the formula; anybody who holds "divide by 2r" as a rule will one day meet a stem that quotes the rate for the pair and will halve it twice.
§ 07

Three domains, and why the tree was redrawn

The ideaUntil the 1970s the deepest division in life was taken to be between cells with a nucleus and cells without. Then somebody sequenced a molecule that every organism on earth possesses and compared it across the board, and that division turned out to be shallow — while one of the two so-called prokaryote groups turned out to be more closely related to us than to the other. Almost everything that had made the older five-kingdom picture tidy broke at the same moment, and for the same reason: it had been sorting cells by what they looked like and what they lacked.

What broke in the five-kingdom picture

Whittaker's five kingdoms was an intelligent scheme built on the best evidence available in 1969: cell type (prokaryotic vs eukaryotic), organisation (unicellular vs multicellular) and nutrition (photosynthesis, absorption, ingestion). Every one of those criteria is a description of a grade, and each of them broke for the reason § 01 predicts.

Four sentences about domains that examiners lift verbatim

"Archaea live only in extreme environments." The famous ones do — hyperthermophiles at Yellowstone, halophiles in the Dead Sea, methanogens in swamps. But ammonia-oxidising archaea (Nitrososphaeria) are among the most abundant organisms in soil and in the open ocean, and Methanobrevibacter smithii is a normal resident of the human colon. "Extremophile" is an ecological description, and plenty of bacteria are extremophiles too.

"Prokaryotes form a kingdom (or a domain)." They form neither. Bacteria and Archaea are two separate domains, and the term survives only as a description of cell architecture.

"Kingdom is the highest taxonomic rank." Not since 1990.

"Plants and fungi are related — both are sessile, both have cell walls, both absorb nutrients from soil." Three convergences in one sentence. The walls are chemically different (cellulose vs chitin), the nutrition is different (autotrophy vs absorptive heterotrophy), and fungi sit with animals in Opisthokonta. This is a textbook-shaped sentence that is false at every clause.

Go deeper · the two-domain tree, Asgard archaea, and the eukaryote as a chimera

The three-domain tree draws Archaea and Eukarya as sister groups descending from a common ancestor. A large and growing body of phylogenomic work says something slightly different and considerably more interesting: that eukaryotes arose from within the Archaea, specifically as a branch of the Asgard archaea (Lokiarchaeota and relatives, first identified from marine sediment metagenomes in 2015 and since cultured). Asgard genomes carry a startling set of "eukaryote signature proteins" — actin and profilin homologues, ESCRT complexes, small GTPases of the Ras superfamily, ubiquitin machinery — the toolkit for phagocytosis and internal membranes.

If that is right, then Archaea as usually drawn is paraphyletic, in exactly the way Reptilia is: the group excludes one of its own descendant lineages. The two-domain ("eocyte") tree has Bacteria and Archaea, with Eukarya nested inside the latter.

Either way, a eukaryotic cell is a chimera. Its informational machinery is archaeal; its mitochondrion is a captured alphaproteobacterium, and much of its lipid and energy metabolism came with it; a plastid, where present, is a captured cyanobacterium, sometimes captured second-hand from another eukaryote (secondary endosymbiosis, which is how diatoms and euglenids got theirs — and why their plastids have three or four membranes). At the deepest level, then, the history of life is not a tree but a network, with two major fusions and a great deal of horizontal gene transfer among the prokaryotes.

Is this examinable? The three-domain answer is the one to give unless the stem explicitly raises the alternative. But KMU carries a minority of beyond-textbook items every year, and "eukaryotes arose from within the archaea" is precisely the shape of statement that turns up as a correct option people reject because it contradicts what they were taught.

The reliable domain question

"Which feature is shared by Archaea and Eukarya but not Bacteria?" — and the answer is always drawn from the informational machinery: histones, TATA-binding protein, a multi-subunit RNA polymerase, unmodified methionine as the initiator amino acid, sensitivity to diphtheria toxin. The distractors are drawn from the operational and structural side, where archaea resemble bacteria: small size, no nucleus, circular chromosome, operons, 70S ribosomes, binary fission. The one-line summary worth memorising: an archaeon looks like a bacterium and reads its genome like you do. Note also the two features that are archaea-only and therefore always false as "shared" answers: methanogenesis and ether-linked membrane lipids.

The rest of the three-domain table is derivable from the two halves of the hook and does not need learning row by row.

§ 08

Animal body plans: the five characters

The ideaThere are about thirty-five animal phyla and nobody expects you to know them all. What you are expected to do is take an animal described in a sentence and place it, using a handful of yes-or-no questions about how the body is built: is it symmetrical and in what way, how many layers was it built from, is there a cavity inside and how is it lined, which hole in the embryo became the mouth, and is the body one unit or the same unit repeated. Five questions, and they get you to the right phylum far more often than any list of animals will.

Definition & key facts

Body plan
The set of developmental and structural features that organise an animal — symmetry, germ layers, body cavity, embryonic origin of the mouth, and the presence or absence of repeated units. Body plans are conserved within phyla, which is precisely what makes them useful for placing an unknown.
symmetry · germ layers · body cavity · blastopore fate · segmentation answer these five and you have narrowed thirty-five phyla to two or three
Full definition, symbols and units
Germ layers
Ectoderm → epidermis and nervous system. Endoderm → gut lining and its outgrowths (liver, pancreas, lung epithelium, thyroid). Mesoderm → muscle, skeleton, blood, heart, kidney, gonad, dermis. Diploblastic animals have the first two; triploblastic animals add the third, and mesoderm is the innovation that makes bulk, muscle and internal organs possible.
Coelom
A body cavity completely lined by mesoderm-derived peritoneum, with the gut suspended in it by mesenteries. Pseudocoelom: a cavity persisting from the blastocoel, only partly lined by mesoderm. Acoelomate: solid mesodermal packing between gut and body wall.
Metamerism (true segmentation)
Serial repetition of a whole body module — muscle block, nerve ganglion, coelomic compartment, excretory organ, blood vessel — along the long axis. Not to be confused with the proglottids of a tapeworm, which are repeated reproductive units budded from a neck.

Protostome and deuterostome: what the names promise and what they deliver

The gastrula has one opening, the blastopore. In a protostome it becomes the mouth ("first mouth") and the anus breaks through later; in a deuterostome it becomes the anus and the mouth is a second, new opening. Three further characters were traditionally bundled with each: protostomes have spiral cleavage with an early-fixed (determinate, mosaic) fate, and split a solid block of mesoderm to make the coelom (schizocoely); deuterostomes have radial cleavage with fates still regulative (indeterminate) at the four-cell stage, and pinch coelomic pouches off the archenteron (enterocoely).

Learn the bundle — it is examined — but learn it as a tendency. Vertebrates are deuterostomes whose coelom forms largely by schizocoely; several protostome groups cleave radially; and blastopore fate itself is variable within phyla. The reason the two clades survived the molecular revolution is not that the naming characters held up, but that the sequence data independently recovered the same two groups. This is a good general lesson about how classification works: a group can be right for reasons other than the one it was named for.

The protostome bundle — three letters you already know

protostome = SDS: Spiral · Determinate · Schizocoely

The blastopore half needs no hook at all: proto-stome is Greek for "first mouth" and deutero-stome for "second mouth", so the names hand you the fate of the blastopore. What is genuinely arbitrary is the three characters traditionally bundled with each, and they happen to spell the one three-letter string every biochemistry candidate can already say in their sleep: a protostome cleaves Spirally, its cell fates are Determinate (mosaic, fixed early), and its coelom forms by Schizocoely — mesoderm splitting. Deuterostomes are then simply the other state of each: radial, indeterminate, enterocoelous. My own hook, not a standard one, though the SDS-versus-RIE pairing is the standard bundle. Two things it deliberately does not promise, both examined: these are tendencies — vertebrates are deuterostomes whose coelom forms largely by schizocoely, several protostome groups cleave radially, and blastopore fate varies within phyla. And membership of Protostomia is settled by molecules, not by the bundle: Lophotrochozoa + Ecdysozoa were recovered from sequence, which is why the two clades survived even where the naming characters did not.

Five body-plan sentences lifted straight from older textbooks — all wrong

"Coelomates are more advanced than acoelomates; the acoelomate–pseudocoelomate–coelomate series shows increasing complexity." This was the organising idea of animal classification for most of the twentieth century and it is gone. Pseudocoely arose several times independently, and the coelom has been secondarily reduced in arthropods and molluscs, whose functional body cavity is a blood-filled haemocoel. It is not a ladder, and an animal is not primitive for lacking a coelom.

"Nematodes and flatworms are related — both are worms without a true coelom." Two absences and a shape. Nematodes are ecdysozoans, closer to a lobster than to a tapeworm; flatworms are lophotrochozoans.

"Segmentation is a synapomorphy uniting annelids and arthropods (Articulata)." The single most likely wrong option on this topic. Segmentation is homoplastic — see the worked example in § 06.

"All deuterostomes form the coelom by enterocoely." Most vertebrates do not.

"Echinoderms are radially symmetrical, so they belong with the cnidarians." Echinoderm radial symmetry is pentaradial, secondarily derived, and appears only at metamorphosis: the larva is bilateral. Echinoderms are deuterostomes and are our own closest relatives outside the chordates.

How a body-plan question is built

The stem describes an unnamed animal in one or two sentences — "a triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical marine animal with a pseudocoelom, a complete gut, a cuticle that is periodically shed, and only longitudinal muscles" — and asks for the phylum, or for which other animal is its closest relative. Work the five characters in order and stop as soon as the answer is unique; here "moulted cuticle" plus "pseudocoelom" plus "longitudinal muscle only" is Nematoda and nothing else. The other common format gives you a character and asks which phyla share it, which is really a homology-versus-homoplasy question in disguise.

The comparisons that get asked

Sponge versus cnidarian. Sponges have no true tissues at all — no nerves, no muscle, and cells that can be dissociated and will reassemble. Cnidarians have genuine tissues, a nerve net and extracellular digestion. The line between them is the origin of tissues.

Flatworm versus nematode. Both are unsegmented worms and both were once filed together. But the flatworm is acoelomate with an incomplete gut and both circular and longitudinal muscle; the nematode is pseudocoelomate with a complete gut, a moulted cuticle and only longitudinal muscle. They are in different halves of Protostomia.

Annelid versus arthropod. Both segmented, and that is the trap. The annelid has a true septate coelom, closed circulation, soft body and no jointed appendages; the arthropod has a haemocoel, open circulation, a moulted exoskeleton and jointed limbs. Segmentation is convergent; ecdysis is what actually places the arthropod.

Echinoderm versus cnidarian. Both radial as adults, and nothing else in common. The echinoderm is triploblastic, coelomate, deuterostome, and bilateral as a larva.

Go deeper · the helminths that turn up in clinical questions

Medical parasitology draws on exactly two of the phyla above, and the phylum tells you a surprising amount about the disease.

Platyhelminthes. Trematodes (flukes) are leaf-shaped with two suckers and always use a snail as first intermediate host: Schistosoma (the only fluke with separate sexes; cercariae penetrate skin from fresh water; eggs lodge in bladder or bowel wall and drive granuloma, fibrosis and, for S. haematobium, squamous bladder carcinoma), Clonorchis sinensis (raw freshwater fish, endemic in Taiwan and southern China, cholangiocarcinoma), Paragonimus westermani (raw crab; a tuberculosis mimic). Cestodes (tapeworms) have no gut whatever and absorb across the tegument, and their "segments" (proglottids) are budded reproductive units, not true metameres: Taenia solium (pork; the human as accidental intermediate host gives neurocysticercosis), Taenia saginata (beef), Diphyllobothrium latum (fish; vitamin B12 deficiency), Echinococcus granulosus (hydatid cysts, dogs and sheep).

Nematoda. Cylindrical, complete gut, moulted cuticle. Ascaris lumbricoides (largest intestinal nematode; a lung migration phase causing Löffler syndrome). Hookworms (Ancylostoma, Necator) penetrate the foot and cause iron-deficiency anaemia by blood feeding. Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm; perianal itch; the sticky-tape test). Trichinella spiralis (undercooked pork; larvae encyst in striated muscle; periorbital oedema and eosinophilia). Wuchereria bancrofti (mosquito-borne; lymphatic filariasis and elephantiasis). Strongyloides stercoralis (autoinfection, hence hyperinfection in the immunosuppressed).

Two generalisations that carry marks: helminth infections characteristically raise eosinophils and IgE (a type 2 immune response), particularly during tissue migration; and arthropods are the vectors for most of the rest of tropical medicine — Anopheles for malaria, Aedes for dengue and Zika, tsetse for trypanosomiasis, sandflies for leishmaniasis, ticks for Lyme disease and rickettsioses, fleas for plague.

Three facts from this section that recur

(1) Arthropoda is the largest phylum, insects the largest class, beetles the largest order — and molluscs second. (2) The unique structures: cnidocytes are cnidarian and nothing else, the radula is molluscan and nothing else, the water vascular system is echinoderm and nothing else, choanocytes are poriferan and nothing else. Any stem naming one of these has already given you the answer. (3) Echinoderms are deuterostomes — the single most counter-intuitive placement on the animal tree, and therefore the one most often examined.

Exam technique · naming the phylum of an animal you have never heard of

  1. Check first for a giveaway word, because half of these stems contain one. Choanocyte or collar cell → Porifera. Cnidocyte or nematocyst → Cnidaria. Comb rows or colloblasts → Ctenophora. Radula → Mollusca. Water vascular system or tube feet → Echinodermata. Notochord → Chordata. Each of these is unique to one phylum, so the stem has already answered itself: mark it and move on in ten seconds. Do not then read the other four options looking for something better. (Flame cells and protonephridia are a strong hint at Platyhelminthes but not a proof — they turn up in other groups too, so treat them as narrowing evidence rather than as an instant answer.)
  2. Otherwise harvest the stem into the five slots — S · G · C · B · S, "Some Guts Can Be Segmented" — and stop as soon as it is unique. Most descriptions are decided by slots three and four. Write the five letters down the margin and fill what you are given; blanks are fine.
  3. Two-character kills that finish the job. "Moulted cuticle" or "ecdysis" → Ecdysozoa, so only Nematoda or Arthropoda remain; add "pseudocoelom" or "longitudinal muscle only" → Nematoda, add "jointed appendages" or "exoskeleton" → Arthropoda. "Trochophore larva" or "spiral cleavage" → Lophotrochozoa, so it is never a nematode or an arthropod. "Segmented with a septate coelom and closed circulation" → Annelida, not Arthropoda.
  4. The distractor that is present in almost every item of this family is an option grouping annelids with arthropods because both are segmented. Kill it on sight, every time, without re-deriving it: segmentation has three independent origins (annelid, arthropod, chordate). The second most common is an option grouping echinoderms with cnidarians because both are radial — echinoderm radial symmetry is pentaradial, secondary and adult-only, and the larva is bilateral.
  5. Sanity check on any answer you reach by elimination — the described animal must be internally consistent. A diploblast with a coelom (no mesoderm, therefore no mesodermal lining), a deuterostome with a moulted cuticle (ecdysis is the ecdysozoan, hence protostome, synapomorphy), or an animal with both a radula and an exoskeleton it sheds — each is a combination no phylum has. If your answer requires one, you have misread a slot. This catches more errors than re-reading the stem does.
§ 10

Land plants: four lineages, four innovations

The ideaMoving onto land is a list of problems: you dry out, you have nothing to hold you up, water no longer arrives at every cell, and your sperm can no longer swim to an egg. The four great plant lineages are best held in the head as four solutions arriving in order, each keeping everything the one before it had. If you can say which problem each innovation solved, you never have to memorise which group has what — you can derive it. And because plant biology is the most heavily weighted and most often skipped block on this paper, the derivation is worth practising.

Definition & key facts

Alternation of generations
Every land plant has two multicellular bodies in its life cycle: a diploid sporophyte and a haploid gametophyte. Meiosis in the sporophyte makes spores; the spore grows by mitosis into a gametophyte; the gametophyte makes gametes by mitosis; fusion restores the sporophyte.
sporophyte (2n) —meiosis→ spores (n) —mitosis→ gametophyte (n) —mitosis→ gametes (n) —fusion→ 2n the one place plants differ fundamentally from you: meiosis makes SPORES, not gametes
Full definition, symbols and units
Spore versus gamete
A spore is haploid and develops into a new multicellular organism on its own. A gamete is haploid and must fuse with another first. Both are haploid; only one of them is a reproductive dead end without a partner.
Heterospory
Producing two sizes of spore: megaspores (→ female gametophytes) and microspores (→ male gametophytes). Homosporous plants make one kind, giving a bisexual gametophyte. Heterospory is the precondition for the ovule and therefore for the seed.
Ovule and seed
An ovule = megasporangium + one or two integuments + the retained megaspore. After fertilisation it matures into a seed = embryo + food store + seed coat (the hardened integuments).
Pollen grain
The male gametophyte, reduced to two or three cells and wrapped in sporopollenin. It travels through air, not water, and germinates a pollen tube that delivers non-motile sperm directly to the egg.

Reading the table as a chain of consequences

Why bryophytes are short. Two independent ceilings. Without lignified xylem there is no mechanical support and no long-distance water transport, so the plant depends on capillary movement across its surface. And the sperm has a flagellum and must swim, so fertilisation only happens in a film of rainwater a few centimetres deep. A moss cannot be tall and reproduce. Both constraints are lifted by different innovations, and it takes two more steps to lift the second.

Why lignin was not enough. Ferns are fully vascular. They, alongside the tree lycophytes and horsetails that dominated them, built the coal forests of the Carboniferous, and they grow metres tall — yet their gametophyte is still a free-living thumbnail-sized prothallus with antheridia releasing swimming sperm. Fully solving the height problem left the reproductive problem untouched — which is why "vascular tissue freed plants from water" is a wrong answer waiting to be chosen.

What the seed actually solves. Heterospory allows the female gametophyte to stay inside the sporangium and be fed by the parent, and the male gametophyte to be reduced to a dust grain that the wind will carry. Once the sperm is delivered inside a tube growing through the parent's tissue, water is no longer needed at all. And the fertilised ovule becomes a package — embryo, food, waterproof coat — that can wait out a season and travel. Dormancy and dispersal come free with the seed.

What the flower adds. The carpel encloses the ovules, which protects them and allows the stigma to screen pollen. Enclosure also produces the fruit, the matured ovary, and with it animal dispersal. The flower recruits an animal to carry pollen directly to another flower of the same species, which is enormously more efficient than broadcasting into the wind — and it is the origin of one of the great co-evolutionary radiations. Double fertilisation is the accounting improvement: the food store is only built once a sperm has arrived, whereas a pine commits resources to a female gametophyte before it knows whether pollination will succeed.

Worked example · get the ploidy right through a seed

Maize (2n = 20) and pine (2n = 24)

State the chromosome number, and the parent of origin, for each tissue of a mature maize kernel; then do the same for a pine seed.

  1. Maize 2n = 20, so n = 10.
  2. Embryo = egg (n) + sperm (n) → 2n = 20, half maternal, half paternal.
  3. Endosperm = two polar nuclei (n + n) + one sperm (n) → 3n = 30, and note the ratio: two maternal genomes to one paternal, or 67 % maternal. This imbalance is what genomic imprinting in endosperm is about.
  4. Seed coat (testa) develops from the integuments of the ovule — ordinary tissue of the parent sporophyte. 2n = 20, entirely maternal. It has no paternal contribution at all, which is why seed-coat characters do not segregate in an F2 the way embryo characters do.
  5. Pericarp (the fruit wall, fused to the coat in a grain) = matured ovary wall → 2n = 20, maternal.
  6. Pine, 2n = 24, n = 12: embryo 2n = 24; nutritive tissue = female gametophyte, n = 12 — haploid, purely maternal, and built before fertilisation; seed coat 2n = 24, maternal; the papery wing comes from the cone scale and is also 2n maternal. There is no endosperm and no double fertilisation.
Maize: embryo 20 · endosperm 30 · coat 20 · pericarp 20 — Pine: embryo 24 · food store 12 · coat 24

The exam version of this asks for one number and offers the others as distractors. Two habits protect you: always ask which generation is this tissue part of, and remember that anything outside the embryo sac belongs to the mother.

Seven plant statements that read like textbook prose and are wrong or incomplete

"Meiosis in plants produces gametes." It produces spores. Gametes are made by mitosis in an already-haploid gametophyte. This one fact resolves a surprising number of otherwise hard questions.

"Bryophytes have no conducting tissue." Mosses have hydroids (water) and leptoids (sugars). What they lack is lignified xylem, which is why they cannot use those cells for support. "Non-vascular" is a claim about lignin, not about plumbing.

"Ferns lack vascular tissue." Ferns are tracheophytes and have both xylem and phloem. What they lack is seeds.

"Vascular tissue freed plants from needing water for reproduction." No — pollen did. Ferns are vascular and their sperm still swim.

"A pine seed contains endosperm." It contains haploid female gametophyte tissue. Endosperm is 3n and angiosperm-only, and is produced only after the second fertilisation.

"Gymnosperm means the seed has no covering." It means the ovule is not enclosed in an ovary. Gymnosperm seeds have perfectly good seed coats.

"Bryophytes are a paraphyletic grade" and "gymnosperms are paraphyletic." Both were standard teaching and both have been challenged. Recent phylogenomic work supports a monophyletic Bryophyta (hornworts sister to mosses + liverworts) and a monophyletic living Acrogymnospermae, with gnetophytes nested among the conifers. Give the traditional grade answer if the stem demands it, but know that the current literature does not agree — this is exactly the sort of item where a recently updated fact appears as an unexpectedly correct option.

What is in the seed, and when it was put there

Pine prepays — n, packed before · Flower pays on delivery — 3n, cooked after

Pine prepays — in a gymnosperm the food store is the n female gametophyte, and it is laid down before fertilisation, so a pine commits resources without yet knowing whether pollination will succeed. Purely maternal, haploid, and not endosperm. Flower pays on delivery — in an angiosperm, double fertilisation makes 3n endosperm (one sperm + two polar nuclei) and it is built only after a sperm has arrived, which is the accounting improvement the flower brings. So "a pine seed contains endosperm" is false twice over, on ploidy and on timing. My own hook; the underlying contrast is standard teaching and the prepay / pay-on-delivery framing is the "accounting" metaphor this section's prose already uses. It carries the two facts examiners actually swap — the ploidy and the before/after — and nothing else. It says nothing about the embryo (2n in both) or the seed coat (2n and maternal in both), and it does not need to: those need no mnemonic, because the seed coat is the ovule's integuments and the integuments are ordinary parent-sporophyte tissue. See the technique block below for the one question that generates every ploidy in the seed.

What the paper reliably asks about plant diversity

(1) Which generation is dominant in a named group, and which structure is haploid or diploid — nearly always answerable from the alternation-of-generations diagram alone. (2) Which innovation first permitted some capability: height → lignified vascular tissue; reproduction away from water → pollen; dormancy and dispersal of the embryo → the seed; animal-mediated pollination and dispersal → the flower and fruit. (3) Ploidy through a seed. (4) Monocot versus eudicot, which is pure recall: one cotyledon, parallel venation, scattered vascular bundles, fibrous roots, floral parts in threes, one pollen aperture — against two, netted, ringed, tap-rooted, fours and fives, three apertures.

Exam technique · ploidy through a seed, and which-innovation-first items

  1. Ploidy needs no mnemonic and you should not build one — it needs one question asked of each named tissue: which generation is this part of, and whose body is it? Sporophyte tissue is 2n, gametophyte tissue is n, and endosperm alone is 3n. That is the entire content of the topic.
  2. Then apply the one boundary rule — anything outside the embryo sac is ordinary tissue of the parent sporophyte: 2n and entirely maternal, with no paternal contribution at all. Integument → seed coat, ovary wall → pericarp, cone scale → the pine seed's papery wing. Inside: embryo 2n (half each), endosperm 3n (two maternal genomes to one paternal). Run the two rules and the maize kernel answers itself — 20, 30, 20, 20 — without recall.
  3. Read the plant's name first, because it decides whether 3n exists at all. Pine, spruce, cycad, Ginkgo, "a conifer" → no double fertilisation, no endosperm, food store haploid. Any option offering 3n endosperm for a gymnosperm is a free kill, and it is offered often.
  4. Which-innovation-first items: match the problem to the solution, never the group to the feature. Height and long-distance water transport → lignified xylem. Reproduction away from standing water → pollen. Dormancy, provisioning and dispersal of the embryo → the seed. Animal-mediated pollination and dispersal → flower and fruit. Enclosure and screening of the ovule → the carpel.
  5. Lignified vascular tissue is the standing distractor for the water question and it is the most attractive wrong answer in the plant block, because lignin genuinely is transformative. One sentence kills it every time: a fern is fully vascular and its sperm still swim. Keep that sentence loaded.
  6. One free kill available anywhere in plant biology — strike any option in which meiosis produces gametes in a plant. Meiosis makes spores; gametes come from mitosis in an already-haploid gametophyte. This is worth checking on every plant item even when the stem is about something else.
§ 11

The vertebrates

The ideaVertebrate classification is a nested series of answers to the same handful of problems: how to catch food, how to move, how to breathe, and how to reproduce away from water. Each named group is simply the set of animals that inherited one more answer than the group before it. Learn the innovations in order and the groups fall out of them; learn the groups as a list and the innovations never do.

Definition & key facts

Chordate
An animal possessing, at some stage of its life cycle, four features: a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits or clefts, and a muscular post-anal tail. "At some stage" is the whole of the definition — most of the four are embryonic in you.
notochord · dorsal hollow nerve cord · pharyngeal slits · post-anal tail the four chordate synapomorphies — present at some stage, not necessarily in the adult

The nested series of vertebrate innovations, in order

Head · spine · jaws · bone · lobes · limbs · egg

Seven monosyllables, seven steps, read left to right and each one keeps everything before it. Head — cranium and neural crest → Craniata. Spine — vertebrae → Vertebrata. Jaws — from pharyngeal arch 1, plus paired appendages → Gnathostomata. Bone — an ossified endoskeleton, with operculum and lungs → Osteichthyes. Lobes — the muscular, bone-supported fin lobe → Sarcopterygii. Limbs — with digits, plus a neck and a pelvis fused to the spine → Tetrapoda. Egg — the amniotic egg → Amniota. Because the series is nested, it answers the commonest item on the section directly: the innovation that defines a group is the one appearing at its own step, never one inherited from an earlier step — which is why "four limbs" is a wrong answer for Amniota even though every amniote has them. My own hook, compressing the chain this section already prints into seven beats. It encodes only the spine of the tree, not the side branches hanging off it — Chondrichthyes, Actinopterygii, Amphibia, Reptilia + Aves and Mammalia are not in it, and each of those is defined by its own novelty in the table above. Two of them are traps the hook will not save you from: chondrichthyan cartilage is a derived reversal, not a stage before bone; and the swim bladder is derived from lungs, not the other way round.

The three convergences you will be asked about

Endothermy arose independently in the bird line and in the mammal line — their common ancestor was a Carboniferous ectotherm. The four-chambered heart arose independently at least twice: crocodilians have one and are archosaurs, mammals have one and are synapsids, and the intermediate stages differ. Viviparity has arisen more than a hundred times across vertebrates, including in sharks, in many squamates, and in mammals. Every one of these is a ready-made polyphyletic grouping, and every one of them is a ready-made exam question about homology versus analogy.

Set against those, the classic homology: the mammalian malleus and incus are the reptilian articular and quadrate — the two bones that formed the jaw joint — repurposed as ossicles when the dentary–squamosal joint took over. The fossil series through the cynodonts documents the transition bone by bone, and the developmental evidence agrees: both derive from the first pharyngeal arch. It is the single best demonstration in vertebrate biology that homology is about origin and position, not function.

Five vertebrate statements to reject

"Cartilaginous fishes are primitive because cartilage came before bone." Bone is ancient — the earliest jawless vertebrates were armoured in it. Chondrichthyan cartilage is a secondary loss, a reversal, and reversals are homoplasy just as convergence is.

"Lungs evolved from swim bladders." The other way round. Lungs are ancestral for bony fishes; the swim bladder of ray-finned fishes is the derived organ.

"Adult humans have a notochord." The embryo does. The adult has nuclei pulposi, which are its remnants — and a stem that says "at some stage of the life cycle" is testing precisely this.

"Birds are not reptiles; Aves is a separate class." Traditional rank-based classification says so; phylogeny says birds are nested inside archosaurs, hence inside Reptilia. This is the standard illustration of paraphyly and is examined as such.

"Amphibians were the first vertebrates to live on land, so their eggs are terrestrial." Amphibian eggs lack a shell and lack the amnion; almost all must be laid in water or in permanently damp places. Terrestrial reproduction arrives with the amniotes, not with the first legs.

The two questions this section generates

First, "which innovation defines group X" — answer from the table, and note that the answer is always the character that appeared on that branch, never one inherited from further back. Saying that amniotes are defined by having four limbs is wrong not because it is false but because it is ancestral. Second, a grade question: "which of these is not a valid clade", with Mammalia, Amniota, Osteichthyes (which contains the tetrapods) and "fish" on offer — three complete branches and one grade. The memorable version of the reasoning: a lungfish is more closely related to a cow than to a salmon. Once that sentence stops feeling strange, this whole note has landed.
