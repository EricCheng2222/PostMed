Prokaryotes, Viruses, Protists and Fungi

This block is worth 8.0 % of the general-biology half and has produced exactly four questions in every year examined — between 4 and 8 marks depending on whether they land in Part I or Part II, and the most predictable return on revision anywhere on the paper. One idea organises the whole of it: the organisms here are sorted by what they are made of and how they get carbon and energy, not by how they look. A bacterium and an archaeon can be the same size and shape and still belong to different domains; a water mould and a fungus can both build hyphae and be no more related than you are to a kelp; a virus and a rickettsia can both be obligate intracellular parasites while only one of them is a cell. Every distinction the examiners reuse is a consequence of that: read the chemistry of the envelope, the source of carbon and electrons, and the machinery for copying information, and the classification falls out on its own.

§ 01

The prokaryotic cell — and what "prokaryote" does not mean

The ideaA bacterium is small, and almost everything else about it follows from that. Being small means a large amount of surface for the amount of inside, so material floods in and out fast enough that the cell does not need internal delivery systems. It also means the whole cell can be run from one loop of DNA sitting loose in the middle, with no wrapping and no gate, so a message can be read and turned into protein while it is still being written. The word "prokaryote" itself, though, only says what these cells lack — a nucleus — and lumping two utterly different groups together on the strength of a missing part is exactly the kind of shortcut the exam likes to punish.

Definition & key facts

Prokaryotic cell
A cell whose genetic material is not enclosed by a nuclear envelope and which lacks membrane-bounded organelles. It is a negative definition — a description of absences — and it covers two domains, Bacteria and Archaea, that are not each other's closest relatives.
prokaryote = cell without a nucleus — a grade of organisation, not a clade Archaea are more closely related to you than to E. coli
Full definition, symbols and units
Nucleoid
The irregular region where the chromosome sits, condensed by supercoiling and by nucleoid-associated proteins (HU, H-NS, Fis). It is not membrane-bounded, so there is no import or export step between transcription and translation.
Plasmid
A small, usually circular, self-replicating DNA molecule carrying genes that are useful but not essential — resistance, virulence, metabolic tricks. Because it replicates from its own origin it can be gained or lost without killing the cell, which is what makes it such an effective vehicle for horizontal transfer (§ 06).
Surface-area-to-volume ratio
For a sphere of radius r, SA/V = 3/r. Exchange with the environment scales with surface; demand scales with volume — so small cells can support far higher metabolic rates per unit mass.

What is actually inside

Work outward. In the middle is the nucleoid: usually one circular, double-stranded chromosome, supercoiled into a body that occupies perhaps a quarter of the cell but is fenced off by nothing. Around it, free 70S ribosomes in their thousands. Around them, a plasma membrane that has to do all the jobs a eukaryote spreads across mitochondria, ER and Golgi — it carries the electron-transport chain and the ATP synthase, it makes lipids, and it secretes. Outside that, in almost every species, a cell wall, and outside that, optionally, a capsule, pili and flagella (§ 02–03).

The consequences of having no nucleus are not cosmetic. There is no compartment to export mRNA from, so a ribosome can begin translating a transcript while RNA polymerase is still elongating it. That makes introns and splicing essentially impossible in the bacterial scheme, and it makes the operon — one promoter, one polycistronic mRNA, several proteins of a shared pathway — the natural unit of regulation. It also gives bacteria a regulatory device eukaryotes cannot use: attenuation, in which the ribosome's progress on a leader peptide decides whether transcription of the rest of the operon continues.

Four textbook sentences about prokaryotes that are wrong or half-wrong

"Prokaryotic cells have no internal membranes." They have no organelles, which is not the same claim. Cyanobacteria carry an extensive internal thylakoid system; magnetotactic bacteria build magnetosomes from membrane invaginations; Planctomycetes have a membrane-bounded anammoxosome. What they lack is a nucleus and the classical organelle set. (Conversely, mesosomes — still drawn in older texts as respiratory infoldings — are now understood to be fixation artefacts.)

"Prokaryotes have no cytoskeleton." False since the late 1990s. FtsZ is a tubulin homologue that forms the constricting ring at division; MreB is an actin homologue that makes a rod a rod rather than a sphere; crescentin is an intermediate-filament homologue. The eukaryotic cytoskeleton was inherited, not invented.

"Bacteria outnumber your own cells ten to one." The 10:1 figure traces to a 1970s back-of-envelope estimate and has been repeated ever since. Careful re-measurement gives roughly 3.8 × 10¹³ bacteria against 3.0 × 10¹³ human cells — about 1.3 : 1. Quote the ratio if you must, but know it is an approximate parity, not an order of magnitude.

"Prokaryotes are a natural group." They are not. Bacteria and Archaea diverged before either gave rise to eukaryotes, and on current evidence eukaryotes arose from within the archaea. "Prokaryote" is a useful shorthand for a cell architecture, in the same way "invertebrate" is a useful shorthand for an animal without a backbone — and just as unnatural.

The distinctions this section is actually tested on

Three recur. (1) 70S versus 80S, and the corollary that mitochondrial ribosomes are 70S-like — which is the reason chloramphenicol suppresses bone marrow, linezolid causes cytopenias, and aminoglycosides are ototoxic. Selective toxicity is never perfect. (2) Binary fission is not mitosis: no spindle, no chromosome condensation, no nuclear envelope to break down. A stem saying "bacteria divide by mitosis" is always false. (3) Circular versus linear is a tendency, not a law — Borrelia burgdorferi has a linear chromosome and Vibrio cholerae has two chromosomes. If an option says "all bacteria have a single circular chromosome", the word "all" is doing the damage.

Why size sets the ceiling

The arithmetic is worth doing once because it explains the whole architecture. A spherical cell of radius r has SA/V = 3/r. At r = 0.5 µm that is 6 µm⁻¹; at r = 10 µm it is 0.3 µm⁻¹ — a twenty-fold difference in exchange capacity per unit of metabolising cytoplasm. A bacterium can therefore run its entire respiratory chain on the sheet of membrane it already has, and can rely on diffusion to move metabolites from one end of itself to the other in milliseconds. A eukaryotic cell cannot: it needs internal membranes to restore the surface area, and motor proteins on tracks to move material about. Compartmentalisation is not a refinement, it is the price of getting big.

There is a lower bound too. A cell must hold a genome, a few hundred ribosomes and its enzymes; the smallest free-living bacteria, Mycoplasma, are around 0.1–0.3 µm across with a 580 kb genome and about 470 genes. Below that the parts do not fit.

§ 02

The wall, the Gram stain, and what the stain predicts

The ideaA bacterium lives with far more dissolved material inside it than outside, so water is permanently trying to rush in. Nothing but a rigid outer mesh stops it from bursting, and that mesh is one enormous cross-linked molecule that wraps the whole cell like a string bag. Some bacteria build the bag thick and leave it exposed; others build it thin and then wrap a second greasy sheet around the outside of it. That single difference in architecture is what a hundred-year-old staining trick reports on, and it decides which drugs can reach their target, what makes you feverish when the cell dies, and which laboratory reagents will dissolve the cell.

Definition & key facts

Peptidoglycan (murein)
A single covalently continuous macromolecule — a sacculus — built from alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) joined by β-1,4 bonds, with short peptides hanging off each NAM and cross-linked between neighbouring chains. It resists turgor pressure the way the mesh of a string bag resists the load inside it.
Gram + : thick PG, no outer membrane → purple  ·  Gram − : thin PG + outer membrane → pink the colour is a readout of whether the wall can trap a dye complex during alcohol washing
Full definition, symbols and units
Transpeptidase (penicillin-binding protein, PBP)
The enzyme that forms the peptide cross-link, displacing the terminal D-alanine of a D-Ala–D-Ala pair. β-Lactams are structural mimics of that dipeptide and acylate the active-site serine irreversibly.
Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)
The outer leaflet of the Gram-negative outer membrane: lipid A (the endotoxin, a TLR4 agonist) + core oligosaccharide + O antigen (the serotyping polysaccharide — the O in E. coli O157:H7).
Periplasm
The aqueous compartment between the outer and plasma membranes of a Gram-negative cell, containing the thin peptidoglycan layer, binding proteins, and — clinically — most of the cell's β-lactamase.

Why the wall exists at all

A bacterium in fresh water or in tissue fluid faces a turgor pressure of several atmospheres. Remove the wall enzymatically in an isotonic buffer and you get a viable, round protoplast; do it in water and the cell lyses instantly. That is the whole mechanism of β-lactams and of lysozyme: neither of them punctures anything. They stop the mesh from being repaired and extended, and the cell then destroys itself from within the moment it tries to grow. Hence the single most useful downstream fact in this section: β-lactams only kill growing cells. A dormant or stationary-phase population is phenotypically tolerant, which is why abscesses need drainage and why persisters survive a course of penicillin without carrying any resistance gene.

Why the stain works — the mechanism, not the recipe

Crystal violet is a small cationic dye and it enters every cell, Gram-positive and Gram-negative alike. Gram's iodine is a mordant: it complexes with the dye inside the cell to form a large, poorly soluble crystal-violet–iodine aggregate. At this point both cell types are purple, and if you stopped here the test would be useless.

The discrimination happens at step three. Ethanol or acetone does two different things to the two envelopes. In a Gram-positive cell it dehydrates the thick, highly cross-linked peptidoglycan, shrinking it and closing the pores through which the bulky complex would have to escape; the dye is trapped. In a Gram-negative cell the alcohol dissolves the lipid-rich outer membrane outright, and the one or two remaining layers of peptidoglycan cannot retain anything — the complex washes straight out and the cell is left colourless. Safranin, applied last, stains everything it can, but on a cell already saturated with dark purple it is invisible. Gram-negative cells are pink because they are the only ones left transparent enough to take a counterstain.

The four Gram-stain sentences that look right and are not

"Gram-negative bacteria do not take up crystal violet." They do. Every Gram-negative cell is purple after step 1 and after step 2. Losing the dye is not the same as never binding it, and this is the single most common way the stain is mis-explained.

"Safranin stains Gram-positive cells red." Safranin is applied to every cell on the slide and binds to Gram-positive cells too; it simply cannot be seen against the retained crystal violet.

"Gram-positives are harder to kill because their wall is thicker." Backwards. Thickness is not a permeability barrier — the Gram-positive sacculus is an open mesh that small molecules cross freely, which is exactly why penicillin G and vancomycin work so well against them. The Gram-negative outer membrane is the barrier, and combined with periplasmic β-lactamases and efflux pumps it is why Gram-negatives are intrinsically the more resistant group.

"A pink cell is a Gram-negative organism." Only if the stain was done well on a young culture. Over-decolorisation turns Gram-positives pink, and Gram-positive cells in old, stationary-phase or antibiotic-damaged cultures lose wall integrity and stain Gram-variable. The stain reports on the state of the wall at that moment, not on the organism's taxonomy.

Endotoxin versus exotoxin — an examiner's favourite pair

Endotoxin is not a secreted poison. It is lipid A, a structural component of the Gram-negative outer membrane, and it is released chiefly when the cell dies — which is why a large dose of a bactericidal antibiotic can transiently worsen a Gram-negative sepsis. It is a lipid, so it is heat-stable (it survives autoclaving, hence the separate requirement for pyrogen-free water), it is only weakly immunogenic, and it cannot be converted into a toxoid vaccine. Its potency is not chemical but immunological: TLR4 on macrophages reads lipid A as a signal of Gram-negative invasion and releases TNF-α, IL-1 and IL-6, producing fever, vasodilation and, at high dose, septic shock.

Exotoxins are the opposite in almost every respect: secreted proteins, made by Gram-positives and Gram-negatives alike, heat-labile, extremely potent by mass, specific in their action (tetanus toxin cleaves synaptobrevin; diphtheria toxin ADP-ribosylates eEF-2), strongly antigenic, and convertible to toxoids — which is why we vaccinate against tetanus and diphtheria and not against endotoxin.

Go deeper · the organisms the Gram stain fails on, and what to say instead

Roughly one clinically important group in five is not usefully described by a Gram result, and each failure has a structural reason.

  • Mycobacteria — acid-fast. M. tuberculosis and M. leprae are Gram-positive by ancestry and wall chemistry but stain poorly and inconsistently, because outside the peptidoglycan lies an arabinogalactan layer esterified to mycolic acids — β-hydroxy fatty acids 60–90 carbons long that form a waxy, nearly impermeable second membrane. The stain that does work is Ziehl–Neelsen: carbol fuchsin driven in with heat, then acid-alcohol, which fails to remove it — hence "acid-fast" — then methylene blue as counterstain. The same wax explains the clinical picture: slow growth (generation time 15–20 h), resistance to drying and disinfectants, survival inside macrophages, and drug regimens measured in months. Isoniazid inhibits mycolic-acid synthesis and is therefore active against essentially nothing else.
  • Mycoplasma — no wall at all. The smallest free-living cells, bounded by a plasma membrane that uniquely among bacteria contains sterols for rigidity. They are pleomorphic, invisible on Gram stain, pass filters that retain other bacteria, and are intrinsically resistant to every β-lactam and to vancomycin — not by acquiring a resistance gene, but because the drug target does not exist. Treat with a macrolide, a tetracycline or a fluoroquinolone. This is the cleanest example on the paper of resistance caused by absence of a target.
  • Spirochaetes (Treponema, Borrelia, Leptospira) are Gram-negative in architecture but too slender to see by light microscopy after Gram staining; they are visualised by dark-field or by silver stains, and their axial filaments (endoflagella) run inside the periplasm, producing the corkscrew motility that lets them bore through viscous tissue.
  • Chlamydia and Rickettsia are true Gram-negative bacteria — cells with ribosomes, walls and metabolism — that happen to be obligate intracellular parasites. They stain poorly and are conventionally identified by other means. Their existence is why "obligate intracellular parasite" alone never distinguishes a virus from a cell (§ 08): these are killed by doxycycline, and no virus is.
  • L-forms are wall-deficient variants that arise under β-lactam pressure and can revert. They are a reminder that the wall is dispensable in an osmotically protected environment such as tissue fluid.

A useful habit: when a stem describes an organism by its stain, immediately ask what the stain implies about the envelope, and only then reach for a drug. "Gram-negative rod" should make you think "outer membrane, porins, periplasmic β-lactamase, lipid A", not a list of species names.

Antibiotic targets that follow directly from this section

Wall synthesis: β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams) acylate the transpeptidase; vancomycin is different in kind — it binds the substrate, the D-Ala–D-Ala terminus, rather than the enzyme, which is why resistance requires re-engineering the peptide to D-Ala–D-Lac and why its size confines it to Gram-positives. Outer membrane: polymyxin B and colistin bind lipid A — Gram-negatives only. Ribosome (70S): aminoglycosides and tetracyclines on the 30S, macrolides and chloramphenicol on the 50S. Nucleic acids: fluoroquinolones inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, rifampicin binds the β subunit of bacterial RNA polymerase. Folate: sulfonamides and trimethoprim work because bacteria must synthesise folate while we absorb it. Every one of these is selective for a structure or a pathway we do not have — and the exceptions to that selectivity (70S-like mitochondrial ribosomes) are exactly where the side-effects come from.

§ 03

Shape, surface, motility and the endospore

The ideaEverything a bacterium bolts onto its outside is there for one of three jobs: to stick to something, to move towards something, or to avoid being eaten. And when conditions become impossible, a small number of species can do something none of the rest can — build a sealed, dried-out, almost indestructible copy of their own DNA inside themselves and then die around it, leaving a grain that will still be alive when the food comes back. That is a survival device, not a way of making more bacteria, and confusing the two is one of the reliable ways to lose a mark here.

Definition & key facts

Endospore
A dormant, dehydrated, multi-layered survival structure formed inside a vegetative cell of certain Gram-positive bacteria (Bacillus, Clostridium) when nutrients run out. One cell produces exactly one spore and then lyses, so the cell count never rises.
one vegetative cell → one endospore → one vegetative cell survival, never multiplication — sporulation is not reproduction
Full definition, symbols and units
Capsule / glycocalyx
An organised polysaccharide layer outside the wall (poly-D-glutamate in Bacillus anthracis is the protein exception). It blocks phagocytosis by preventing opsonin binding, mediates adhesion, and is the antigen in the pneumococcal, meningococcal and Hib vaccines.
Fimbriae and the sex pilus
Fimbriae are short, numerous adhesins. The sex (conjugative) pilus is long, present in ones and twos, encoded by the F plasmid, and its job is to draw a donor and recipient together for conjugation (§ 06). Different structures, different genes, different purposes.
D-value (decimal reduction time)
The time at a stated temperature required to kill 90 % of a population — one log₁₀ unit. Killing is first-order, so survival is exponential in time and no finite treatment reaches zero; sterility is defined probabilistically.

Shape, and why it is a weak identifier

The vocabulary is small: coccus (sphere), bacillus (rod), vibrio (comma), spirillum (rigid helix, external flagella) and spirochaete (flexible helix, internal axial filaments). Arrangement adds a second layer, determined by the plane of division and whether daughters separate: diplo- (pairs), strepto- (chains, division in one plane), staphylo- (grape-like clusters, division in several planes). "Gram-positive cocci in clusters" is a genuinely informative phrase — it is most of the way to Staphylococcus — but shape is maintained by the MreB cytoskeleton and is not deeply phylogenetic. Rods and cocci turn up all over the bacterial tree, and shape alone identifies nothing.

The bacterial flagellum is not a small eukaryotic flagellum

This comparison is examined more often than its length would suggest, because the two structures share a name and nothing else. The bacterial flagellum is a rigid helical filament of a single protein, flagellin, screwed into a hook and a basal body embedded in the envelope. The basal body is a rotary motor — it spins, at several hundred revolutions per second, driven directly by protons flowing down their electrochemical gradient across the plasma membrane. There is no ATP hydrolysis at the motor and no motor protein walking along a track. The whole assembly sits outside the cell, not inside a membrane sleeve.

The eukaryotic flagellum is an extension of the cell, wrapped in plasma membrane, containing a 9 + 2 array of microtubules. It does not rotate; it bends, because dynein arms hydrolyse ATP to slide adjacent doublets past one another. Archaea have a third solution again — the archaellum, which rotates like a bacterial flagellum but is built from type IV pilin subunits and is powered by ATP. Three independent inventions of swimming, which is why "flagellum" is a functional word, not a homology.

Motility only pays if it is directed, and it is: chemotaxis works as a biased random walk. Counter-clockwise rotation bundles the flagella and the cell runs in a straight line; clockwise rotation flies the bundle apart and the cell tumbles, reorienting at random. Receptors compare the current concentration of an attractant with the concentration a few seconds ago — a temporal, not a spatial, comparison, because the cell is far too small to detect a gradient across its own body — and suppress tumbling while things are improving. The result is net movement up the gradient without any steering.

The endospore, and why it changes what "sterile" means

When a Bacillus or Clostridium runs out of nitrogen or carbon, an alternative developmental programme starts. The cell divides asymmetrically, the larger compartment engulfs the smaller, and the engulfed forespore is progressively fortified: a cortex of loosely cross-linked peptidoglycan, a proteinaceous coat, and in Bacillus anthracis an outer exosporium. Two chemical changes do most of the work. The core is dehydrated to perhaps 10–25 % water and loaded with calcium dipicolinate, which can be a tenth of the spore's dry mass and both stabilises and dries the core; and the DNA is saturated with small acid-soluble proteins that change its conformation and make UV photoproducts far less likely. Dry macromolecules do not denature the way wet ones do, which is the whole basis of the spore's heat resistance.

The result survives boiling for hours, desiccation for decades, ionising radiation, most disinfectants, and — clinically important — alcohol hand rub, which is why C. difficile requires soap-and-water handwashing and hypochlorite cleaning. Only the two Gram-positive genera above matter for the exam; no Gram-negative pathogen makes endospores. And germination is triggered by specific germinants (L-alanine, ribosides, in C. difficile bile salts), so a spore is not simply waiting for warmth — it is waiting for evidence that it has arrived somewhere worth waking up in.

Worked example 1 — how long must an autoclave hold, and why boiling is not sterilisation

Spores of the biological indicator Geobacillus stearothermophilus have D121 = 1.5 min and z ≈ 10 °C. A batch of instruments carries 106 spores per item. (a) How long at 121 °C is needed to reach the pharmacopoeial sterility assurance level of 10⁻⁶? (b) What would the same regime require at 111 °C? (c) A colleague proposes boiling at 100 °C instead. Comment.

  1. Thermal killing is first-order: each equal interval removes the same fraction, so survivors fall by a fixed factor of ten every D minutes and never reach exactly zero. Sterility is therefore defined as a probability, conventionally one chance in a million that a single viable organism remains.
  2. (a) Count the log₁₀ units you must cross: from N₀ = 106 down to N = 10−6 is 6 − (−6) = 12 log reductions — the "12-D" standard.
  3. t = D × 12 = 1.5 × 12 = 18 min. That is exactly why textbooks quote 121 °C for 15–20 minutes: it is not a round number, it is twelve decimal reductions with a margin.
  4. Sanity check with the survival equation: log₁₀N = 6 − 18/1.5 = 6 − 12 = −6 ✓.
  5. (b) A z-value of 10 °C means D changes ten-fold per 10 °C. So D111 = 1.5 × 10 = 15 min, and 12 D becomes 12 × 15 = 180 min — three hours instead of eighteen minutes for a drop of ten degrees.
  6. (c) Extrapolate one step further: D101 ≈ 150 min. Boiling would need on the order of 30 hours. Boiling reliably kills vegetative cells and most viruses within minutes, and it disinfects — but it does not sterilise, and no amount of patience makes it convenient. That is the entire argument for pressurising the vessel to 15 psi so that steam can reach 121 °C.
(a) 18 min · (b) 180 min at 111 °C · (c) ≈30 h at 100 °C — boiling disinfects, it does not sterilise

Sporulation is not reproduction — and three related traps

The count does not go up. One cell makes one spore and the mother cell lyses. Compare a fungal spore, or an actinomycete's chains of exospores, which are reproductive and do multiply the individual. If a stem calls the endospore "a means of reproduction", it is wrong however plausible the sentence reads.

Spores are not "dormant cells". They are metabolically inert — no detectable ATP turnover — which is different from a slow-growing or stationary-phase cell. It is also why antibiotics do nothing to them: there is no wall synthesis, no transcription and no translation to inhibit.

Prions are harder to destroy than spores. Standard autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 minutes destroys endospores but not PrPSc (§ 09), which needs 134 °C for extended cycles and 1 M NaOH. "The most heat-resistant infectious agent" is not the endospore.

§ 04

Metabolic diversity: energy, carbon, oxygen

The ideaEvery living thing has to answer two separate questions: where does my energy come from, and where do the carbon atoms of my body come from? Big organisms only ever manage two of the four possible answers — plants take energy from light and carbon from air, animals take both from food. Prokaryotes fill in the other two squares as well, living on the energy in rocks and gases, or using sunlight for power while still eating organic matter. That completed grid is the whole of prokaryotic nutrition, and it is why bacteria run the planet's chemical cycles. A third question — whether oxygen is fuel, irrelevant or poison — cuts across all four squares independently.

Definition & key facts

Nutritional mode
Two independent axes. The prefix names the energy source — photo- (light) or chemo- (oxidation of a chemical). The suffix names the carbon source — -autotroph (CO₂) or -heterotroph (organic molecules). The four combinations all exist, and two of them exist only in prokaryotes.
mode = [ photo- | chemo- ] × [ -autotroph | -heterotroph ] prefix = energy · suffix = carbon · the two are independent
Full definition, symbols and units
Chemolithoautotroph
The precise name for the "chemoautotroph": energy from oxidising an inorganic electron donor (H₂S, S⁰, NH₃, NO₂⁻, Fe²⁺, H₂), carbon from CO₂. Litho- = "from rock", and it is the part that makes the mode unique to prokaryotes.
Facultative anaerobe
An organism that respires oxygen when it is available and switches to fermentation or anaerobic respiration when it is not. Not the same as an aerotolerant anaerobe, which never uses oxygen but is not harmed by it.
Nitrogen fixation
Reduction of atmospheric N₂ to ammonia by nitrogenase. Exclusively prokaryotic, and exclusively anaerobic in practice, because the enzyme's iron–sulfur and iron–molybdenum clusters are irreversibly destroyed by O₂.

Reading the grid the way an examiner writes it

Questions on this almost always give you a physiological description and ask for the label. The reliable method is to answer the two questions separately and in order. Where does the energy come from? If the stem mentions light, chlorophyll, bacteriochlorophyll or a rhodopsin, it is photo-; if it mentions oxidising anything, it is chemo-. Where does the carbon come from? If the stem says the organism fixes CO₂, or is described as a primary producer, it is -autotroph; if it consumes or absorbs organic molecules, it is -heterotroph.

The classic trap is a bacterium that "oxidises hydrogen sulfide and fixes carbon dioxide". Students see "sulfide" — an inorganic chemical — and reach for something exotic; the answer is simply chemoautotroph, because the sulfide is the energy source and CO₂ is the carbon source. The mirror-image trap is the purple non-sulfur bacterium that "uses light but grows on acetate": that is a photoheterotroph, and calling it a photoautotroph because of the word "light" ignores the carbon axis entirely. Say the two answers out loud before choosing.

Two further points that earn marks. Not all photosynthesis makes oxygen. Cyanobacteria run the oxygenic kind with water as electron donor and were the source of the Great Oxidation Event; green and purple sulfur bacteria run the anoxygenic kind with H₂S or H₂ as donor, produce elemental sulfur rather than O₂, and have only one photosystem. And mixotrophy is real: Euglena photosynthesises in the light and ingests particles in the dark, so an organism need not sit in exactly one square.

Oxygen: fuel, irrelevance or poison

Oxygen is a superb terminal electron acceptor — its reduction potential is what makes aerobic respiration yield so much more ATP than fermentation — and it is also chemically dangerous, because partial reduction produces superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and, worst of all, the hydroxyl radical. Whether an organism can live in air is therefore a question about its defences and about the chemistry of its central enzymes.

"Obligate anaerobes lack catalase, and that is why oxygen kills them"

This is the standard textbook sentence and it is not quite right, in a way the exam has room to exploit. Lactobacillus and most streptococci are catalase-negative and grow perfectly well on an open bench: they are aerotolerant. What actually separates tolerance from death is superoxide dismutase (or a manganese-based substitute), which removes the far more damaging superoxide radical — organisms lacking any means of disposing of O₂˙⁻ cannot survive air. And there is a second mechanism that has nothing to do with enzymes at all: strict anaerobes run their central metabolism on oxygen-labile enzymes such as pyruvate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase and nitrogenase, whose exposed iron–sulfur clusters are destroyed by O₂ directly. So the safe formulation is: obligate anaerobes lack adequate defences against reactive oxygen species and depend on enzymes that oxygen inactivates. Catalase is a convenient laboratory test (it separates Staphylococcus, catalase-positive, from Streptococcus, catalase-negative), not the explanation.

Go deeper · the nitrogen cycle, and why nitrogenase forced bacteria to build houses

Nitrogen is the textbook case of prokaryotes running a global cycle no eukaryote can join, and it is examined in ecology questions as often as in this block.

  • Fixation. N₂ has a triple bond of 941 kJ·mol⁻¹ and is essentially inert. Nitrogenase breaks it at a cost of 16 ATP per N₂ — 8 per ammonia — and inevitably wastes reducing power making one H₂ per turnover. Only prokaryotes have the enzyme: free-living Azotobacter, symbiotic Rhizobium in legume root nodules, cyanobacteria such as Anabaena, and Frankia in alder.
  • The oxygen problem, and three solutions. Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by O₂, yet fixing nitrogen needs enormous amounts of ATP, which is most easily made by aerobic respiration. The conflict is resolved structurally: Anabaena differentiates specialised heterocysts with thick glycolipid walls and no photosystem II, so the cell that fixes nitrogen makes no oxygen; legume nodules produce leghaemoglobin, which buffers free O₂ to nanomolar levels while still delivering it to the bacteroid's respiratory chain; and some cyanobacteria simply separate the two processes in time, photosynthesising by day and fixing by night. If a stem asks why heterocysts exist, the answer is one sentence about enzyme chemistry.
  • Nitrification. Ammonia is oxidised to nitrite by Nitrosomonas and nitrite to nitrate by Nitrobacter — both chemoautotrophs living on the energy released. Marine ammonia oxidation is dominated by archaea (Thaumarchaeota), which is one of the strongest arguments against the "archaea are extremophiles" cliché.
  • Denitrification. Under low oxygen, facultative organisms respire nitrate instead, reducing it stepwise to N₂ and returning it to the atmosphere. This is anaerobic respiration, not fermentation — there is still an electron transport chain and a proton gradient, just a different terminal acceptor. It is also why waterlogged soil loses fertiliser.
  • Ammonification and anammox. Decomposers return organic N to ammonia; the anammox reaction (NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂), performed by planctomycete bacteria in a membrane-bounded anammoxosome, accounts for a large share of the ocean's nitrogen loss and was only characterised in the 1990s.

The exam-relevant summary: plants cannot use N₂. Every nitrogen atom in your protein entered the biosphere either through a bacterial nitrogenase or through the Haber–Bosch process, which uses an iron catalyst, 400–500 °C and 200 atmospheres to do at industrial scale what a root nodule does at 25 °C.

What gets asked here

Overwhelmingly two things. Classification from a description — answer the energy question and the carbon question separately, then combine. And the oxygen classes, usually via a thioglycollate tube diagram or a clinical vignette (a deep puncture wound and trismus ⇒ C. tetani, an obligate anaerobe; a gastric biopsy ⇒ H. pylori, a microaerophile). Keep the two axes clean: an obligate anaerobe can be a chemoheterotroph (Clostridium) or a chemoautotroph (a methanogen), because oxygen tolerance and nutritional mode are independent questions.

§ 05

Binary fission and the growth curve

The ideaA bacterium does not have babies one at a time; it splits into two, and then each of those splits, so the population does not grow by addition but by repeated doubling. Doubling gets out of hand astonishingly fast — a single cell dividing every twenty minutes would, if nothing stopped it, outweigh the Earth within two days. So the interesting question is never how fast bacteria can grow but what stops them, and the familiar four-phase curve is really a record of a population running into its own waste and its own consumption.

Definition & key facts

Binary fission
Replication of the chromosome from a single origin, active segregation of the two copies to opposite poles, then constriction of an FtsZ ring at midcell and septum synthesis. There is no spindle, no chromosome condensation and no nuclear envelope to dismantle — it is not mitosis, and the two words are not interchangeable.

The four phases
Lag — no increase in number but intense metabolic activity, inducing the enzymes the new medium requires. Exponential (log) — constant, maximal doubling rate; the only phase in which cells are physiologically uniform. Stationary — division rate equals death rate as a nutrient runs out or waste accumulates; cells switch on the stress sigma factor RpoS and become smaller and tougher. Death (decline) — exponential loss of viability.

Fission, and a puzzle worth resolving

Replication starts at a single origin, oriC, and runs bidirectionally until the two forks meet at the terminus; ParA/ParB systems pull the daughter origins apart; then FtsZ polymerises into a ring at midcell, recruits the wall-synthesising divisome, and constricts. Nothing about it resembles mitosis, and a stem that says otherwise is testing whether you noticed.

Here is the puzzle. Replicating the E. coli chromosome takes about 40 minutes at 37 °C, yet E. coli divides every 20 minutes in rich medium. How can a cell divide faster than it can copy its own genome? The answer is multifork replication: a new round of replication initiates before the previous one has finished, so a rapidly growing cell contains several partially replicated chromosomes at once and hands each daughter a genome that was started two generations ago. The consequence to remember is that fast-growing bacteria carry more than one genome equivalent, and genes near the origin are present at higher copy number than genes near the terminus — which is why highly expressed genes such as rRNA operons cluster near oriC.

Worked example 2 — reading a growth curve, forwards and backwards

Broth is inoculated with 5 × 10³ CFU·mL⁻¹ and sampled hourly: 0 h: 5 × 10³ · 1 h: 5 × 10³ · 2 h: 2 × 10⁴ · 3 h: 8 × 10⁴ · 4 h: 3.2 × 10⁵ · 5 h: 1.28 × 10⁶. (a) How long is the lag phase? (b) What is the generation time? (c) Predict the count at 8 h if exponential growth continued. (d) When would the culture reach 10⁹ CFU·mL⁻¹? (e) Why will it not?

  1. (a) The count is unchanged between 0 and 1 h, so the lag phase is 1 hour. Note what is not implied: the cells are transcribing and translating hard, building the transport proteins and enzymes the new medium needs. Lag is a preparation phase, not dormancy.
  2. (b) Take two points inside the exponential region — say 2 h and 5 h. N/N₀ = 1.28 × 10⁶ / 2 × 10⁴ = 64, and log₂ 64 = 6, so 6 generations elapsed in 180 min.
  3. g = 180 / 6 = 30 min. Check it against a shorter interval: 3 h → 4 h is 8 × 10⁴ → 3.2 × 10⁵, a factor of 4 = 2², so 2 generations in 60 min ✓. Consistency across intervals is what tells you the culture really is in exponential phase.
  4. (c) From 5 h to 8 h is 180 min = 6 more generations, so N = 1.28 × 10⁶ × 2⁶ = 1.28 × 10⁶ × 64 = 8.19 × 10⁷ CFU·mL⁻¹.
  5. (d) From 1.28 × 10⁶ to 10⁹ is a factor of 781, and log₂ 781 = 9.61 generations = 9.61 × 30 = 288 min = 4.8 h, i.e. at about 9.8 h after inoculation. The quick route to the same answer: the increase is 2.89 log₁₀ units and each ten-fold takes 3.32 generations, so 2.89 × 3.32 × 30 min ≈ 288 min.
  6. (e) It will not, because a batch culture is closed. Somewhere around 10⁹ CFU·mL⁻¹ a nutrient becomes limiting or waste accumulates, growth decelerates and the culture enters stationary phase. Every number in (c) and (d) is an extrapolation of exponential phase and is only valid while that phase lasts — which is precisely why examiners like to ask for a prediction well beyond the data.

The arithmetic that shows up, and the two slips that cost marks

The first slip is counting the lag phase as exponential growth. In the example above, using the full 5 hours instead of the 4 hours of actual growth gives 10 generations and 5.12 × 10⁶ — four times too high. Always establish when growth started. The second is confusing n with t: n is the number of doublings, t is clock time, and they are equal only in the accidental case g = 1. Read the units in the stem before you touch the exponent. If you are given log₁₀ data, convert with the ten-fold rule (3.32 generations per log₁₀ unit) rather than reaching for a calculator you do not have.

Exponential growth never lasts — and the arithmetic of why

Take E. coli at g = 20 min and let it run unchecked for 48 hours. That is n = 2880/20 = 144 generations, so 2¹⁴⁴ ≈ 2.2 × 10⁴³ cells. At roughly 10⁻¹² g each, the culture would weigh about 2.2 × 10²⁸ kg — some 3700 times the mass of the Earth. The number is absurd, and that is the point: unrestricted exponential growth is never the biological reality. It is also worth carrying into the ecology note, where the same equation reappears as the exponential model of population growth and gets the same correction, a carrying capacity. A stem describing a culture "growing exponentially for several days" is describing something impossible in a closed vessel, though a chemostat — in which fresh medium is added and culture removed at a fixed dilution rate — genuinely can hold cells in exponential phase indefinitely.

§ 06

Horizontal gene transfer: transformation, transduction, conjugation

The ideaYou inherited all your genes from your parents. A bacterium does not have to. It can pick up a piece of DNA left behind by a neighbour that burst, receive one delivered by a virus that packed the wrong cargo, or be handed one directly by a donor cell that reaches across and connects. Genes therefore move sideways between lineages that are not related, which is why a resistance gene that appears in one species this year can appear in an unrelated species in a different hospital the next. None of these three processes makes a new cell, and none of them is sex in the sense you know it — there is no fusion of gametes and no zygote, only a donor, a recipient and a fragment.

Definition & key facts

Horizontal (lateral) gene transfer
Movement of genetic material between organisms other than from parent to offspring. In bacteria it takes exactly three forms — transformation (uptake of naked DNA), transduction (delivery by a bacteriophage) and conjugation (transfer during direct cell contact) — plus the internal reshuffling done by transposons.
transformation = naked DNA  ·  transduction = phage  ·  conjugation = contact identify the vehicle and the mechanism names itself

Competence
The physiological state in which a cell can bind and import DNA. It is natural and regulated in Streptococcus pneumoniae, Bacillus subtilis, Haemophilus and Neisseria; it is induced artificially in the laboratory with CaCl₂ and heat shock, or by electroporation.
F factor
A ≈100 kb conjugative plasmid carrying the tra operon (pilus and transfer machinery) and an origin of transfer, oriT. A cell carrying it is F⁺; one that does not is F⁻; one in which F has integrated into the chromosome is Hfr (high frequency of recombination).
R plasmid
A conjugative plasmid carrying antibiotic-resistance genes, usually assembled from cassettes captured by an integron and moved in by transposons. One conjugation event can transfer resistance to several unrelated drug classes at once.

Transformation

A cell lyses; its DNA is released into the medium; a competent neighbour binds a fragment, imports one strand while degrading the other, and — if the sequence is similar enough — RecA splices it into the chromosome by a double crossover. Everything the experiment turns on is in that description. The DNA is naked during transit, so adding DNase to the medium abolishes transformation and nothing else. And because integration requires homology, transformation works best between close relatives — an important limit on how promiscuous the process really is.

Historically this is where molecular biology started. Griffith showed in 1928 that a heat-killed virulent pneumococcus could convert a live avirulent strain into a virulent one — "the transforming principle" — and Avery, MacLeod and McCarty showed in 1944 that the principle was destroyed by DNase but not by protease or RNase. The logic of that experiment is exactly the logic you need for a modern exam question about which mechanism a treatment rules out.

Transduction

A bacteriophage packages DNA into its capsid, and sometimes it packages the wrong DNA. Two variants, and the distinction between them is one of the most reliably examined items in this whole block:

  • Generalised transduction occurs during a lytic infection. The phage chops the host chromosome up, and a packaging system that measures by volume rather than by sequence occasionally fills a head with a random piece of bacterial DNA. Any host gene can be transferred, at low and roughly equal frequency, and the resulting particle carries no phage genome at all — it cannot lyse the next cell, it merely injects bacterial DNA into it.
  • Specialised transduction occurs only with a temperate phage that has been integrated as a prophage. On induction, excision is occasionally imprecise and takes a slice of the flanking chromosome with it. Only genes adjacent to the integration site can move — for phage λ, integrated between the gal and bio operons, that means gal and bio and essentially nothing else — and the transducing particle is a hybrid carrying both phage and host genes.

The one-line discriminator: generalised = any gene, from a lytic cycle, by mispackaging; specialised = only the neighbours of the prophage, from a lysogenic cycle, by faulty excision. Note also that the transferred DNA is protected inside a protein capsid, so transduction is DNase-resistant and does not require cell contact — the two facts that let an experiment identify it.

Conjugation, F, Hfr and the map in minutes

The donor extends a sex pilus, contacts a recipient, and retracts it to bring the two cells together into a stable mating pair. A relaxase nicks the plasmid at oriT and a single strand is transferred into the recipient while rolling-circle replication regenerates the double strand on both sides. That last detail explains the standard result: in an F⁺ × F⁻ cross the donor keeps a copy and the recipient gains one, so both cells end up F⁺, but no chromosomal genes move at all.

An Hfr donor is one in which F has recombined into the chromosome. Transfer still begins at oriT, but now the chromosome trails behind it, so chromosomal genes enter the recipient in a strict order determined by their distance from the integration site. Two consequences follow, and both are examined. First, the further a gene is from oriT, the later it enters and the more likely the fragile mating bridge is to have broken first — so distal markers appear at lower frequency. Second, the remaining part of the F sequence is transferred last, so it almost never arrives: the recipient in an Hfr cross acquires chromosomal genes but remains F⁻. Only if the entire chromosome transfers — which is rare — does it become Hfr itself.

Because transfer proceeds at a constant rate, time of entry is map distance. That is why the classical E. coli map is calibrated in minutes, with 100 minutes for the whole circle, and why the technique is called interrupted mating: you mix the strains, remove samples at intervals into a blender to shear the mating bridges, and score which donor markers each sample has acquired.

Three sentences that read like textbook prose and are wrong

"Conjugation is bacterial sexual reproduction." No new individual is produced — the count of cells is the same before and after — there is no gamete, no fusion, no zygote and no meiosis. Transfer is unidirectional, donor to recipient. It is gene transfer, and reproduction in bacteria is always binary fission. The same warning applies to ciliate conjugation in § 10, which likewise exchanges nuclei without making a new organism.

"After an Hfr × F⁻ cross the recipient becomes Hfr." Almost never, because the F sequence is split by integration and its remaining part enters last. Expect a recombinant F⁻ cell. The parallel error is thinking an F⁺ × F⁻ cross transfers chromosomal genes; it does not, it transfers the plasmid.

"Antibiotic resistance arises because bacteria mutate in response to the drug." Two errors in one sentence. Mutations arise independently of the drug — Luria and Delbrück's fluctuation test and the Lederbergs' replica plating both demonstrated this — and the antibiotic selects, it does not induce. And in the clinic the dominant route is not mutation at all but horizontal transfer of an R plasmid that already carries several resistance cassettes, which is why resistance spreads far faster than a mutation rate would allow.

How this is asked

Almost always as a small experiment rather than a definition. Two auxotrophic strains are mixed and prototrophs appear — now the stem adds one manipulation and asks what it proves. DNase in the medium kills transformation only. A filter between the strains that blocks cells but passes virions kills conjugation only. Both survive ⇒ transduction. Neither works ⇒ conjugation. A second family of question gives interrupted-mating times and asks for gene order, as in worked example 3. Get into the habit of writing the three vehicles down the margin and crossing them off.

§ 07

Archaea: the third domain

The ideaUnder a microscope an archaeon looks like a bacterium: same size, same lack of a nucleus, often the same shape. But the molecules it is built from tell a different story. Its membrane is stitched together with a different kind of chemical bond and the wrong-handed backbone; its wall is not made of the bacterial material at all; and the machinery it uses to read and copy its DNA looks like a simplified version of yours rather than a version of E. coli's. That mixture — a bacterial body running eukaryotic software — is not a curiosity. It is the evidence that these are a separate domain, and that your own cells came from somewhere inside it.

Definition & key facts

Archaea
One of the three domains of life, recognised by Carl Woese in 1977 from small-subunit rRNA sequences. Prokaryotic in cell architecture, distinct from Bacteria in membrane and wall chemistry, and closer to Eukarya in the machinery of transcription and translation.
bacterial body plan  +  membranes of its own  +  eukaryote-like information machinery three claims; a stem that gets any one of them backwards is the wrong option

Where they live, and the cliché to avoid

The three groups every syllabus lists are real and worth knowing. Methanogens are strict anaerobes of swamps, sediments, landfill, sewage digesters and the rumen and human gut. Extreme halophiles such as Halobacterium live in the Dead Sea and in salt-evaporation ponds at 15–30 % NaCl and colour them pink; some run a light-driven proton pump, bacteriorhodopsin, that has nothing to do with chlorophyll and generates a proton-motive force directly from a photon. Extreme thermophiles such as Sulfolobus and Pyrococcus grow in hot springs and hydrothermal vents; the current record for growth, held by Methanopyrus kandleri, is 122 °C under pressure.

But "all archaea are extremophiles" is false and increasingly obviously so. Thaumarchaeota are among the most abundant organisms in the open ocean and are the dominant ammonia oxidisers there; archaea are ordinary members of soil, plankton and the human gut microbiota. What the extremes really demonstrate is that archaeal chemistry — ether bonds, branched chains, sometimes a monolayer membrane rather than a bilayer — tolerates conditions that would hydrolyse a bacterial membrane. And note the corollary that examiners like: no archaeon is a known human pathogen, which is remarkable given how many of them live in and on us.

Four archaeal traps, one of them notorious

Thermus aquaticus is a bacterium, not an archaeon. The source of Taq polymerase is a thermophilic bacterium from a Yellowstone hot spring. "Lives somewhere hot" is not a taxonomic argument, and this particular substitution appears in question banks constantly. (Pfu polymerase, from Pyrococcus furiosus, genuinely is archaeal.)

"Archaea have peptidoglycan with a different cross-link." They have no peptidoglycan at all. Pseudomurein is a different sugar backbone (β-1,3, N-acetyltalosaminuronic acid) cross-linked through L-amino acids rather than D. The similarity of the name is the trap.

"Archaea are an intermediate between bacteria and eukaryotes." No — a domain is not a halfway house. Archaea are a lineage with their own long history; the current two-domain view is that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea (the Asgard group is their closest known relative), by acquiring an alphaproteobacterial endosymbiont that became the mitochondrion. So the eukaryotic cell is an archaeal host with bacterial power stations — which is exactly why your nuclear information machinery looks archaeal and your mitochondrial ribosomes look bacterial.

"Archaea are ancient and bacteria are modern" — or the reverse. The name means "ancient ones" and is a historical accident of the first habitats they were found in. The two prokaryotic domains are the same age; both have been evolving for the same 3.5 billion years.

The three-way comparison is the question

Almost every archaeal item on this paper is a three-column comparison in disguise: you are given a character and asked which domains share it. Learn the pattern rather than the rows. Archaea resemble bacteria in everything about cell architecture — size, no nucleus, circular chromosome, 70S ribosomes, operons, binary fission. They resemble eukaryotes in everything about information handling — RNA polymerase, general transcription factors, histones, methionine initiation, DNA replication proteins. And they resemble neither in membrane and wall chemistry, which is uniquely theirs. Three buckets, and almost any character sorts into one of them.

§ 08

Viruses: structure, and why they are not cells

The ideaA virus is a set of instructions in a box. It carries the information for making more of itself and nothing whatever for doing the making — no machinery for building proteins, no way of generating energy, no ability to grow or to divide. Outside a host it is an inert particle, chemically no more alive than a crystal of salt. Inside one it takes over an existing factory and redirects it. That is the whole definition, and it is why the question "is a virus alive?" has an unsatisfying answer: it has genes and it evolves, but it does not metabolise, and every criterion you might use puts it on a different side of the line.

Definition & key facts

Virus
An obligate intracellular parasite consisting of a nucleic-acid genome — DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded — enclosed in a protein capsid, sometimes wrapped in a host-derived envelope. It has no ribosomes, no metabolism and no capacity to divide.
virus = genome + capsid ( ± envelope )  —  no ribosomes, no ATP, no division the three absences are what make it not a cell
Full definition, symbols and units
Capsid and capsomere

Symmetry is helical (tobacco mosaic virus, rabies), icosahedral (poliovirus, adenovirus) or complex (T-even phages with an icosahedral head and a contractile tail; poxviruses).
Envelope
A lipid bilayer stolen from a host membrane — plasma membrane, ER or nuclear envelope — and studded with virus-encoded glycoproteins. Enveloped viruses are consequently fragile: alcohol, detergents and drying destroy them, which is why soap works so well on influenza and SARS-CoV-2 and much less well on a naked enterovirus.

Determined by whether a viral surface protein fits a molecule on the target cell. HIV needs CD4; rabies uses the acetylcholine receptor; phage λ uses the maltose porin LamB. Tropism is a lock-and-key statement, not a statement about which cells are "suitable".

Why "obligate intracellular parasite" is not the answer

This is the single most common mistake in reasoning about viruses, and it is easy to fix. Chlamydia trachomatis and Rickettsia rickettsii are obligate intracellular parasites too — they cannot be grown on agar and can only reproduce inside a host cell. They are nevertheless unambiguously cells: they have a plasma membrane, a peptidoglycan-derived wall, their own 70S ribosomes, their own metabolism (limited — they steal ATP), and they divide by binary fission. And they are killed by doxycycline, because they have a ribosome for it to bind. No virus is killed by any antibiotic.

So the criterion that actually works is the absence of translational and metabolic machinery. A virus has no ribosome, so it cannot make a protein; it has no electron-transport chain or glycolytic pathway, so it cannot make ATP; it has no membrane it can grow and constrict, so it cannot divide. Its particles are assembled from separately synthesised parts, like a machine coming off a production line, rather than produced by the growth and division of a pre-existing one. That is a different mode of reproduction from anything a cell does, and it is why viruses fall outside the cell theory rather than at the small end of it.

"Antibiotics don't work on viruses because viruses are too small"

The conclusion is right and the reason is wrong, which is exactly the shape of a good distractor. Size has nothing to do with it. Antibiotics work by hitting structures that bacteria have and we do not — a peptidoglycan wall, a 70S ribosome, DNA gyrase, folate synthesis. A virus has none of those, so there is nothing for the drug to bind. Antiviral drugs therefore have to target the few virus-encoded enzymes that exist: reverse transcriptase and integrase and protease for HIV, RNA-dependent RNA polymerase for hepatitis C and SARS-CoV-2, neuraminidase for influenza, thymidine kinase-activated aciclovir for herpes. The short antiviral list compared with the long antibacterial one is a direct consequence of how little machinery a virus brings with it.

Worked example 4 — titre, multiplicity of infection and burst size

A phage lysate is diluted ten-fold in series. 0.1 mL of the 10⁻⁶ dilution is plated with a lawn of E. coli and gives 74 plaques. (a) What is the titre? (b) 0.1 mL of the undiluted lysate is added to 5 mL of culture at 2 × 10⁸ cells·mL⁻¹ — what is the MOI? (c) In a separate one-step experiment, 5 × 10⁷ cells are infected and, after a single round, the lysate contains 1.0 × 10¹⁰ PFU. What is the burst size? (d) At an MOI of 0.05, what fraction of cells escapes infection altogether?

  1. (a) Each plaque is a hole in the lawn started by one infectious particle, so the plate carried 74 PFU. Undo the volume first, then the dilution: 74 PFU ÷ 0.1 mL = 740 PFU·mL⁻¹ in the diluted tube.
  2. Undo the dilution: 740 ÷ 10⁻⁶ = 7.4 × 10⁸ PFU·mL⁻¹. Forgetting the 0.1 mL step — quoting 7.4 × 10⁷ — is the single commonest error on this calculation, so always write the volume correction as its own line.
  3. (b) Virions added: 7.4 × 10⁸ × 0.1 = 7.4 × 10⁷ PFU. Cells present: 2 × 10⁸ × 5 = 1.0 × 10⁹. So MOI = 7.4 × 10⁷ / 1.0 × 10⁹ = 0.074. MOI is a ratio, not a concentration — it has no units and does not change if you dilute both together.
  4. (c) burst size = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ / 5 × 10⁷ = 200 virions per infected cell, which is a typical T4 value. Note this requires MOI ≪ 1 so that each cell is infected once; at high MOI several phages enter one cell and the arithmetic no longer gives a per-infection yield.
  5. (d) Virions distribute over cells at random, so the number arriving at a given cell is Poisson. The probability of receiving none is e^(−MOI) = e^(−0.05) = 0.951, so 95.1 % of cells escape and only 4.9 % are infected. This is why phage stocks are titred at low MOI: you want plaques from single independent infections, well separated on the lawn.

What is genuinely examined about viral structure

Three things. The three absences (ribosomes, metabolism, division) as the definition of "not a cell", with the Chlamydia/Rickettsia counter-example ready. Enveloped versus naked and the practical consequence — envelopes come from host membranes, make the virion labile to alcohol and detergent, and are where the receptor-binding glycoproteins sit. Genome diversity: viruses are the only entities that use all four possible nucleic-acid configurations, so "all viruses contain DNA" and "all viruses contain both DNA and RNA" are both false, and a single virion contains one kind of nucleic acid only. Plaque arithmetic accounts for most of the calculational items.

§ 09

Lytic, lysogenic, retroviral — and less than a virus

The ideaOnce a virus is inside, it has a strategic choice: cash in now or wait. Cashing in means hijacking everything, making hundreds of copies and bursting the cell — fast, but it destroys the habitat. Waiting means splicing your genome quietly into the host's own and letting the host copy you every time it divides, so you multiply without anyone dying. Some viruses go further and write themselves permanently into the host's DNA in the wrong direction, from RNA back to DNA. And at the far end of the spectrum are agents that have given up nucleic acid altogether and still manage to be infectious.

Definition & key facts

Lytic and lysogenic cycles
In the lytic cycle the phage genome commandeers the host at once, is replicated, packaged, and released by lysis, killing the cell. In the lysogenic cycle it integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage and is replicated passively with it. A phage able to do both is temperate; one that can only lyse is virulent.
lytic: replicate now and burst  ·  lysogenic: integrate and be copied for free the same phage, two programmes, one repressor deciding between them
Full definition, symbols and units
The λ switch
Lysogeny is maintained by one protein, the cI repressor, which binds the operators OL and OR and blocks transcription of every lytic gene — including its rival, cro. DNA damage activates RecA, which stimulates cI to cleave itself; the lytic genes are derepressed and the prophage excises. A prophage is therefore not silent — it is being actively repressed by a gene it expresses.
Lysogenic conversion
Acquisition of a new phenotype by the host because of prophage genes. Diphtheria toxin, cholera toxin, Shiga toxin, botulinum toxin and the erythrogenic toxin of scarlet fever are all phage-encoded: cure the bacterium of its prophage and it stops being pathogenic.
Retrovirus
A (+)-sense ssRNA virus, diploid (two genome copies per virion), carrying reverse transcriptase, integrase and a protease. Its DNA copy integrates as a provirus and is thereafter a permanent part of the host genome.

Why lysogeny is a good strategy, and what a prophage is doing

It is tempting to read the lytic cycle as "the real one" and lysogeny as dormancy. That gets the arithmetic backwards. A lysogen divides every twenty minutes and copies the prophage every time, so a single integration event produces millions of phage genomes in a day — without killing a single host, and while enjoying complete protection from the environment. The lysogen is also immune to superinfection by the same phage, because the cI it is making represses any incoming genome of the same type.

Prophages are not passengers either. A large fraction of the genes that make bacteria dangerous arrive this way: Corynebacterium diphtheriae is harmless without its β-prophage, Vibrio cholerae without CTXφ, and enterohaemorrhagic E. coli makes Shiga toxin from a prophage — which is why antibiotics that damage DNA can induce the prophage, increase toxin release and worsen haemolytic uraemic syndrome. The link to § 06 is direct: faulty excision of exactly these prophages is what produces specialised transduction.

"A prophage is a dormant, transcriptionally silent virus"

The most quoted description of lysogeny and the most misleading. The prophage is continuously transcribing the cI repressor — that is precisely what maintains lysogeny, and an induction stimulus works by destroying cI, not by supplying an activator. Two related errors follow. Lysogeny is not the same as latency in animal viruses: herpes simplex persists in sensory ganglia as an episome that never integrates, whereas HIV latency is integrated, in resting memory CD4 T cells — which is exactly why HIV cannot currently be cured while herpes reactivation can be suppressed. And lysogeny does not mean the phage has lost the ability to lyse; it means the ability is repressed and one dose of UV will reveal it.

Retroviruses and HIV

A retrovirus inverts the usual flow of information. Its virion carries two copies of a (+)-sense RNA genome and, crucially, the enzymes to convert them: reverse transcriptase makes a double-stranded DNA copy in the cytoplasm, integrase splices that copy into a host chromosome as a provirus, and thereafter the host's own RNA polymerase II transcribes it from the LTR promoter as though it were a host gene. Late in the cycle a virus-encoded protease cleaves the Gag and Gag-Pol polyproteins into their functional parts — a maturation step that happens after budding, and without which the released particle is non-infectious.

HIV-1 adds specifics worth knowing because they are directly clinical. Entry requires gp120 to bind CD4 and then a co-receptor — CCR5 on macrophages and memory T cells early in infection, CXCR4 on naive T cells later — after which gp41 drives membrane fusion. People homozygous for the CCR5-Δ32 deletion are largely resistant to infection, which is the basis of the handful of cures achieved by transplanting Δ32/Δ32 marrow. The virus destroys CD4⁺ T cells; when the count falls below about 200 cells·µL⁻¹, opportunistic infections define AIDS — Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, cerebral toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis, all of which appear later on this page.

Worked example 5 — the arithmetic that forces combination antiretroviral therapy

Take the standard order-of-magnitude figures: the HIV-1 genome is 9.7 × 10³ nucleotides; reverse transcriptase has no proofreading and an error rate of about 10⁻⁴ per base per replication cycle; an untreated patient produces roughly 10¹⁰ new virions per day. (a) How many mutations does an average new genome carry? (b) How many virions produced today carry a resistance mutation at one specified position? (c) At two specified positions? (d) At three? (e) What does that imply for therapy?

  1. (a) Expected errors per genome per cycle = 9.7 × 10³ × 10⁻⁴ = 0.97 ≈ 1. Roughly every genome differs from its template at one position. HIV in one patient is not a virus, it is a swarm of variants — the technical term is a quasispecies.
  2. (b) A given base is mutated in a fraction 10⁻⁴ of genomes, so today's yield contains 10¹⁰ × 10⁻⁴ = 10⁶ virions bearing that specific change. Every possible single-point resistance mutation is generated a million times a day, before any drug is given. Monotherapy cannot work: it is not creating resistance, it is selecting a variant that was already there.
  3. (c) Two independent mutations in the same genome: 10¹⁰ × (10⁻⁴)² = 10¹⁰ × 10⁻⁸ = 10² — a hundred a day. Still comfortably enough for a two-drug regimen to fail.
  4. (d) Three: 10¹⁰ × 10⁻¹² = 10⁻² per day, i.e. about one such genome every hundred days — and that genome then has to survive and expand. Three drugs push the required mutant below the daily production of the virus, which is the whole design principle of combination antiretroviral therapy.
  5. (e) Therefore: at least three agents, from at least two classes with non-overlapping resistance pathways — nucleoside RT inhibitors (zidovudine, tenofovir: chain terminators lacking a 3′-OH), non-nucleoside RT inhibitors, integrase inhibitors (raltegravir, dolutegravir), protease inhibitors (the -navir drugs) and entry inhibitors (maraviroc against CCR5, enfuvirtide against gp41). Combination therapy suppresses viral load below the level at which replication — and therefore mutation — continues at all.
  6. State the assumption, because it is where the model bends. This treats each drug as requiring one independent mutation. That is close to true for lamivudine and the NNRTIs, which have a low genetic barrier and fail rapidly as monotherapy — exactly as the arithmetic predicts. Boosted protease inhibitors and second-generation integrase inhibitors need several mutations, each individually costly in fitness, and correspondingly rarely fail. The arithmetic explains the shape of the guidelines, not the choice of a particular drug.

Prions and viroids: infectious agents that are less than a virus

A viroid is a naked circular single-stranded RNA of 246–401 nucleotides with no capsid and no protein-coding capacity at all. It is replicated by the host's own RNA polymerase II by a rolling-circle mechanism, and it causes disease — potato spindle tuber, coconut cadang-cadang — by interfering with host gene regulation, probably through RNA silencing pathways. Viroids infect plants only. (Hepatitis delta is the nearest animal analogue, but it is a satellite that encodes one protein and borrows the hepatitis B envelope, so it needs HBV to be infectious.)

A prion goes one step further: it contains no nucleic acid whatsoever. PrPC is a normal, GPI-anchored, α-helix-rich neuronal glycoprotein that everyone has. PrPSc is the same polypeptide, same sequence, refolded into a β-sheet-rich form that aggregates, resists proteases, and — this is the mechanism — acts as a template that converts adjacent PrPC molecules into its own conformation. The information being transmitted is a shape, not a sequence, which is why prions were so hard to accept and why Prusiner's protein-only hypothesis was so contentious.

Three consequences follow directly and are examined. First, prion disease can be sporadic, inherited or acquired — the same disease from a spontaneous misfolding, a germline PRNP mutation, or ingestion of infected tissue — which no conventional infection can manage. Second, there is no immune response and no inflammation: the protein is a self protein, so the brain shows spongiform vacuolation and gliosis without any infiltrate, and there is no fever and no antibody test. Third, prions are the most resistant infectious agents known: a routine 121 °C autoclave cycle does not destroy them, and decontamination requires 134 °C for extended cycles, 1 M NaOH or incineration. The clinical set is Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (sporadic, familial and the variant form from BSE-infected beef), kuru, Gerstmann–Sträussler–Scheinker and fatal familial insomnia in humans, with scrapie in sheep, BSE in cattle and chronic wasting disease in deer.

The distinctions to have ready

Virulent vs temperate phage — the ability to lysogenise, not the severity of the disease. Lytic vs lysogenic — two cycles of one temperate phage, not two kinds of phage. Prophage vs provirus — the same idea in bacteria and animals, but a provirus is permanent and a prophage can excise. Latency vs lysogeny — herpes latency is episomal, HIV latency is integrated. Prion vs viroid — protein with no nucleic acid versus nucleic acid with no protein; the pair is almost designed for a matching item, and the reliable way to keep them straight is that a viroid contains RNA and infects plants, while a prion is protein and infects mammalian brain.

§ 10

Protists: a grab-bag with a clinical core

The idea"Protist" is not a group. It is what is left over after you take the plants, the animals and the fungi out of the eukaryotes, and the leftovers include organisms as different from each other as you are from a mushroom. So there is no set of shared features to learn. What there is instead is a small number of organisms in that leftover pile that cause an enormous amount of human disease — and one of them, the malaria parasite, has probably shaped the human genome more than any other organism alive.

Definition & key facts

Protist
An informal term for any eukaryote that is not a land plant, an animal or a fungus. The group is paraphyletic — it excludes some descendants of its own common ancestor — so the old "Kingdom Protista" has been abandoned. Modern classifications distribute these organisms across several eukaryotic supergroups.
protist = eukaryote − (plants + animals + fungi) a definition by subtraction, and therefore not a clade
Full definition, symbols and units
Nutritional modes among protists
Photoautotrophic (the algae — diatoms, dinoflagellates, red, green and brown algae); heterotrophic by ingestion (protozoa, which phagocytose); heterotrophic by absorption (oomycetes and some parasites); and mixotrophic (Euglena, which does both depending on light). One informal group, four nutritional strategies — further evidence that it is not a natural group.
Definitive vs intermediate host

Malaria, and why the fever comes in waves

A female Anopheles mosquito injects sporozoites with its saliva. These reach the liver within an hour and multiply inside hepatocytes — the exo-erythrocytic stage, which produces thousands of merozoites and causes no symptoms at all, which is why malaria has an incubation period. Merozoites released into the blood invade erythrocytes, and there the cycle that produces the disease begins: ring form → trophozoite → schizont → rupture, releasing 16–32 new merozoites to invade fresh red cells.

The cycle is synchronised, so a large cohort of red cells ruptures at once, releasing haem, parasite debris and glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchors that trigger a burst of TNF-α and IL-1. That burst is the fever spike, and its rhythm is simply the length of the erythrocytic cycle: 48 hours in P. falciparum, P. vivax and P. ovale (fever on day 1 and day 3 by the old inclusive counting — hence tertian), 72 hours in P. malariae (day 1 and day 4 — quartan). The exponential arithmetic is worth noticing: at 16 merozoites per schizont, three cycles multiply the parasite burden 16³ = 4096-fold — six days to turn a million parasites into four billion, which is why untreated falciparum malaria deteriorates so abruptly.

Some merozoites differentiate instead into gametocytes, which do nothing in the human but are taken up by the next biting mosquito. Fertilisation and meiosis happen in the mosquito gut, an oocyst forms on the gut wall, and sporozoites migrate to the salivary glands. Because the sexual stage is in the insect, the mosquito is the definitive host and we are the intermediate host — a phrase that reliably feels wrong and is reliably tested.

Two species-specific facts carry clinical weight. P. vivax and P. ovale leave dormant hypnozoites in the liver that reactivate weeks to years later, so clearing the blood stage is not a cure — primaquine or tafenoquine is needed, after checking G6PD status because these drugs cause haemolysis in deficiency. And P. falciparum is the lethal one: it invades red cells of all ages, so parasitaemia can reach very high levels, and it exports PfEMP1 to the erythrocyte surface, causing infected cells to adhere to capillary endothelium. That sequestration is what produces cerebral malaria and placental malaria, and it is also how the parasite avoids being filtered by the spleen.

Malaria as evolution's clearest case study

This block and the population-genetics block meet here, and the paper exploits it. P. falciparum has imposed such intense selection that several human polymorphisms are maintained at high frequency despite being harmful in homozygotes — the textbook definition of balanced polymorphism by heterozygote advantage. Sickle-cell trait (HbAS) gives substantial protection against severe malaria while HbSS causes sickle-cell disease; the thalassaemias and G6PD deficiency do something similar; and being Duffy-negative confers near-complete resistance to P. vivax, because Duffy is the receptor vivax uses to enter red cells — which is why vivax is largely absent from West Africa. If a question asks for an example of natural selection acting on a human population within recorded history, this is the answer.

Go deeper · the supergroups, secondary endosymbiosis, and the algae that matter

Modern eukaryotic classification replaces "Protista" with a handful of supergroups. You will not be asked to recite them, but the shape of the tree explains several exam-relevant facts.

  • Excavata — organisms with a feeding groove and, in several lineages, reduced mitochondria: diplomonads (Giardia, mitosomes), parabasalids (Trichomonas, hydrogenosomes) and euglenozoans, which include the euglenids and the kinetoplastids (Trypanosoma, Leishmania, named for the single large mitochondrial DNA mass, the kinetoplast). The reduced mitochondria are secondarily simplified, not primitively absent — a claim that was believed for years and is now known to be wrong.
  • SAR — three lineages united by molecular data. Stramenopiles: diatoms (silica frustules; a large share of global primary production and of the oxygen you breathe), brown algae (Laminaria, giant kelp), and the oomycetes. Alveolates: dinoflagellates (red tides, saxitoxin; many are bioluminescent), apicomplexans (Plasmodium, Toxoplasma) and ciliates (Paramecium). Rhizarians: foraminiferans and radiolarians, whose shells built the chalk.
  • Archaeplastida — red algae, green algae and the land plants, all descended from the single primary endosymbiosis that captured a cyanobacterium.
  • Amoebozoa and Opisthokonta — the amoebozoans (Amoeba, Entamoeba, slime moulds) and, crucially, the opisthokonts, which contain both the fungi and the animals. That is why § 11 insists fungi are not plants.

Ciliate nuclear dimorphism is a favourite because it makes a general point. A Paramecium has a polyploid macronucleus that runs the cell and a diploid micronucleus that is used only for sex. In conjugation two cells exchange haploid micronuclei; both then separate and later divide. Genetic recombination and reproduction are therefore completely decoupled — no new individual is made by conjugation, exactly as in bacteria.

Oomycetes are the trap. Water moulds, downy mildews and Phytophthora infestans — the agent of the Irish potato famine — grow as fine filaments, absorb nutrients, and were classified as fungi for a century. They are stramenopiles. Their walls are cellulose, not chitin; their life cycle is diploid-dominant, not haploid; and they produce biflagellate zoospores. Convergent evolution on a filamentous absorptive lifestyle, nothing more. If a stem describes a "fungus-like organism with cellulose walls", the answer is never a fungus.

§ 11

Fungi: absorption, hyphae, a haploid life

The ideaA fungus eats by digesting its food where the food lies and then drinking the results. Because everything has to be absorbed across a surface, the winning body shape is one with almost nothing but surface: a network of threads a few micrometres wide, spreading through whatever it is feeding on. Two further facts complete the picture, and both cut against the intuition that fungi are a kind of plant. Their walls are made of the same material as an insect's shell, not of cellulose — and they spend nearly their whole lives with a single set of chromosomes, becoming diploid only for the brief moment before meiosis.

Definition & key facts

Fungus
A eukaryotic heterotroph that feeds by absorption: it secretes hydrolytic exoenzymes, digests its substrate externally, and takes up the small molecules. Its cell wall is chitin, its membrane sterol is ergosterol, and it is more closely related to animals than to plants.

Why the body is a net of threads

Absorptive nutrition sets the design brief. Digestion happens outside the organism, so the rate of feeding is proportional to the area of contact between fungus and substrate — and a filament is the cheapest way to buy area. A hypha grows only at its extreme tip, extending into fresh material, while the older regions behind it transport nutrients back. The result is a body with an enormous surface-to-volume ratio and essentially no transport problem, and it is why fungi have no need of anything resembling a vascular system despite reaching hectares in size.

The trade-off is water. A filament that thin loses water instantly in dry air, which is why fungal bodies live inside their food and only the reproductive structure — the mushroom, which is nothing but tightly packed hyphae — is pushed out into the open. Yeasts represent the other solution: give up the filament, live unicellularly in a liquid environment, and divide by budding. "Yeast" is a growth form, not a taxon; brewer's yeast is an ascomycete and Cryptococcus is a basidiomycete. Several pathogens are thermally dimorphic — Histoplasma, Blastomyces, Coccidioides, Sporothrix — growing as moulds at 25 °C in the environment and as yeasts at 37 °C in tissue, which means the same organism looks entirely different depending on where you sampled it.

A life spent haploid

This is the part that is genuinely different from anything else on the syllabus, and it is worth slowing down for. In an animal, the dominant, visible, growing organism is diploid and the haploid stage is a single cell that lives for hours. In most fungi it is the reverse: the mycelium you can see is haploid, and the diploid stage is a single nucleus that exists for a matter of hours before meiosis destroys it.

Between those two there is a stage with no counterpart elsewhere. When two compatible mycelia meet, their cytoplasms fuse (plasmogamy) but their nuclei do not. The resulting cell contains two distinct haploid nuclei side by side — a dikaryon, written n + n — and in the Basidiomycota this state can persist for years and constitutes the bulk of the organism's life. It is not diploid: there are two nuclei, each with one set of chromosomes, and they replicate and divide in step without ever merging. Only when the fruiting body forms do the paired nuclei finally fuse (karyogamy), and meiosis follows almost at once.

"The dikaryon is diploid" and four other fungal sentences to reject

n + n ≠ 2n. A dikaryotic cell has two haploid nuclei; a diploid cell has one nucleus with two chromosome sets. Alleles in a dikaryon are in separate nuclei and cannot recombine or be dominant over one another. If a question asks for the only diploid cell in the life cycle of a typical fungus, the answer is the zygote produced at karyogamy, and the dikaryon is the distractor placed there to catch you.

"Fungal cell walls contain cellulose." Chitin. Cellulose walls belong to plants and to the oomycetes, which is exactly why oomycetes are not fungi. If you remember only one molecule from this section, remember this one.

"Fungi are plants that have lost their chlorophyll." Fungi and animals are both opisthokonts — they share a common ancestor with a single posterior flagellum, retained today in the chytrid zoospore and in your sperm. Fungi store carbohydrate as glycogen, as you do, not as starch. They never had chlorophyll to lose.

"Fungi ingest their food." They absorb it, after digesting it externally. No fungus phagocytoses; that is what separates them from the amoebozoans they superficially resemble in nutrition.

"Lichens are a single organism." A lichen is a fungus (usually an ascomycete) farming a green alga or a cyanobacterium — or both, and often with a basidiomycete yeast in the cortex as well, a third partner only discovered in 2016. The fungus supplies structure, mineral capture and water retention; the photobiont supplies fixed carbon, and a cyanobacterial partner also fixes nitrogen. Lichens are pioneer colonisers of bare rock and, because they take up minerals directly from rainfall with no way of excluding pollutants, they are classic bioindicators of sulphur-dioxide air pollution.

What fungi do to the world, and to us

Decomposition. Fungi and bacteria are the principal decomposers, but fungi have a near-monopoly on one substrate that matters enormously: lignin, the aromatic polymer that stiffens wood. Only the white-rot basidiomycetes, with their lignin and manganese peroxidases, break it down efficiently. Without them, carbon locks up in undecayed wood — which is, on the leading account, exactly what happened in the Carboniferous and produced the coal seams. Every mineral nutrient a plant takes up has been through a decomposer, so fungi are the reason nutrient cycles close.

Mutualism. Around 80–90 % of plant species have mycorrhizae, and the relationship is old enough to be visible in 400-million-year-old fossils of the earliest land plants. The fungus's hyphae are far thinner than a root hair and reach a much larger volume of soil, so they deliver phosphate — the least mobile major nutrient — far more effectively than roots can. In exchange the plant gives up something like 10–20 % of its photosynthate. Treat this as the standard example of mutualism whenever the ecology block asks for one, and note the direct link to § 08 of the plant notes: colonisation of land was almost certainly a fungal-plant joint venture. Fungi also live inside insects and in ruminant guts, and the leaf-cutter ants farm them deliberately.

Pathogens. Fungal disease in humans is largely opportunistic, which is a statement about our immunity rather than about fungal aggression. Candida albicans is a normal commensal that causes thrush when the bacterial flora is suppressed by antibiotics or immunity falls; Aspergillus fumigatus is inhaled constantly by everyone and only causes invasive disease in the neutropenic; Cryptococcus neoformans causes meningitis when CD4 counts collapse; and Pneumocystis jirovecii — a fungus, reclassified from the protozoa, and treated with the antibacterial folate inhibitor co-trimoxazole — defines AIDS. The dermatophytes (Trichophyton and relatives) are the exception that infects the healthy, because they digest keratin and never go deeper than dead skin. In plants the picture reverses entirely: rusts, smuts and blights are among the most destructive pathogens in agriculture.

Go deeper · antifungals, and why they are so much harder to design than antibiotics

Selective toxicity is easy against a bacterium and hard against a fungus, for the reason this section opened with: a fungus is a eukaryote, so almost every target it offers, we have too. Its ribosomes are 80S, its polymerases are eukaryotic, its metabolism is ours. The entire antifungal pharmacopoeia is built on the handful of exceptions.

  • Ergosterol — the fungal membrane sterol, where ours is cholesterol. This is the single largest target. Azoles (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole) inhibit lanosterol 14-α-demethylase and block its synthesis; the enzyme is a cytochrome P450, which is why azoles interact with so many other drugs and why they inhibit human steroidogenesis at high dose. Polyenes (amphotericin B, nystatin) bind ergosterol directly and punch pores in the membrane — highly effective, and nephrotoxic precisely because the discrimination between ergosterol and cholesterol is imperfect.
  • The cell wall — the one structure we entirely lack. Echinocandins (caspofungin) inhibit β-1,3-glucan synthase, and are correspondingly well tolerated. The wall is the fungal equivalent of the peptidoglycan target, and it is the reason this class was such an advance.
  • Microtubules — griseofulvin binds fungal tubulin preferentially and disrupts mitosis; the margin is narrow, which is why it is confined to skin and nail infection.
  • Nucleic acids — flucytosine is taken up by a fungal-specific permease and deaminated by a fungal-specific cytosine deaminase into 5-fluorouracil. Human cells lack the enzyme, so the selectivity is in the activation rather than the target. Resistance develops fast, so it is used in combination.

A neat way to fix this in memory: the four classes map onto membrane sterol, wall glucan, tubulin and pyrimidine metabolism — and every one of them exists because of a small chemical difference between a fungal eukaryote and a human one. Compare with the antibacterial list in § 02, where whole structures (peptidoglycan, 70S ribosome, folate synthesis) are simply absent from us. The difficulty of antifungal chemotherapy is a direct measure of how closely related we are.

The fungal facts that carry marks

Chitin, ergosterol, absorption, glycogen, haploid. Those five words answer most of what is asked. Then the two spore counts — 8 in an ascus, 4 on a basidium — and the reason for the difference (one extra mitosis after meiosis in the ascus). Then n + n is not 2n. Then fungi are opisthokonts, closer to animals than plants. And finally the two ecological roles the paper genuinely rewards: fungi as the decomposers that alone degrade lignin, and mycorrhizae as the mutualism present in the great majority of land plants — the second of which reappears in the plant-nutrition note and is worth reading twice.
