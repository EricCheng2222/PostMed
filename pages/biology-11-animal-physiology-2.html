Biology · Note 11

Animal Physiology II — Nerve, Muscle and the Endocrine System

Note 10 dealt with the bulk transport problems — moving blood, gas, food and water. This note deals with control, and control in an animal is done twice over by the same underlying trick: a cell sets up a gradient at leisure, then spends it in a hurry. A neuron stockpiles Na⁺ outside and K⁺ inside and spends that battery in a millisecond to make a signal. A muscle fibre stockpiles Ca²⁺ inside the sarcoplasmic reticulum and spends it in a few tens of milliseconds to make a movement. An endocrine gland stockpiles hormone and spends it over hours, and because its signal travels in the blood it must be switched off by feedback rather than by a wire. Learn the gradient, learn what opens the gate, and learn what closes it again, and nerve, muscle and hormone stop being three separate syllabuses.

§ 01

The resting membrane potential

The ideaA living cell keeps different amounts of salt inside and outside itself. That difference is stored work, like water held behind a dam, and by itself it produces no voltage at all. The voltage appears the moment the cell opens one kind of doorway — a doorway that lets potassium out but will not let anything else follow it. A few potassium ions leave, each taking a positive charge with them and leaving an unaccompanied negative charge behind, and the inside of the cell goes slightly negative. The negativity then pulls on the remaining potassium and stops any more from leaving. The resting voltage is simply the point where that pull exactly cancels the urge to spread out. Everything else in this note is a variation on that one sentence.

Definition & formula

Equilibrium potential
For one ion, the membrane voltage at which the electrical force on it exactly balances its concentration gradient, so there is no net movement. It depends only on that ion's two concentrations and on temperature — never on how many channels are open.
EX = (61.5 / z) log₁₀ ( [X]out / [X]in ) mV, at 37 °C the Nernst equation（能斯特方程式）; 61.5 mV per tenfold gradient for a monovalent ion
Full definition, symbols and units
Membrane potential Vm
The potential of the cytosol measured against the extracellular fluid, taken as 0 V. A "resting potential of −70 mV" means the inside is 70 mV negative. Sign errors here cost more marks than any other single slip in this topic.
Driving force
Vm − EX. This, not the concentration gradient, decides which way an ion actually moves when its channel opens. If Vm > EX the ion leaves a cell (for a cation); if Vm < EX it enters.
Electrogenic pump
A transporter that moves net charge, so it contributes to Vm directly as well as by maintaining gradients. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase exports 3 Na⁺ for every 2 K⁺ imported, so each cycle removes one net positive charge — worth only about −3 to −5 mV directly.

Where the numbers come from

Four ions matter. Their gradients are built and held by pumps and carriers, and the direction of each gradient is worth knowing cold, because every ion channel in the rest of this note simply lets one of them run downhill.

Two features of that table deserve a second look. First, ENa is +62 mV and not +90 mV — a tenfold gradient gives exactly one 61.5 mV step, and Na⁺ is a tenfold gradient. Second, the Ca²⁺ gradient is by far the steepest, and it is steep because the cytosolic figure is 10⁻⁷ M, not 10⁻³ M. That four-order-of-magnitude hole is what lets a tiny absolute number of Ca²⁺ ions act as an unambiguous signal: nothing else in the cytosol changes its concentration a hundredfold in a millisecond. Hold onto that — it is the whole reason § 08 works.

Why the resting potential is near EK but never equal to it

At rest a neuron's membrane is studded with K⁺ leak channels (the two-pore-domain K2P family) and very few open Na⁺ channels — a permeability ratio of roughly PK : PNa = 1 : 0.04. Feed those numbers into the GHK equation and you get −68 mV, not −89 mV. The 21 mV discrepancy is the small Na⁺ leak: a trickle of Na⁺ entering constantly, dragging Vm a little way from EK towards ENa. The membrane potential is always a permeability-weighted tug-of-war, and it always sits nearest the equilibrium potential of whichever ion is most permeable at that instant. That single sentence predicts the entire action potential in § 02 without any new physics.

The pump does not "create" the resting potential

This is the most-repeated wrong sentence in the whole topic, and it appears in textbook prose often enough that it makes an excellent distractor. Blocking the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase with ouabain does not collapse Vm in a second; the resting potential decays over minutes to hours as the gradients slowly run down. The pump's direct electrogenic contribution is only a few millivolts. What the pump does is maintain the gradients — it is the generator that charges the battery, not the battery, and certainly not the wire.

Two corollaries examiners like. A red blood cell has the same Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and a similar gradient but a much lower K⁺ permeability, and rests near −10 mV. And a cell with no open channels at all has Vm = 0 no matter how steep its gradients are. Gradient plus selective permeability; neither alone will do.

Exam technique · any Nernst, GHK or driving-force question

  1. Sort the stem into one of three types in five seconds — two concentrations of one ion → Nernst. Several ions with permeabilities or a permeability ratio → GHK. A membrane potential and an equilibrium potential → driving force, and no logarithm is needed at all. Do not start computing before you know which of the three you are in; the three have different distractors.
  2. Run the Nernst chain in one fixed direction — 61.5 ÷ z, then log₁₀(out/in). Always out over in, never the other way, and the valence carries its sign. One tenfold gradient is exactly one 61.5 mV step, so Na⁺ at 150/15 must be +62 mV and nothing else; K⁺ at 5/140 is a 28-fold gradient, i.e. about 1.45 steps, hence −89 mV.
  3. Kill on sign before you compute anything — for the standard gradients, EK and ECl are negative and ENa and ECa are positive, always. An option offering a positive EK or a negative ENa is dead with no arithmetic. In hyperkalaemia EK must move up (less negative), because the outward gradient has shrunk — any option that makes it more negative has the log the wrong way round, and that is the single commonest slip in the topic.
  4. Direction of movement is Vm − EX, never the concentration gradient — for a cation, Vm above EX means it leaves, below means it enters. This one line answers "which way does the ion go" stems in ten seconds, and it is the same line that answers the hair cell in § 06.
  5. Three free eliminations that recur across the whole paper — "the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase generates the resting potential", "ouabain abolishes Vm within seconds", and "at rest the membrane is permeable only to K⁺". All three are lifted from real textbook prose, all three sound right, and all three are wrong. The third one is self-refuting: if it were true Vm would be exactly −89 mV, and the measured value is −70 mV.
  6. Where the time goes — a bare Nernst is a 30-second question and should never be left blank. A four-term GHK is worth the full 66 seconds. If you are asked for Vm and given only one ion's concentrations, you are almost certainly being asked for EX and not for Vm — reread the stem and look for the permeability ratio before you commit.
Worked example 1 — Nernst, GHK, and what hyperkalaemia does to a nerve

A neuron has [K⁺]o = 5 mM, [K⁺]i = 140 mM, [Na⁺]o = 150 mM, [Na⁺]i = 15 mM, and PK:PNa = 1:0.04 at rest. (a) Find EK and ENa. (b) Find Vm. (c) Plasma K⁺ now rises to 10 mM. Recompute, and predict the clinical effect.

  1. (a) EK = 61.5 × log₁₀(5/140) = 61.5 × (−1.447) = −89.0 mV.
  2. ENa = 61.5 × log₁₀(150/15) = 61.5 × 1 = +61.5 mV — a clean tenfold gradient, hence exactly one Nernst step.
  3. (b) Numerator = (1)(5) + (0.04)(150) = 5 + 6 = 11. Denominator = (1)(140) + (0.04)(15) = 140 + 0.6 = 140.6.
  4. Vm = 61.5 × log₁₀(11/140.6) = 61.5 × (−1.106) = −68.1 mV. Note the small Na⁺ leak has pulled Vm 21 mV away from EK despite carrying only 4 % of the permeability — because the driving force on Na⁺ (−68 − 62 = −130 mV) is huge.
  5. (c) EK = 61.5 × log₁₀(10/140) = −70.5 mV: the gradient is halved, so EK climbs by one 61.5 × log₁₀ 2 = 18.5 mV step.
  6. New numerator = 10 + 6 = 16, denominator unchanged at 140.6, so Vm = 61.5 × log₁₀(16/140.6) = −58.1 mV.
  7. Reading it. The cell has depolarised by 10 mV, from −68 to −58 mV, and threshold is −55 mV. Excitable tissue therefore becomes transiently hyperexcitable — then, because a sustained depolarisation keeps voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in the inactivated state (§ 02), it becomes inexcitable. That biphasic story is exactly the ECG evolution of hyperkalaemia: peaked T waves first, then a widening QRS, then arrest.

Go deeper · why the Nernst equation alone can never give you Vm

The Nernst equation answers a question about one ion in isolation: at what voltage would this ion stop moving? A real membrane is permeable to several ions at once, and no single voltage can satisfy all of them simultaneously — at −68 mV, K⁺ is still leaking out (because −68 > −89) and Na⁺ is still leaking in (because −68 < +62). The resting cell is therefore not at equilibrium; it is at a steady state, in which the outward K⁺ current exactly cancels the inward Na⁺ current so that net current is zero even though net ion movement is not. The pump runs continuously to pay for it.

That distinction has teeth. "The resting membrane potential is the equilibrium potential of potassium" is the kind of sentence that reads well and is false; the honest version is "the resting potential lies close to EK because K⁺ dominates resting permeability, but it is a steady state, not an equilibrium, and it costs ATP indefinitely." Roughly 20–25 % of a typical resting cell's ATP budget goes on the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase alone; in neurons, which must also repay every spike and every synaptic current, the share is higher still, and in the brain as a whole ion pumping is the single largest line of the energy bill.

GHK also explains why clinical medicine worries about extracellular K⁺ and not about extracellular Na⁺. Halving [Na⁺]o from 150 to 75 mM takes the numerator from 11 to 8 and Vm from −68.1 to −76.6 mV — a shift of 8.5 mV. Doubling [K⁺]o from 5 to 10 mM takes the numerator from 11 to 16 and Vm to −58.1 mV — a shift of 10.0 mV, and in the dangerous direction, towards threshold. Per unit of relative change the two ions are comparable; what is not comparable is how far each is allowed to move. Plasma Na⁺ is held within a few percent and never halves in a living patient, whereas plasma K⁺ genuinely doubles in renal failure. The physics is symmetrical; the physiology is not, and that is why it is potassium that stops hearts.

§ 02

The action potential

The ideaAn action potential is what happens when a cell's own voltage is allowed to control its own doors. Sodium doors open when the cell becomes less negative — and sodium coming in makes the cell less negative still, which opens more doors. That is a runaway, and once it starts nothing can stop it half-way; this is why the signal is always the same size no matter how hard you push. Two things then end it. The sodium doors have a second, slower latch that swings shut and cannot reopen until the voltage has gone back down, and the potassium doors, which are late and slow, finally open and let the cell fall back past where it started. The slow latch is also why a nerve cannot be re-fired instantly, and why the signal travels forwards and never backwards.

Definition & key facts

Action potential（動作電位）
A stereotyped, self-regenerating, all-or-none reversal of membrane potential produced by sequential voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ conductances. In a mammalian neuron it lasts 1–2 ms and swings from about −70 mV to about +35 mV and back.
depolarise → gNa↑ → depolarise more (positive feedback, above threshold only) the one loop that makes the spike; everything else is how it is switched off
Full definition, symbols and units
Threshold
≈ −55 mV. Not a magic voltage but a tipping point: the Vm at which inward Na⁺ current first exceeds outward K⁺ and leak current, so the positive feedback can run without help.
All-or-none
Above threshold, amplitude and duration are set by the ion gradients and the channels, not by the stimulus. A stimulus twice as strong gives the same spike — information about intensity is carried by frequency and by how many neurons fire, never by amplitude.
Absolute refractory period
≈ 1 ms. No stimulus of any size can fire a second spike, because essentially every voltage-gated Na⁺ channel is inactivated.
Relative refractory period
≈ 2–4 ms after that. A stronger-than-normal stimulus can fire a spike: some Na⁺ channels have recovered, but K⁺ conductance is still elevated and the cell is hyperpolarised.
Afterhyperpolarisation (undershoot)
Vm transiently goes below rest, towards EK, because the slow K⁺ channels are still open after Vm has returned to −70 mV. Its size and duration set the maximum sustainable firing rate.

Two gates on one channel — the fact that explains almost everything

The voltage-gated Na⁺ channel has two independent gates in series, and a channel conducts only when both are open. The activation gate is shut at rest and opens fast (tens of microseconds) on depolarisation. The inactivation gate is open at rest and closes slowly (about a millisecond) on depolarisation — a tethered intracellular loop that swings into the pore like a ball on a chain. Because one is fast-opening and the other is slow-closing, depolarisation produces a brief window of conduction and then shuts the channel down.

Crucially, the inactivation gate does not reopen when the depolarising stimulus stops. It reopens only when the membrane has actually repolarised. That single asymmetry generates four separate examinable facts at once, and if you can derive them you never need to memorise them.

Read it sideways and you have unidirectional propagation: the patch of membrane behind the advancing spike is refractory, so the local currents that spread in both directions can only regenerate a spike in front. Do not build a mnemonic for that table — it has one moving part

Five rows, three columns of gate states: it looks like something to chant, and it is not. Every cell in it follows from one asymmetry you already have — the activation gate is fast and follows Vm up and down; the inactivation gate is slow, closes on depolarisation, and reopens only after the membrane has actually repolarised. Put a finger on any point of the trace, ask "is Vm depolarised, and has it been depolarised for a millisecond yet?", and both gate states drop out. Absolute refractory = inactivation gates shut. Relative refractory = some reset, but K⁺ conductance is still high and Vm is further from threshold. Unidirectional propagation = the membrane behind the spike is still in the first of those two states. A chant for this would be a second thing to remember and would decay into noise within a week; the asymmetry will not, because you can rebuild it from the word "inactivation" itself.

Five sentences that sound right and are not

"Repolarisation is caused by the K⁺ channels opening." Half true. It is caused by gNa collapsing (inactivation) and gK rising. Block K⁺ channels with TEA and the spike still repolarises, just far more slowly.

"The Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores the resting potential after each spike." No — repolarisation is done entirely by ion channels, in about a millisecond. The pump restores the gradients, over seconds to minutes, and is not needed for any individual spike (see worked example 2).

"A stronger stimulus gives a bigger action potential." Never, above threshold. It gives a higher frequency, and recruits more axons.

"The refractory period is caused by the K⁺ channels being open." The relative refractory period is partly that. The absolute one is Na⁺ inactivation, full stop.

"During the overshoot the membrane is permeable only to Na⁺." If it were, Vm would reach +62 mV. It peaks around +30 to +40 mV because PNa/PK is finite — about 20:1 at its maximum, not infinite — and because the spike is cut short before Vm can settle: even that 20:1 ratio has a GHK ceiling of only ≈+51 mV, and Na⁺ inactivation plus the rising gK turn the trace around well below it.

Worked example 2 — how many ions actually move during one spike?

Specific membrane capacitance is 1 µF·cm⁻². An action potential swings Vm by 100 mV. (a) How much charge crosses 1 cm² of membrane? (b) In an unmyelinated axon 1 µm in diameter, by how much does [Na⁺]i rise, given [Na⁺]i = 15 mM? (c) What follows for the role of the pump?

  1. (a) Q = C ΔV = (1×10⁻⁶ F·cm⁻²)(0.100 V) = 1×10⁻⁷ C·cm⁻².
  2. In moles of monovalent ion: n = Q/F = 1×10⁻⁷ / 96 485 = 1.04×10⁻¹² mol·cm⁻², i.e. about 1 picomole per square centimetre.
  3. (b) A cylinder of radius r has surface-to-volume ratio 2/r. With r = 0.5 µm = 0.5×10⁻⁴ cm, that is 4×10⁴ cm⁻¹.
  4. Δ[Na⁺]i = (1.04×10⁻¹² mol·cm⁻²)(4×10⁴ cm⁻¹) = 4.15×10⁻⁸ mol·cm⁻³ = 4.15×10⁻⁵ M = 41.5 µM.
  5. As a fraction of the 15 mM already inside: 41.5 µM / 15 000 µM = 0.28 %, or about one part in 360.
  6. (c) So a single spike changes the intracellular composition by a quarter of one percent — and in a squid giant axon (500 µm across, 500× the volume per unit surface) by about one part in 180 000. The gradients are enormous reservoirs; the spike is a rounding error on them.

This is why an axon poisoned with ouabain still fires for tens of thousands of spikes before failing, and why the pump is a housekeeper, not a participant. It also tells you the metabolic price: restoring that Na⁺ costs n/3 = 0.35 pmol of ATP per cm² per spike, because the pump exports three Na⁺ per ATP.

The three states, not two

A voltage-gated Na⁺ channel is closed-but-available, open, or inactivated — and "inactivated" is not the same as "closed". Closed channels can open; inactivated ones cannot, until repolarisation resets them. Half the traps in this section are built on collapsing three states into two.

Drugs and toxins that make the point

Tetrodotoxin (pufferfish, 河豚毒素) plugs the outer mouth of the voltage-gated Na⁺ channel — no upstroke, flaccid paralysis, sensation lost. Tetraethylammonium blocks delayed-rectifier K⁺ channels — the spike still fires but repolarises slowly and broadens. Local anaesthetics such as lidocaine bind the inactivated state from the inside, so they preferentially silence axons that are firing fast — which is why they work better on pain than on touch, and why they are described as use-dependent. Class I antiarrhythmics exploit the same state-dependence in cardiac muscle. Notice that all three stories are consequences of the two-gate model rather than extra facts.

§ 03

Conduction, myelin and saltatory propagation

The ideaAn axon is a leaky garden hose full of salt water. Push charge in at one end and it spreads sideways for a short distance and then dribbles out through the wall, dying away with distance. A bare axon deals with this by regenerating the signal at every single point along its length, which works but is slow, because every point has to be charged up from scratch. The alternative is to wrap most of the axon in fat so that nothing leaks out and very little charge is needed to change the voltage, and to leave bare patches at intervals where the signal is boosted back to full strength. The signal does not literally jump; it slides quickly and passively under the insulation and is rebuilt only at the gaps. That is why a wrapped nerve fibre a hundredth of a millimetre wide beats a bare one as thick as your little finger.

Definition & formula

Length constant λ（空間常數）
The distance over which a passively spreading (electrotonic) voltage falls to 1/e — about 37 % — of its starting value. Bigger λ means the signal reaches further before it needs regenerating, which means fewer regeneration stops and faster conduction.
V(x) = V₀ e−x/λ, λ = rm/ri passive decay along a cable; raise membrane resistance or lower axial resistance and λ grows
Full definition, symbols and units
Time constant τ
τ = rmcm: how long the membrane takes to charge to 63 % of its final voltage. A smaller τ is faster. Myelin cuts cm about a hundredfold, because capacitance falls as the insulator gets thicker — but it raises rm by a similar factor, so the internodal τ itself barely moves. The gain from myelin is the smaller charge needed at the next node, and the longer λ, not a shorter τ.
Saltatory conduction（跳躍式傳導）
Regeneration of the action potential only at the nodes of Ranvier, with fast passive spread under each internode. From saltare, to leap. The regeneration is discontinuous; the current flow is not.

What myelin actually buys

Myelin is stacked plasma membrane — up to a hundred wraps of it — laid down by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system (one cell makes one internode) and by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system (one cell can myelinate up to about fifty internodes on different axons). It changes two numbers, and both of them in the helpful direction:

  • Membrane resistance goes up, roughly a hundredfold, because the wraps are almost devoid of ion channels and the current has a hundred membranes in series to cross. Less charge leaks out, so λ = √(rm/ri) rises by about √100 = 10. A signal that died within half a millimetre now reaches five.
  • Membrane capacitance goes down, also roughly a hundredfold, because capacitance is inversely proportional to the thickness of the insulator. Since Q = CΔV, a hundredfold smaller C means a hundredth of the charge is needed to swing the voltage at the next node — so the next node reaches threshold far sooner.

Add the third, structural, contribution — Na⁺ channels are not spread thinly along the whole axon but concentrated at the nodes, where they are needed — and the result is that a 20 µm myelinated fibre conducts at about 120 m·s⁻¹. To match that with a bare axon you would scale up from the squid giant axon (500 µm, ≈25 m·s⁻¹) using v ∝ √d: you would need 500 × (120/25)² ≈ 11 500 µm, an axon 1.2 cm across and about 3×10⁵ times the cross-section. There is not room in a vertebrate for more than a handful of those, which is why the squid runs its escape response on a single pair of giant axons and you have millions of myelinated ones.

"The impulse jumps from node to node" — what that sentence hides

It is a useful image and a misleading one. Nothing jumps, and nothing is instantaneous. Current flows continuously through the axoplasm and back across the membrane; what is discontinuous is the regeneration. Two consequences that the jumping image obscures and that examiners test. First, if the passive signal arriving at a node has fallen below threshold, conduction fails completely — there is no half-speed fallback, which is why demyelination produces conduction block, not merely slowing. Second, saltatory conduction is not only faster but cheaper: Na⁺ enters only at the nodes, so the pump has far less to undo. Speed and metabolic economy come from the same modification.

Demyelinating disease, mapped onto the mechanism

Multiple sclerosis destroys oligodendrocytes and therefore CNS myelin — optic neuritis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, sensory and motor deficits disseminated in space and time. Guillain–Barré syndrome attacks Schwann-cell myelin and therefore the PNS — ascending flaccid paralysis with lost reflexes, often after a Campylobacter infection. Note the electrophysiology in each: a demyelinated internode has low rm and high cm, so λ collapses and the charge required rises — the signal slows, then fails. That is why nerve conduction studies in these diseases show slowed velocity and conduction block, whereas axonal loss (as in diabetic neuropathy) shows reduced amplitude with velocity preserved. One test, two entirely different signatures, both derivable from cable theory.

Exam technique · nerve conduction studies, and fibre-type stems

  1. Two stimulation sites means one formula, always — v = Δdistance / Δlatency. Write both subtractions on the paper before you look at the options, because the options are designed to catch you mid-thought.
  2. The two distractors are pre-built, so use them as a check — the examiner will offer distance ÷ proximal latency and distance ÷ distal latency. With 25.0 cm, 3.5 ms and 11.0 ms those are 22.7 and 71.4 m·s⁻¹, straddling the correct 33.3. If your answer sits between two offered options that are exactly those two quotients, you have almost certainly got it right.
  3. Recognition rule for the interpretation half — slowed velocity, with or without conduction block → demyelinating (MS if the lesion is central, because oligodendrocytes; Guillain–Barré if peripheral, because Schwann cells). Reduced amplitude with velocity preserved → axonal. Normal motor conduction is above 50 m·s⁻¹, so anything in the thirties is abnormal and you may say so without hedging.
  4. Kill on the velocity–diameter rule without doing physiology — myelinated v ≈ 6 × d(µm), unmyelinated v ∝ √d and never more than about 2 m·s⁻¹ in a real C fibre. An option that hands an unmyelinated fibre 30 m·s⁻¹, or a 1 µm fibre 100 m·s⁻¹, is dead on arithmetic alone. Going backwards works too: 62.5 m·s⁻¹ implies a fibre of about 10 µm.
  5. There is no mnemonic for the fibre table and you do not need one — it is already sorted. Aα → Aβ → Aδ → B → C runs monotonically down in diameter, down in myelination and down in velocity, in Greek-then-Latin alphabetical order. The only genuinely arbitrary content is the modality split at the bottom: Aδ carries fast sharp localised pain and cold; C carries slow dull aching pain, warmth and itch. Learn that pair and generate the rest.
  6. Anything about block order is the size principle in reverse — thin and unmyelinated fibres are blocked first by a local anaesthetic and are also the last to be affected by compression. So pain goes before touch and touch before motor power, and an option that has motor power failing first is wrong for anaesthesia (though right for a compressive lesion, which is the discrimination the stem is usually testing).
Worked example 3 — a nerve conduction study, and the trap inside it

The median nerve is stimulated at the wrist; the muscle response in the thenar eminence begins 3.5 ms later. Stimulating again at the elbow, 25.0 cm proximal, the response begins 11.0 ms later. (a) What is the forearm conduction velocity? (b) Why must you use the two latencies rather than one? (c) What if the same 25 cm gave a difference of 4.0 ms instead?

  1. (a) Latency difference = 11.0 − 3.5 = 7.5 ms.
  2. v = 0.250 m / 7.5×10⁻³ s = 33.3 m·s⁻¹.
  3. Normal for a large myelinated motor fibre is > 50 m·s⁻¹, so 33 m·s⁻¹ is markedly slow — a demyelinating pattern.
  4. (b) Because the distal latency is not pure axonal conduction: it also contains the neuromuscular delay (0.5–1 ms) and conduction through the fine terminal branches.   5. (c) 0.250 / 4.0×10⁻³ = 62.5 m·s⁻¹ — comfortably normal. Back-calculating with v ≈ 6d, that corresponds to a fibre of about 62.5/6 ≈ 10 µm.

Go deeper · the cable equation, and why bigger is faster in both kinds of axon

Treat the axon as a leaky cable. Current injected at one point can either flow along the axoplasm (resistance ri per unit length) or leak out across the membrane (resistance rm per unit length). The steady-state solution is the exponential in the defbox, and the length constant λ = √(rm/ri) is the distance at which those two routes are balanced. Numerically: at x = λ, V = 0.368 V₀; at 2λ, 0.135 V₀; at 3λ, 0.050 V₀. A signal is essentially gone after three length constants.

Now scale the diameter. Axial resistance falls with cross-sectional area, so ri ∝ 1/d². Membrane resistance falls with circumference, so rm ∝ 1/d. Then λ ∝ √(d²/d) = √d — which is exactly the observed v ∝ √d for unmyelinated fibres. Fattening a bare axon works, but with brutally diminishing returns: four times the diameter, sixteen times the volume and metabolic cost, twice the speed.

Myelin sidesteps the scaling law entirely by changing rm and cm instead of d. Because the number of internodes per unit length falls as the fibre gets thicker (internode length ≈ 100 × diameter), velocity in a myelinated fibre ends up linear in diameter rather than square-root. There is an optimum g-ratio — axon diameter divided by total fibre diameter — of about 0.6–0.7: thinner myelin leaks, thicker myelin wastes space that could have been axon. Evolution found it; so did the engineers who specify coaxial cable.

The time constant matters for a different reason. τ = rmcm sets how long a synaptic potential lingers, and therefore how wide the window for temporal summation is (§ 04). A neuron with τ = 20 ms can add up inputs arriving over 20 ms; one with τ = 2 ms is a coincidence detector. Auditory brainstem neurons that compute interaural time differences of tens of microseconds have famously short time constants — the cable properties are not incidental to the computation, they are the computation.

§ 04

The synapse: transmission and integration

The ideaWhere one neuron meets the next there is usually a gap, and electricity does not cross gaps. So the arriving electrical signal is converted into a puff of chemical, the chemical drifts across, and on the far side it is converted back into electricity. That sounds wasteful — it costs half a millisecond and a great deal of machinery — but it buys three things a wire cannot: the receiving cell can be made less likely to fire as easily as more likely, the strength of the connection can be turned up or down and remembered, and one cell can be made to listen to thousands of others at once and add them all up. A neuron is not a relay. It is a device that sums a few thousand small yes and no votes and fires only if the total, measured at one specific spot, crosses a line.

Definition & key facts

Chemical synapse
A junction at which a presynaptic action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, and the resulting Ca²⁺ influx triggers exocytosis of neurotransmitter into a 20–40 nm cleft. The transmitter binds postsynaptic receptors and changes their ionic conductance. Unidirectional, with a 0.5–1 ms delay.
AP → Ca²⁺ in → vesicle fusion → transmitter → receptor → EPSP or IPSP Ca²⁺ is the electrical-to-chemical converter; every step is a drug target
Full definition, symbols and units
EPSP / IPSP
Excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials: graded, local, decremental depolarisations or hyperpolarisations, typically 0.2–1 mV in a cortical neuron, that spread passively to the axon hillock. They are not all-or-none and they summate — which action potentials cannot.
Reversal potential
The Vm at which a given synaptic current is zero. For a mixed Na⁺/K⁺ cation channel (nicotinic, AMPA) it is ≈ 0 mV; for a Cl⁻ channel (GABAA, glycine) ≈ −70 mV; for a K⁺ channel (GABAB) ≈ −90 mV. Whether a synapse is "excitatory" is a statement about its reversal potential relative to threshold, not about the transmitter.
Temporal summation
One synapse firing repeatedly, faster than the postsynaptic potential decays (time constant τ).
Spatial summation
Many synapses at different sites firing together, their potentials converging on the axon hillock.
Axon hillock / initial segment
The trigger zone: the highest density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in the neuron, hence the lowest threshold. Integration is decided there, not at the synapse and not at the soma.

The seven steps, and what breaks each one

Transmission is worth learning as a numbered chain, because every clinically important toxin and drug in this topic knocks out exactly one link, and questions are built by naming the lesion and asking for the step.

  1. The action potential invades the terminal and depolarises it. Blocked by tetrodotoxin, local anaesthetics.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open (P/Q- and N-type, clustered at the active zone) and Ca²⁺ floods in down its 20 000-fold gradient. Blocked by Mg²⁺ (which competes), and by the autoantibodies of Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome.
  3. Ca²⁺ binds synaptotagmin, the vesicle's calcium sensor, which forces the SNARE complex (synaptobrevin/VAMP on the vesicle; syntaxin and SNAP-25 on the membrane) to zip the two membranes together. Cleaved by botulinum toxin — flaccid paralysis and the reason Botox works — and by tetanus toxin, which does the same thing but selectively in inhibitory spinal interneurons, removing inhibition and causing spastic paralysis. Same enzymatic mechanism, opposite clinical picture, decided entirely by which neuron the toxin gets into.
  4. The vesicle fuses and releases a fixed packet — a quantum, about 5000–10 000 ACh molecules. Release is quantal, not continuous: this is why postsynaptic responses come in integer multiples of a unit size.
  5. Transmitter diffuses 20–40 nm, which takes microseconds. The diffusion is not the delay; the 0.5–1 ms synaptic delay is almost entirely steps 2 and 3.
  6. Receptors bind it. Ionotropic receptors are the channel itself — response in under a millisecond. Metabotropic receptors are GPCRs — response in tens of milliseconds to seconds, but amplified and longer-lasting (§ 11). Blocked by curare and by the anti-AChR antibodies of myasthenia gravis.
  7. The signal is terminated. Three mechanisms — enzymatic destruction, reuptake, diffusion — and knowing which applies to which transmitter is a routine exam point (see the warning below).

Three things about the synapse that textbook prose regularly gets loose

"Neurotransmitters are broken down by enzymes in the cleft." That is true of acetylcholine, and essentially of acetylcholine alone. Glutamate, GABA, dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin are cleared by reuptake transporters (EAAT, GAT, DAT, NET, SERT) into the terminal or into astrocytes, and only then metabolised — which is precisely why the major antidepressants are reuptake inhibitors, and why cocaine and amphetamine act on transporters. ACh is the exception because it must be cleared in under a millisecond at a junction with a huge safety factor.

"GABA is inhibitory." GABA is a molecule; inhibition is a consequence of a reversal potential. GABAA opens a Cl⁻ channel with ECl ≈ −70 mV, which is roughly the resting potential — so the resulting IPSP may be tiny or absent, and the inhibition works mainly by shunting: the extra conductance short-circuits incoming excitatory current, so other EPSPs become smaller. Worse for the naive version: in immature neurons the NKCC1 transporter keeps [Cl⁻]i high, ECl is about −45 mV, and GABA is depolarising and excitatory. This is why neonatal seizures respond poorly to benzodiazepines.

"Electrical synapses are primitive." They are gap junctions (connexons, 3.5 nm gap), they are bidirectional, they have essentially zero delay, and vertebrates use them wherever synchrony matters more than computation — cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, the retina, and networks of inhibitory interneurons in mammalian cortex. What they cannot do is invert a signal, amplify it, or change their own strength quickly. That is the real trade.

Exam technique · putting a toxin, a drug or an antibody on the seven-step chain

  1. Name the step before you read a single option — the stem gives you a lesion and wants the step, or gives you a step and wants the lesion. Step 1 (AP invades the terminal): tetrodotoxin, local anaesthetics. Step 2 (Ca²⁺ channels): Mg²⁺, Lambert–Eaton. Step 3 (SNARE zipping): botulinum, tetanus. Step 6 (receptor): curare, myasthenia gravis. Step 7 (termination): anticholinesterases and organophosphates. Five labels on a chain you already have — this converts a 60-second question into a 15-second one.
  2. Presynaptic versus postsynaptic settles two things at once — if the lesion is presynaptic (botulism, Lambert–Eaton) then all cholinergic release fails, so the stem will carry autonomic signs: dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation. If it is postsynaptic against the muscle nicotinic receptor (myasthenia, curare) the autonomic system is spared entirely. A stem that mentions dry mouth has already told you the answer is presynaptic.
  3. The exercise direction is derived, not memorised — repeated firing loads Ca²⁺ into the terminal, so a presynaptic Ca²⁺-channel lesion gets better with use (Lambert–Eaton, incremental response) while a receptor lesion eats through its safety factor and gets worse (myasthenia, decremental response, ptosis worst in the evening). Rebuild it from that sentence rather than learning the pair — the pair is easy to invert under pressure and the mechanism is not.
  4. Free elimination on clearance — only acetylcholine is destroyed by an enzyme sitting in the cleft. Any option that has glutamate, GABA, dopamine, noradrenaline or serotonin "broken down by enzymes in the synaptic cleft" is dead on sight; they are taken back up by transporters and metabolised afterwards. Nitric oxide is cleared by neither route — it just decays. With five options and negative marking, one kill like this already makes answering better than leaving it blank.
  5. Free elimination on "excitatory" and "inhibitory" — those are properties of a reversal potential, not of a molecule. If the stem is about immature neurons, about shunting inhibition, or about a chloride gradient, then an option that treats "GABA" and "inhibitory" as synonyms is the trap, not the answer.
  6. Sanity-check summation arithmetic two ways — number of excitatory inputs = (depolarisation needed ÷ EPSP size), then add one extra EPSP for each IPSP of equal size. Your answer must be a whole number and must be larger than the no-inhibition figure. With 15 mV, 0.5 mV EPSPs and sixteen IPSPs that is 30 → 46. If you get 30 you ignored the inhibition, and if you get 62 you charged two EPSPs per IPSP instead of one — both of those are standard offered options.

Integration: how a neuron makes a decision

A cortical pyramidal neuron receives on the order of ten thousand synapses. A single one produces an EPSP of perhaps 0.5 mV at the soma, and the gap from rest (−70 mV) to threshold (−55 mV) is 15 mV. So roughly thirty simultaneous excitatory inputs are needed — or fewer inputs firing repeatedly within one membrane time constant. That arithmetic is the whole of spatial and temporal summation, and it is why synapse position matters: an EPSP generated on a distal dendrite decays as e−x/λ on its way to the hillock, so a distal synapse is worth less than a perisomatic one. Inhibitory synapses exploit this ruthlessly — they cluster on the soma and the axon initial segment, where a shunt can veto everything arriving from the entire dendritic tree at once.

Worked example 4 — summation arithmetic, and why myasthenia gravis is fatigable

(a) A neuron rests at −70 mV, threshold −55 mV. Each excitatory synapse gives a 0.5 mV EPSP and each inhibitory synapse a −0.5 mV IPSP at the hillock. Sixteen inhibitory synapses are active. How many excitatory synapses must fire together to reach threshold? (b) A muscle fibre rests at −90 mV with threshold −60 mV; a normal nerve impulse produces an end-plate potential that would depolarise it by 90 mV. What fraction of acetylcholine receptors must be lost before the fibre fails to fire, and why does the weakness get worse with repeated use?

  1. (a) Depolarisation needed: −55 − (−70) = 15 mV.
  2. Net: 0.5n − (16)(0.5) = 15, so 0.5n = 15 + 8 = 23 and n = 46. Without the inhibition it would have been 30 — the sixteen IPSPs cost sixteen extra EPSPs, exactly one for one, because summation is linear to this approximation.
  3. (b) Depolarisation needed at the end-plate: −60 − (−90) = 30 mV. Normal EPP drive is 90 mV, so the safety factor is 90/30 = 3.0 — the junction delivers three times the signal it needs. Transmission is meant to be foolproof, unlike a CNS synapse.
  4. EPP amplitude is proportional to the number of functioning receptors. Failure occurs when 90 × (1 − f) < 30, i.e. f > 1 − 30/90 = 66.7 %. Two-thirds of receptors must be lost before a single twitch fails, which is why myasthenia gravis is often advanced before it is noticed.
  5. Now the fatigability. Normal terminals show physiological run-down: during repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz the readily releasable vesicle pool depletes and quantal content falls by roughly 30 % over the first few impulses. In a healthy junction that takes the EPP from 90 to 63 mV — still far above 30, so nothing happens.
  6. In a patient who has lost 60 % of receptors, the first EPP is 90 × 0.4 = 36 mV — still suprathreshold, so the first contraction is normal. After 30 % run-down it is 36 × 0.7 = 25.2 mV, now below 30 mV, and that fibre stops responding. The safety factor was consuming the run-down; remove it and the run-down becomes visible. That is exactly the decremental response on repetitive nerve stimulation, and exactly why ptosis worsens through the day.

Contrast Lambert–Eaton, where antibodies hit the presynaptic Ca²⁺ channel: quantal content starts low, but repeated firing lets Ca²⁺ accumulate in the terminal, so strength improves with exercise — an incremental response. Same junction, opposite direction, and the mechanism tells you which is which without memorising the pair.

Where the marks are in this section

Three distinctions carry most of them. Graded versus all-or-none — a postsynaptic potential is graded, decremental and summable; an action potential is none of those, and the switch between the two regimes happens at the axon hillock. Ionotropic versus metabotropic — speed and duration follow directly from whether the receptor is the channel. And presynaptic versus postsynaptic lesion — botulism and Lambert–Eaton are presynaptic (so autonomic symptoms such as dry mouth appear, because all cholinergic release is affected), myasthenia gravis and curare are postsynaptic (so autonomic function is spared, since the antibody is against the nicotinic receptor of muscle). Notice how much clinical medicine falls out of one diagram.

§ 05

How the nervous system is organised

The ideaThe whole nervous system is built out of one repeated shape: something senses, something decides, something acts. In the simplest version the deciding is done by a single junction in the spinal cord and the whole loop takes a fraction of a second — that is a reflex, and it happens whether the brain approves or not. Everything more elaborate is that same loop with more deciding stuffed into the middle, and with a second, parallel set of wires that controls the organs you never think about. The parallel set comes in two flavours that generally oppose each other, and almost every drug in a pharmacy is either imitating or blocking one of them.

Start from one design decision and most of the table falls out. The adrenal medulla is innervated by a preganglionic fibre directly, with no ganglion, because the chromaffin cells are modified postganglionic neurons — they release ≈80 % adrenaline and ≈20 % noradrenaline into the blood.

The four cranial nerves that carry parasympathetic fibres, and what each one is for

Our own hook rather than a standard one, but the rhyme fixes the order and every verb names an organ you can picture. 3 sees = CN III, the oculomotor nerve — pupil constriction and accommodation, which are precisely the two things you do in order to look at something. 7 weeps = CN VII, facial — the lacrimal gland, tears. 9 wets = CN IX, glossopharyngeal — the parotid gland, saliva. 10 keeps house = CN X, vagus — the thoracic and abdominal viscera, the whole of rest-and-digest housekeeping. Everything below that, the pelvic viscera, is handed to the sacral outflow at S2–S4. Four digits, 3-7-9-10, and one sacral range. Two things it does not cover. CN VII also supplies the submandibular and sublingual glands, so weeps is only the discriminator against IX (which has the parotid), not the whole of VII's job — if a stem asks about submandibular secretion the answer is VII, not IX. And it says nothing about the sympathetic side, which needs no hook at all: "thoracolumbar" already is the answer, T1–L2. The two rhymes that circulate everywhere for the same territory are "S2, 3 and 4 keep the penis off the floor" and "Point and Shoot" — Parasympathetic = Point (erection), Sympathetic = Shoot (emission and ejaculation) — and that second one is worth having, because it is exactly the note's example of the two divisions cooperating rather than opposing.

Two autonomic facts that are almost always the answer

First: every preganglionic neuron in both divisions releases acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors. Students reliably associate "sympathetic" with "noradrenaline" and then get caught by a question about the ganglion. Second: most organs receive both divisions, but not all — most blood vessels, sweat glands, piloerector muscles, the adrenal medulla, the spleen and the kidney get sympathetic innervation only, so their tone is set by varying sympathetic firing rather than by opposition. That is why "the two divisions always oppose each other" is a sentence to reject: in the male sexual response, parasympathetic activity produces erection and sympathetic activity produces emission — a genuinely cooperative sequence, not an opposition.

One more structural point worth carrying. The enteric nervous system — the myenteric (Auerbach) and submucosal (Meissner) plexuses — contains on the order of 10⁸ neurons and will run peristalsis with the vagus cut. It is sometimes called a third autonomic division for that reason, and it is the reason bowel function survives a spinal cord transection when bladder function does not.

§ 06

The special senses: the eye and the ear

The ideaA sense organ has exactly one job: turn some form of energy the nervous system cannot read — light, air pressure, a chemical, a stretch — into the only currency it can, which is a change in membrane voltage. Every receptor in the body is therefore a transducer, and every one of them works by opening or closing an ion channel, either directly by mechanical force or indirectly through a second messenger. The eye and the ear are worth the most attention because they solve the same problem in opposite ways: the ear opens channels by literally pulling them, in microseconds, while the eye runs an amplifying chemical cascade that is slower but can register a single photon.

The eye — optics first, then chemistry

Light is bent almost entirely at the air–cornea interface, because that is where the refractive index changes most (1.00 to 1.38). The lens contributes only about a third of the total power — but it is the third that can be changed, and that is what makes it interesting. Accommodation for near vision works by a mechanism that reads backwards to almost everyone the first time:

  1. The lens is elastic and its natural resting shape is round — high power.
  2. At rest the ciliary muscle is relaxed, the ciliary ring is wide, the zonular fibres are taut, and they pull the lens flat — low power, focused at infinity.
  3. To see something near, the parasympathetic supply (CN III, Edinger–Westphal nucleus, muscarinic) makes the circular ciliary muscle contract. Contracting a sphincter makes its ring smaller, which moves the attachment points inwards, which slackens the zonules and lets the lens spring back into its round, high-power shape.

So: ciliary muscle contracts → zonules relax → lens rounds → power increases. The classic wrong answer says the ciliary muscle contracts to pull the lens flat, which sounds mechanical and sensible and gets the sign of every step wrong. The near reflex triad is accommodation + convergence + pupillary constriction, all three carried by CN III — but only two of them parasympathetically: accommodation and constriction run through the Edinger–Westphal parasympathetic outflow, whereas convergence is ordinary somatic motor supply to the medial recti. A distractor that calls convergence parasympathetic is using a real distinction.

Presbyopia then needs no new mechanism: the lens protein hardens with age, so it can no longer spring round even when the zonules slacken. The ciliary muscle is fine. This is why presbyopia is universal and why it needs reading glasses rather than a change in the distance prescription.

Worked example 5 — refractive errors in dioptres

(a) A 50-year-old can focus no closer than 50 cm and wants to read at 25 cm. What reading addition is needed? (b) A myope's far point is 20 cm. What distance correction is needed, and why is the sign negative? (c) A 20-year-old's near point is 10 cm — what is her amplitude of accommodation?

  1. (a) Focusing at 25 cm requires 1/0.25 = 4.0 D of total near power beyond the distance prescription; she can supply 1/0.50 = 2.0 D.
  2. Addition needed = 4.0 − 2.0 = +2.0 D. A convex (positive, converging) lens, because we are adding the converging power the lens can no longer supply.
  3. (b) A myopic eye is too powerful for its length, so parallel light focuses in front of the retina. The far point — the furthest point in focus — is 0.20 m, so the correction must take an object at infinity and place its image at the far point: P = −1/0.20 = −5.0 D.
  4. Negative means diverging (concave): we must subtract power from an eye that has too much. Hyperopia is the mirror image — the eye is too weak or too short, the correction is positive. A useful sanity check: a myope's correction is always negative and its magnitude is the reciprocal of the far point in metres.
  5. (c) Amplitude = 1/0.10 − 1/∞ = 10.0 − 0 = 10 D — which decays roughly linearly to about 1 D by the age of 60, and that decay is presbyopia.

Phototransduction — the cascade that runs backwards

This is the single most counter-intuitive mechanism in sensory physiology and consequently one of the most examined. In the dark, a rod is not quiet. Cyclic GMP is high, cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels are held open, Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ pour in as a standing "dark current", the cell sits depolarised at about −40 mV, and it is continuously releasing glutamate. Light switches this off:

  1. A photon isomerises 11-cis-retinal to all-trans-retinal inside opsin. This is the only step light performs — everything after it is ordinary chemistry.
  2. The activated rhodopsin catalytically activates hundreds of molecules of transducin, a G protein.
  3. Transducin activates phosphodiesterase 6, which hydrolyses cGMP at about a thousand molecules per second each.
  4. cGMP falls, the CNG channels close, the dark current stops.
  5. The rod hyperpolarises towards −70 mV and releases less glutamate.

Three consequences worth stating explicitly because each one is a distractor waiting to happen. Vertebrate photoreceptors hyperpolarise in response to light — the opposite of every other sensory receptor. Photoreceptors and bipolar cells generate graded potentials only, with no action potentials; the first spikes in the visual system appear in the ganglion cells. And because glutamate is being reduced, "ON" bipolar cells must invert the sign — they do, using a metabotropic mGluR6 receptor at which glutamate is inhibitory, while "OFF" bipolar cells use an ordinary ionotropic receptor and keep the sign. Two channels for two polarities of contrast, built from one cascade.

Which way every arrow points once the light arrives

light shuts the four Cs — cGMP · Channels · Charge · Chemical

Our own hook, not a standard one. The reason this cascade is misremembered is never the names of the proteins — it is the direction, because "a stimulus arrives, therefore something goes up" is the habit every other receptor has trained. In a rod, light makes four things fall in a row, and all four begin with the same letter. cGMP: PDE6 destroys it. Channels: the cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels shut and the standing dark current stops. Charge: Vm falls from about −40 mV towards −70 mV, i.e. the cell hyperpolarises. Chemical: it releases less glutamate — the transmitter is the last domino, not the first. If any option has a rod depolarising, cGMP rising, PDE being inhibited, or glutamate increasing, it has broken the chain of falls and is wrong. It deliberately omits the two upstream steps — photon isomerises 11-cis-retinal to all-trans, activated rhodopsin activates transducin, transducin activates PDE6 — because those are ordinary "each enzyme switches on the next" chemistry and need no hook. It also does not cover the sign inversion downstream: ON bipolar cells read that fall in glutamate as excitation through metabotropic mGluR6, so "less glutamate" does not mean "less signal" past the photoreceptor.

Rods and cones — the trade-off, not two lists

There are about 120 million rods and 6 million cones feeding roughly 1 million ganglion cells, so on average 126 photoreceptors converge on each output fibre. That convergence is the whole story. Rods pool their inputs heavily, which sums photons and makes them exquisitely sensitive — a rod can respond to a single photon — but destroys resolution, and there is only one rod pigment so rods cannot see colour. Cones converge barely at all in the fovea, roughly one to one, so acuity is superb and there are three pigments (S ≈ 420 nm, M ≈ 530 nm, L ≈ 560 nm) for colour — but sensitivity is poor and cones are useless at night. Hence the practical consequence people notice: at night you see a dim star better by looking slightly beside it, because the fovea is all cones and has no rods at all. Note also that the optic disc has no photoreceptors of either kind — the blind spot — and that dark adaptation takes 20–30 minutes because that is how long rhodopsin regeneration takes.

The ear — an impedance-matching transformer with a frequency analyser bolted on

Sound arrives in air and must end up in fluid. At a bare air–water interface about 99.9 % of the acoustic energy is reflected, a loss of roughly 30 dB. The middle ear exists to recover that, and it does so with two mechanical tricks in series: the tympanic membrane is about 17 times the area of the stapes footplate (55 mm² against 3.2 mm²), concentrating the same force onto a smaller area, and the ossicular chain acts as a lever with a ratio of about 1.3. Total pressure gain ≈ 17 × 1.3 ≈ 22×, which is 20 log₁₀ 22 ≈ 27 dB — very nearly exactly what the interface would have cost. That is a genuinely satisfying piece of biological engineering, and the arithmetic is examinable.

Inside the cochlea the basilar membrane does the frequency analysis passively, by mechanical resonance: narrow and stiff at the base (high frequency, up to 20 kHz), wide and floppy at the apex (low frequency, down to 20 Hz). A pure tone therefore produces a travelling wave with a peak at one place, and which hair cells are excited encodes pitch — the place code. Loudness is encoded by firing rate and by the number of fibres recruited.

The transduction itself is the fastest in the body because it is purely mechanical. Stereocilia on the hair cells are joined near their tips by protein tip links. Deflection towards the tallest stereocilium stretches the tip links, which pull mechanically-gated channels open in microseconds; deflection the other way slackens them and closes the channels that were open at rest. There is no second messenger and no delay.

The hair cell's strangest fact, and why it is asked

The channel that opens is non-selective, and the ion that flows in is K⁺ — the ion that everywhere else in the body flows out and hyperpolarises. It works because the fluid bathing the stereocilia is endolymph, which is uniquely high in K⁺ (≈150 mM, resembling cytosol) and is held at an endocochlear potential of +80 mV by the stria vascularis. The hair cell interior sits at about −45 mV, so the potential across the apical membrane is −45 − 80 = −125 mV while EK across that membrane is about +2 mV — a driving force of roughly 127 mV pushing K⁺ inwards. Depolarisation follows, then Ca²⁺ entry at the base, then glutamate release. The lesson generalises: the direction an ion moves is set by the driving force, never by the concentration gradient alone. Note also the division of labour — the ≈3500 inner hair cells are the true sensors and take 90–95 % of the afferent fibres, while the ≈12 000 outer hair cells are motors, changing length via the protein prestin to amplify the travelling wave. Loud-noise and aminoglycoside damage hits outer hair cells first, which is why high-frequency sensitivity goes first.

Exam technique · the eye-and-ear questions that actually recur

  1. Accommodation: redraw the ring, do not recall the chain — there is no mnemonic for this and you do not want one, because the mnemonic would be a second chance to invert it. Contracting a circular muscle makes its ring smaller; a smaller ring moves the zonule attachments inwards; slack zonules let an elastic lens spring back to its natural round shape; round means more power, which is what near vision needs. Any option in which the ciliary muscle contracts and the lens flattens is dead, and it is the classic distractor because it sounds mechanically sensible.
  2. If the stem says "light" and the option says "up", kill it — depolarises, cGMP rises, PDE6 inhibited, more glutamate, or "the rod fires an action potential" are five wordings of the same single error, and one of them is almost always on the paper. Photoreceptors and bipolar cells never spike; the first action potentials in the visual system are in the ganglion cells.
  3. Dioptre arithmetic: everything in metres, then check the sign — myopia correction = −1/(far point), so a far point of 0.20 m gives −5.0 D. Reading addition = 1/(desired distance) − 1/(near point), so reading at 25 cm with a near point of 50 cm needs 4.0 − 2.0 = +2.0 D. The sign is the sanity check: a myope's correction is always negative, a reading add and a hyperope's correction are always positive. An option with the wrong sign can be struck without arithmetic.
  4. Tonotopy needs no mnemonic either — think of a guitar string — short, narrow and stiff sounds high, so the cochlear base takes high frequencies and the wide floppy apex takes low ones. The same one line then explains the clinic: outer hair cells are the amplifier and sit worst-exposed, so noise and aminoglycosides take high frequencies first.
  5. Any "which way does the ion move" question in the ear is a driving-force question — the same rule as § 01. K⁺ flows into the hair cell because the apical membrane sees roughly −125 mV against an EK of about +2 mV across it. An option that reasons from "K⁺ is high inside cells, so it leaves" has used the gradient instead of the driving force and is the intended trap.
  6. Where the time goes — accommodation direction, photoreceptor direction and dioptre sign are ten-second questions once these rules are in place. The receptor-and-adaptation catalogue (Merkel, Meissner, Ruffini, Pacinian; the five taste modalities) is pure recall: answer it fast if you know it and move on if you do not, but still guess, because one eliminated option makes guessing profitable.
Go deeper · the vestibular system, and the other senses in outline

Vestibular. The same hair cell, packaged twice. Three semicircular canals, mutually perpendicular, each with a gelatinous cupula that is deflected by endolymph lagging behind during angular acceleration — they detect rotation, and they stop responding during constant-velocity spin, which is why a spinning dancer gets vertigo when she stops rather than while she turns. The utricle and saccule carry otoliths, calcium carbonate crystals on a gel, which are pulled by gravity and by linear acceleration — the utricle horizontally, the saccule vertically. Displaced otoliths falling into a canal cause benign paroxysmal positional vertigo.

Taste. Five modalities. Salt (Na⁺ through ENaC) and sour (H⁺ through Otop1) act on ion channels directly. Sweet (T1R2+T1R3), umami (T1R1+T1R3, glutamate) and bitter (≈25 T2R receptors) are all GPCRs signalling through gustducin, PLCβ and TRPM5. Two things follow: bitter has many receptors because it is a poison detector and false negatives are fatal, whereas sweet has one because sugar is sugar. And the "tongue map" of school textbooks is simply wrong — all modalities are detectable over the whole tongue.

Smell. About 400 functional olfactory receptor genes in humans, each olfactory sensory neuron expressing exactly one. All are GPCRs signalling through Golf → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → CNG channel. Two exam-grade peculiarities: neurons expressing the same receptor converge on the same glomerulus in the olfactory bulb, giving a spatial odour map; and olfaction is the only sense that reaches the cortex without a thalamic relay, projecting directly to piriform cortex and amygdala — the anatomical reason smell is so tightly bound to emotion and memory. Olfactory sensory neurons are also among the very few neurons that are continuously replaced through life.

Somatosensation. Mechanoreceptors graded by adaptation speed and receptive field: Merkel (slow, small — form and texture), Meissner (fast, small — flutter and slip), Ruffini (slow, large — skin stretch), Pacinian (fast, large — vibration; its onion-like lamellae are what make it phasic, and stripping them makes it tonic). Thermoreception uses TRP channels: TRPV1 responds to noxious heat and to capsaicin, TRPM8 to cold and to menthol — which is the whole explanation of why chilli feels hot and mint feels cold. Nociceptors are free nerve endings, are tonic rather than phasic (adaptation to pain would be lethal), and signal along Aδ and C fibres as in § 03.

§ 07

Muscle ultrastructure and the sliding filament

The ideaA muscle shortens without any of its parts getting shorter. Inside it are two sets of rods of fixed length, interleaved like the fingers of two hands, and contraction is simply the two sets sliding further past one another. Every visible stripe in a muscle fibre is a direct consequence of where the two kinds of rod overlap, so if you know which stripe is "thick rod" and which is "thin rod only", you can predict exactly which stripes narrow during contraction and which cannot possibly change. The sliding itself is done by little arms on the thick rods that grab, pull, let go and reset, thousands of times a second, each one contributing a fraction of the movement and the force.

Which band is which, and which ones are allowed to change

dArk A · lIght I — and A is Always 1.6 µm

The letter half of this is the standard textbook hook and worth using because you will meet it everywhere: the A band is Anisotropic and therefore dArk, the I band is Isotropic and therefore lIght. The second half is the payload. The A band is the thick filament, thick filaments do not change length, so A is always 1.6 µm at every sarcomere length and in every state of contraction. What narrows is everything defined by the gap between filaments: the I band, the H zone, and the Z-to-Z distance itself. It covers which band changes, not by how much — but you do not need a mnemonic for that either, because the two subtractions fall straight out of the filament lengths: I = L − 1.6 (the sarcomere minus the thick filament) and H = L − 2.0 (the sarcomere minus the two 1.0 µm thin filaments). It also says nothing about the M line and Z disc, which are lines rather than bands: M in the middle cross-linking the thick filaments, Z at the boundary anchoring the thin ones.

Exam technique · sarcomere band arithmetic in twenty seconds

  1. Write three lines before you read the options — A = 1.6, I = L − 1.6, H = L − 2.0. That is the entire question family, and writing it costs less time than reading one option carefully.
  2. Kill any option in which the A band changed — the single most common distractor in the topic, and it can be struck without computing anything at all. If a stem itself claims the A band shortened, the stem is describing something other than contraction.
  3. Kill any option in which every band was scaled by the same factor — 2.4 → 2.0 µm is a factor of 0.833, and an option offering A = 1.33 µm has multiplied 1.6 by it. That is the "filaments shorten" misconception wearing arithmetic, and it is the hardest distractor to spot because the numbers look worked out.
  4. Floor the H zone at zero — at L = 2.0 µm the two thin filaments have just met at the M line, so H = 0. It never goes negative; below 2.0 µm the thin filaments start overlapping each other instead. An option offering a positive H at 2.0 µm, or a negative H at all, is dead.
  5. For whole-muscle shortening use the fraction, not the sarcomere count — 2.4 → 2.0 µm is 0.4/2.4 = 16.7 %, so a 30 cm muscle shortens 5.0 cm. Counting 125 000 sarcomeres in series gives the same 5.0 cm and three extra chances to drop a power of ten.
  6. Both length–tension landmarks are geometry, not memory — active tension reaches zero when overlap does, at L = 1.6 + 2 × 1.0 = 3.6 µm, and the plateau at 2.0–2.2 µm is where the extra overlap is against the head-free bare zone, so more overlap adds no cross-bridges. If you can reconstruct those two you never need the curve.

The cross-bridge cycle, with the energy in the right place

Myosin II is a two-headed motor. Each head is an ATPase and an actin-binding site, and the cycle has four steps that are almost always recalled in the wrong order because the intuitive story — "ATP is hydrolysed and that pulls the filament" — is not what happens.

  1. ATP binds the myosin head. This reduces myosin's affinity for actin, so the head detaches. Binding, not hydrolysis, is the release step.
  2. ATP is hydrolysed to ADP + Pi, both of which stay bound. The energy is used to cock the head into a high-energy, 90° conformation. The energy is now stored in the protein, not in the nucleotide.
  3. The head binds actin — if, and only if, tropomyosin has been moved off the binding site (see § 08). This forms the cross-bridge.
  4. Pi is released, and the head snaps back to 45°: the power stroke, which drags the thin filament about 10 nm towards the M line. ADP then leaves, and the head sits tightly bound in the rigor state until the next ATP arrives.

Two things fall straight out of that ordering. Rigor mortis is step 1 failing for want of ATP: with no ATP to bind, every head remains locked to actin, and the muscle stiffens — it resolves only when the proteins themselves decay. And relaxation costs ATP too, twice over: once to detach the heads and once for the SERCA pump to put Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. "Contraction uses ATP, relaxation is passive" is a sentence to reject on sight.

The four steps in the order they actually happen

ATP lets go · hydrolysis cocks · actin catches · Pi fires

Our own hook rather than a standard one, but it is built from the note's own image — a resting muscle is a loaded gun — and it fixes the two places the intuitive story goes wrong. ATP lets go: ATP binding is what detaches the head, not hydrolysis. Hydrolysis cocks: hydrolysis raises the head to 90°, storing the energy in the protein while ADP and Pi stay bound — the gun is now loaded and the energy is no longer in the nucleotide. Actin catches: the cocked head binds actin, but only if tropomyosin has been moved aside, which is the whole of Ca²⁺'s job in § 08. Pi fires: phosphate leaves and the head snaps to 45°, dragging the thin filament about 10 nm — the power stroke. Note where the trigger sits: the stroke is fired by phosphate leaving, not by hydrolysis — hydrolysis only loads the gun. It stops one beat early: after the stroke, ADP leaves and the head sits tightly bound in the rigor state until the next ATP arrives. That tail matters, because rigor mortis is precisely step 1 failing for want of ATP. It also does not encode that relaxation costs ATP twice — once to detach the heads and once for SERCA to pump Ca²⁺ back — which is a separate examinable point.

The plateau is 2.0–2.2 µm: shortening from 2.2 to 2.0 only adds overlap against the myosin bare zone, which carries no heads, so force stops rising rather than continuing to climb — and this is precisely why a joint has an optimum angle for strength.

The two statements that will be offered to you as facts

"During contraction the actin and myosin filaments shorten." They do not, and the constancy of the A band is the experimental proof — this is the observation from which the sliding-filament model was inferred in 1954, so it is not a detail but the entire basis of the theory. "The A band shortens during contraction." Also false, and it is the single most common distractor in this topic: I band and H zone narrow, A band does not, and the sarcomere and the whole muscle shorten. If you remember only one thing from this section, remember that the dark band is dark because it is the thick filament and therefore cannot change.

§ 08

Excitation–contraction coupling and motor units

The ideaA muscle fibre has to convert an electrical event on its surface into a mechanical event deep inside, and it has to do it everywhere at once, because a fibre is far too thick for a signal to diffuse across in time. It solves this with tunnels: the surface membrane dives into the fibre at regular intervals, carrying the electrical signal to within a few tens of nanometres of the internal calcium store. There the voltage change is read directly and the store is opened. Calcium then does one specific job — it moves a blocking strap off the actin filament so the motors can grip. Take the calcium away and the strap slides back and the muscle relaxes. The whole of muscle control, including how hard you grip a pen versus a hammer, is variations on how often and how many.

Definition & key facts

Excitation–contraction coupling
The chain that converts a sarcolemmal action potential into cross-bridge cycling: T-tubule depolarisation → voltage sensor → sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ release → troponin C binds Ca²⁺ → tropomyosin moves → cross-bridges form.

Calcium's actual job: it does not "cause contraction"

Say precisely what Ca²⁺ does and half of this section is done. In a resting skeletal muscle fibre, myosin heads are already cocked and ready and the ATP is already hydrolysed — the muscle is a loaded gun. What stops it is purely steric: tropomyosin is lying across the myosin-binding sites on actin. Ca²⁺ binding to troponin C removes that block. So contraction is disinhibited, not initiated, and the correct one-line answer to "what does calcium do in muscle?" is it moves tropomyosin off the actin binding sites, not "it activates myosin" and not "it provides energy".

That framing pays off immediately when you meet smooth muscle, where Ca²⁺ does something entirely different — it binds calmodulin, which activates myosin light chain kinase, which phosphorylates myosin so that it can work at all. Same ion, same trigger, opposite target: thin filament in striated muscle, thick filament in smooth muscle. A question that asks "which of these uses troponin?" is really asking whether you know that.

Skeletal versus cardiac coupling — the highest-yield distinction in this section

Skeletal muscle. DHPR and RyR1 are physically coupled. Depolarisation moves DHPR, DHPR mechanically opens RyR1, SR Ca²⁺ floods out. No extracellular Ca²⁺ is required — put a skeletal fibre in a Ca²⁺-free bath and stimulate it directly and it contracts perfectly well, for as long as the SR holds out.

Cardiac muscle. DHPR (Cav1.2) is a genuine channel; Ca²⁺ enters through it during the plateau, and that Ca²⁺ binds RyR2 and opens it. This is calcium-induced calcium release, and it means cardiac contraction absolutely requires extracellular Ca²⁺. Remove it and the heart stops beating mechanically within a few beats while still generating action potentials — the phenomenon of electromechanical dissociation. It also explains why Ca²⁺-channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) reduce cardiac contractility but have no direct effect on skeletal muscle, and why cardiac contraction is gradeable by adrenaline while a skeletal twitch is all-or-none.

A related trap: in a Ca²⁺-free bath, stimulating a skeletal muscle through its nerve also fails — but at the neuromuscular junction, where transmitter release needs extracellular Ca²⁺ (§ 04), not inside the fibre. Read carefully which part of the pathway the question is about.

Keeping the skeletal and cardiac machinery apart

carDiac Dyad, all 2s · skeletal all 1s, one 3

Our own hook, not a standard one, and it exists because these proteins are named by number and the numbers are the only arbitrary thing here. carDiac Dyad: the D of carDiac is the D of Dyad — one T-tubule with one cisterna, two elements, sitting at the Z disc — and once that is stuck the rest of the cardiac set is twos as well, RyR2 and Cav1.2. Skeletal is all ones: RyR1, Cav1.1. One 3 is the exception, and you must keep it: skeletal muscle's junction is a triad, three elements — one T-tubule flanked by two cisternae, two of them per sarcomere at the A–I junctions. So skeletal is 1, 1 and 3. Once the numbers are stuck, the mechanism follows the note: the skeletal pair is mechanically linked so no Ca²⁺ needs to enter, while the cardiac pair is linked only by Ca²⁺ itself, so cardiac contraction cannot happen without extracellular Ca²⁺. The hook carries nothing about smooth muscle, which has no T-tubules at all (caveolae instead) and no troponin — and there the discriminator is not a number but a protein: calmodulin, not troponin C.

Grading force: recruitment and rate, and nothing else

A single fibre obeys the all-or-none law, so the nervous system cannot make one fibre pull harder. It has exactly two levers.

  • Recruitment — activating more motor units. The size principle (Henneman) says they are recruited in order of motor neuron size, smallest first, because a small neuron has a higher input resistance and so reaches threshold with less synaptic current. Small neurons drive small, slow, fatigue-resistant units; large ones drive large, fast, fatigable units. So delicate movements use only the precise, tireless units, and the powerful ones are held in reserve — a sorting done for free by cable properties, not by a decision.
  • Rate coding — firing each unit faster so its twitches fuse. One stimulus gives a twitch; stimuli arriving before the previous twitch has relaxed give summation, then unfused (incomplete) tetanus, then fused tetanus at three to four times twitch force. Note the reason summation is possible at all: the muscle action potential lasts 1–5 ms but the twitch lasts 20–200 ms, so a second AP is perfectly legal long before the mechanics have finished. Contrast cardiac muscle in § 09, where that gap has been deliberately closed.

Motor unit size follows the job. Classical human counts: extraocular muscles about 1 neuron : 13 fibres, the first dorsal interosseous of the hand about 1 : 340, medial gastrocnemius about 1 : 1800. Fine control needs many small units so that force can be added in small increments; a calf muscle needs few large ones because the increments do not have to be subtle.

Two muscle-metabolism claims that need qualifying

"Lactic acid causes muscle fatigue and delayed soreness." Neither half survives scrutiny. Lactate is a fuel, is rapidly exported and oxidised elsewhere, and the acidosis in exercising muscle comes mainly from ATP hydrolysis rather than from lactate itself; fatigue correlates far better with accumulated inorganic phosphate (which impairs SR Ca²⁺ release and cross-bridge force) and with impaired excitation. Delayed-onset soreness peaking at 24–48 h is mechanical microdamage from eccentric contraction, and lactate has cleared long before it starts. "Creatine phosphate is the immediate energy source for contraction." Close, but the immediate source is always ATP; creatine phosphate is a rapid buffer that regenerates ATP through creatine kinase for the first ten seconds or so. The distinction matters because the exam likes asking which molecule myosin actually binds.

§ 09

Cardiac and smooth muscle

The ideaThree kinds of muscle, three different jobs, and the differences between them are all consequences of the job. Skeletal muscle has to be switched on and off precisely by a nerve and to be able to pull as hard as possible, so it is under complete external control and can be driven to a fused maximum. Heart muscle must never be driven to a fused maximum — a heart that tetanised would stop pumping — so it has evolved a contraction so long that it cannot be re-triggered until it has finished, and it beats without being told to. Smooth muscle has to hold a tube narrowed for hours without exhausting itself, so it trades speed for extraordinary economy.

Definition & key facts

Functional syncytium
A tissue whose cells are electrically continuous through gap junctions, so an action potential arising anywhere spreads to all of them and they contract as one. Cardiac muscle (via intercalated discs) and single-unit smooth muscle are functional syncytia; skeletal muscle is not — each skeletal fibre is itself a multinucleate syncytium of fused myoblasts, but neighbouring fibres are electrically independent of one another, which is why skeletal fibres can be recruited individually and cardiac cells cannot.
cardiac refractory period ≈ twitch duration ≈ 250 ms → tetanus is impossible the single design constraint that shapes the entire cardiac action potential
Full definition, symbols and units
Plateau phase
Phase 2 of the cardiac ventricular action potential: inward Ca²⁺ through L-type channels almost exactly balances outward K⁺, holding Vm near 0 mV for 200 ms or more. It is why the cardiac AP lasts ~250 ms against a neuron's 1–2 ms, and therefore why the refractory period outlasts the contraction.
Autorhythmicity
Spontaneous depolarisation. Sinoatrial nodal cells have no stable resting potential: the funny current If (a Na⁺ influx through HCN channels, activated by hyperpolarisation) drifts them up to threshold. Autonomic nerves only modulate the slope — the heart beats with every nerve cut, which is why transplanted hearts work.
Latch state
Smooth muscle's economy trick: dephosphorylated cross-bridges that remain attached, maintaining tension at very low ATP cost. This is how a sphincter or an arteriole holds tone for hours.
Single-unit vs multi-unit smooth muscle
Single-unit (visceral: gut, uterus, ureter, most vessels) — coupled by gap junctions, often spontaneously active, stretch-sensitive, contracts as a sheet. Multi-unit (iris, ciliary body, vas deferens, large airways, piloerector) — individually innervated, finely graded, little coupling.

Smooth muscle's economy, stated as numbers

Smooth muscle develops force slower than skeletal muscle by an order of magnitude, because its myosin ATPase is that much slower — but it develops comparable or greater force per cross-sectional area, and it does so at roughly 1/300th of the ATP cost for maintained tension, because latched cross-bridges detach very slowly. That is the correct answer to "why does smooth muscle not fatigue?": not that it has more mitochondria, but that it cycles its cross-bridges far more slowly and keeps them attached. The same slowness explains why it can shorten to a much greater fraction of its resting length — up to about 70 %, against 30 % for skeletal muscle — because it is not constrained by a rigid sarcomere geometry.

§ 10

The endocrine system as feedback loops

The ideaA nerve sends a private message down a wire to one address. A gland shouts into the bloodstream, and every cell in the body hears it — only the ones with the right receptor respond. That makes hormonal control slow, widespread and long-lasting, and it creates one problem a wire does not have: nothing tells the gland when to stop. The solution is always the same. The thing the hormone changes is measured, and the measurement is fed back to switch the gland off. Almost every endocrine disease, and almost every endocrine exam question, is a question about which point in one of those loops has broken — and you can work that out from two numbers on a blood test rather than memorising a list of syndromes.

Definition & key facts

Negative feedback loop
The output of a pathway inhibits its own upstream drivers. Because the loop is negative, the trophic hormone and the target hormone move in opposite directions when the fault is in the target gland, and in the same direction when the fault is upstream. That one sentence localises almost any endocrine lesion.
hypothalamus → (releasing hormone) → anterior pituitary → (trophic hormone) → gland → hormone ⊣ back onto both three tiers, two feedback arrows; the pattern repeats for thyroid, adrenal and gonad
Full definition, symbols and units
Anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis)
True glandular epithelium, derived from Rathke's pouch. It is controlled by hypothalamic hormones that arrive in blood, through the hypophyseal portal system — a set of capillaries that drain into a second set of capillaries rather than back to the heart, so the releasing hormones reach the pituitary at high concentration without being diluted in the systemic circulation. Secretes TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, GH and prolactin.
Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)
Not a gland. It is the axon terminals of neurons whose cell bodies are in the hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. ADH and oxytocin are synthesised there, transported down the axons and merely stored and released here. Hypothalamic damage therefore abolishes them even if the pituitary is intact.
Primary / secondary / tertiary
The tier that has failed: primary = the target gland, secondary = the pituitary, tertiary = the hypothalamus.
Positive feedback
Rare, and always for an event that must go to completion: the LH surge (sustained high oestrogen switches from inhibiting to stimulating GnRH/LH, triggering ovulation), oxytocin in labour (the Ferguson reflex), and milk ejection.

The two pituitary lobes are not two halves of one organ

Anterior: epithelial, controlled by portal blood, makes its own hormones. Posterior: neural, controlled by axons, makes nothing. The classic examinable consequence: cutting the pituitary stalk severs the portal vessels, so TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH and GH all fall — but prolactin rises, because the only thing arriving through those vessels for prolactin was dopamine, and dopamine was inhibitory. A "stalk effect" hyperprolactinaemia is therefore evidence against a prolactin-secreting tumour, and the same logic explains why dopamine-antagonist antipsychotics cause galactorrhoea while dopamine agonists such as bromocriptine treat prolactinomas.

The six anterior pituitary hormones, split the way the exam splits them

FLAT PiG

The standard mnemonic, the one that circulates in 補習班 and USMLE material, so use it rather than anything of your own. FSH · LH · ACTH · TSH · Prolactin · GH — six hormones, six letters, and the lower-case i is filler for pronunciation only. The split is the useful part: FLAT are the four tropic hormones, each aimed at another endocrine gland and each therefore part of a feedback loop you can read off a lab panel; PiG are the two that act on non-endocrine tissue directly, which is why prolactin and GH are the two that do not fit the neat three-tier picture. Use it only for the anterior lobe. The posterior lobe is ADH and oxytocin and needs no mnemonic — two items, both made in the hypothalamus, neither made in the pituitary at all. FLAT PiG also says nothing about which hormone is under tonic inhibition: prolactin is the one, held down by dopamine, and it is the reason a cut stalk lowers the other five and raises that one.

The four axes worth knowing as loops

Thyroid. TRH → TSH → follicular cells trap iodide, thyroid peroxidase organifies it onto thyroglobulin, and the gland secretes about 16 parts T4 to 1 part T3. But T4 is a prohormone: roughly 80 % of circulating T3 is made by peripheral deiodinases removing one iodine from T4, and T3 is three to five times more potent at the receptor. This is why a thyroid axis has an unusual property — the gland's main product is not the active hormone, and the conversion step is a regulated point of its own (it is suppressed in serious illness, giving "sick euthyroid" patterns). Feedback is mainly T3 acting on the thyrotroph. Iodine deficiency: T4 low → TSH high → the gland is driven to grow → goitre, which is a mass caused by the feedback loop working correctly. Graves' disease: an antibody that mimics TSH at its receptor → T4 and T3 high, TSH suppressed — the feedback is intact and is being ignored by the antibody.

Adrenal. The cortex is three layers, outside in: zona Glomerulosa → aldosterone, zona Fasciculata → cortisol, zona Reticularis → androgens (GFR: salt, sugar, sex). Only the inner two are driven by ACTH; the glomerulosa answers primarily to angiotensin II and plasma K⁺, which is why ACTH deficiency causes cortisol deficiency without significant salt wasting, whereas destruction of the whole gland (Addison's) causes both. The medulla is chromaffin tissue — modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons secreting ≈80 % adrenaline. One elegant link: the enzyme PNMT, which converts noradrenaline to adrenaline, needs the very high local cortisol concentrations delivered from the cortex, so the anatomical arrangement (cortex draining through medulla) is functionally necessary rather than incidental.

The three cortical layers, their products, and which way round they go

GFR — Salt, Sugar, Sex; the deeper you go, the sweeter it gets

The standard mnemonic, and worth using precisely because it is the one you will meet elsewhere. Reading outside in: zona Glomerulosa → aldosterone → salt; zona Fasciculata → cortisol → sugar; zona Reticularis → androgens → sex. The tail is doing real work: "the deeper you go, the sweeter it gets" fixes the direction, which is the half students actually lose — G is the thin outermost rind, R is innermost and abuts the medulla. GFR is also the same three letters as the renal abbreviation, which is what makes it stick. Two things it hides, and both are examined. Only F and R are driven by ACTH; the glomerulosa answers to angiotensin II and plasma K⁺, which is exactly why ACTH deficiency gives cortisol deficiency without salt wasting while Addison's disease gives both. And it stops at the cortex: the medulla is a fourth layer, not cortex at all — chromaffin cells, modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons, ≈80 % adrenaline, no ganglion in front of them.

Pancreas. The islets are ≈70 % β cells (insulin), ≈20 % α (glucagon) and ≈5 % δ (somatostatin, which inhibits both neighbours). The β cell is the cleanest glucose sensor in the body, and it is worth being able to recite the chain because it is drug target after drug target: glucose enters through GLUT2, is phosphorylated by glucokinase (the true sensor, because its Km sits in the physiological range), is oxidised, raises ATP/ADP, which closes the KATP channel, which depolarises the cell, which opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, which triggers exocytosis of insulin. Sulfonylureas close that same KATP channel pharmacologically — which is exactly why they cause hypoglycaemia and metformin does not. Note that the axis here is not hypothalamic at all: the controlled variable is a metabolite, and it feeds back directly on the gland. Simple loops do not need a pituitary.

Parathyroid and the calcium axis. Chief cells carry a calcium-sensing receptor and secrete PTH when ionised Ca²⁺ falls. PTH does three things: it drives bone resorption, it makes the kidney reabsorb calcium (distal tubule) while dumping phosphate (proximal tubule), and it activates renal 1α-hydroxylase to make calcitriol, which raises calcium absorption from the gut. Net effect: calcium up, phosphate down — and that phosphate divergence is the discriminator, because calcitriol raises both. Two refinements examiners like. First, osteoclasts have no PTH receptor: PTH acts on osteoblasts, which then express RANKL to activate osteoclasts — so the textbook shorthand "PTH stimulates osteoclasts" is wrong in mechanism, and it is the reason denosumab (an anti-RANKL antibody) works. Second, intermittent PTH is anabolic for bone while continuous PTH is catabolic, which is why teriparatide is given as a daily injection.

PTH versus calcitriol — the phosphate discriminator

PTH = Phosphate Trashing Hormone

A standard and widely circulating expansion of the abbreviation, and it earns its place because calcium is not the discriminator: PTH and calcitriol both raise calcium, so a stem that gives you calcium alone cannot separate them. Phosphate can. PTH: Ca ↑, phosphate ↓ — it makes the proximal tubule dump phosphate even while the distal tubule reclaims calcium. Calcitriol: Ca ↑, phosphate ↑ — it pulls both out of the gut together, because that is what absorbing bone mineral from food looks like. So the moment a panel shows a high calcium with a high phosphate, PTH is not the driver. It encodes PTH's renal effect only. It does not carry the two mechanism refinements the examiners like: osteoclasts have no PTH receptor — PTH acts on osteoblasts, which put out RANKL — and intermittent PTH is anabolic for bone while continuous PTH is catabolic. Nor does it cover calcitonin, which is a C-cell product, lowers calcium, and is minor in adult humans.

Exam technique · reading a two-hormone panel, and the calcium that is not low

  1. Label the two hormones before you look at the numbers — one is trophic (TSH, ACTH, LH/FSH), one is the target hormone (free T4, cortisol, sex steroid). Any option that draws a conclusion from a trophic hormone alone is beatable on that fact alone, whatever its numbers say.
  2. Then read directions, not values — moving in opposite directions puts the lesion in the target gland: low target + high trophic is primary failure, and high target + low trophic is an autonomous gland or a receptor-mimicking antibody, with the loop intact and simply overwhelmed. Moving in the same direction puts it upstream — pituitary, hypothalamus, or exogenous hormone.
  3. Treat "normal" as a direction, not as a reassurance — a TSH of 0.9 beside a clearly low free T4 is inappropriately normal: an intact pituitary would have pushed it well above 4.0, so it counts as moving with the target hormone, and the answer is secondary hypothyroidism. This is the single most reliable trap in the endocrine block, and it is worth spending part of your 66 seconds on.
  4. Correct the calcium before you call anyone hypocalcaemic — Cacorr = Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin) in mg·dL⁻¹. An albumin of 2.0 adds 1.6, so 7.6 becomes 9.2 mg·dL⁻¹, which is normal. Divide by 4.008 if the options are in SI — 9.2 gives 2.30 mmol·L⁻¹. The same free-fraction principle kills any option that reads a raised total T4 in pregnancy as thyrotoxicosis.
  5. Free elimination on the phosphate column — any option that has PTH raising both calcium and phosphate is dead, and so is any option that has an antibody-driven hyperthyroidism running with a high TSH. Both are one-glance kills, and with five options and −0.5 marking, one kill is enough to make answering strictly better than a blank.
  6. Where the time goes — a two-hormone panel is a 20-second question once these three lines are automatic. Spend what you save on the hormone-catalogue stems (source, class, half-life, carrier protein), which are pure recall and cannot be reasoned out under time pressure.
Worked example 7 — reading two-hormone lab panels, and correcting a calcium

(a) Four patients have a low free T4. Their TSH values are 18 mU·L⁻¹, 0.9 mU·L⁻¹, 0.02 mU·L⁻¹ and 6.0 mU·L⁻¹ (reference 0.4–4.0). Localise each lesion. (b) A patient's total calcium is 7.6 mg·dL⁻¹ (reference 8.8–10.4) with albumin 2.0 g·dL⁻¹ (reference 4.0). Is she hypocalcaemic?

  1. (a) TSH 18, high. Trophic hormone and target hormone move in opposite directions, so the pituitary is intact and is compensating: primary hypothyroidism (Hashimoto's, iodine deficiency, post-ablation).
  2. TSH 0.9, "normal". This is the trap. With a free T4 that low, an intact pituitary should be producing a TSH well above 4.0. A numerically normal TSH is therefore inappropriately normal, and the two hormones are effectively moving together: secondary (pituitary) hypothyroidism. Never interpret a trophic hormone without the target hormone beside it.
  3. TSH 0.02, suppressed. Also same-direction, and profoundly so — central hypothyroidism, or suppression by exogenous thyroid hormone or by recent treatment of thyrotoxicosis.
  4. TSH 6.0, mildly high. Opposite direction again, so primary — a milder or earlier primary failure (compare subclinical hypothyroidism, where TSH is raised but free T4 is still normal: the loop is compensating successfully).
  5. (b) Roughly 40 % of plasma calcium is bound to albumin and only the ionised fraction is biologically active, so a low albumin lowers the total without lowering the active fraction. Correct it: Cacorr = 7.6 + 0.8 × (4.0 − 2.0) = 7.6 + 1.6 = 9.2 mg·dL⁻¹.
  6. In SI units, 9.2 / 4.008 = 2.30 mmol·L⁻¹ — squarely inside the 2.20–2.59 mmol·L⁻¹ reference range. She is not hypocalcaemic, and treating her with calcium would be a mistake. This is the same "free hormone" principle as thyroid-binding globulin in pregnancy: only the unbound fraction is active, so binding-protein changes move total concentrations without causing disease.

Endocrine sentences that are half-right

"Hormones act only on their target organs." They act on cells with the receptor — which is a property of the receiving cell, not of the hormone, and is why thyroid hormone affects almost every tissue while TSH affects one. "The hypothalamus controls the pituitary through nerves." True for the posterior lobe only; the anterior lobe is controlled entirely through the portal blood. "Negative feedback keeps hormone levels constant." It keeps the controlled variable near a set point, which is often not the hormone: the calcium axis holds calcium steady by letting PTH swing widely, and it is the variability of PTH that reveals the loop working. "Cortisol is released in response to stress." True but incomplete — its dominant pattern is a circadian rhythm peaking just before waking, which is why a random cortisol is nearly uninterpretable and why timing the sample is half the test.

§ 11

How a hormone talks to a cell

The ideaThere are only two ways a chemical message can get into a cell, and which one a hormone uses is decided by a single physical property: whether it can dissolve through the greasy membrane. A hormone that cannot get in has to knock — it binds a receptor on the outside, and the message is passed inwards by a relay of molecules already sitting in the cytoplasm. That is fast, because everything is pre-built, and it is enormously amplified, because each relay molecule activates many of the next. A hormone that can simply walk in does not need a relay at all: it finds a receptor inside the cell, and the pair goes to the DNA and turns genes on. That is slow, because a protein has to be transcribed and translated, and it lasts a long time for the same reason. Speed versus permanence, decided by solubility.

Definition & key facts

Second messenger
A small intracellular molecule whose concentration is changed by receptor activation and which carries the signal onward — cAMP, cGMP, IP₃, DAG, Ca²⁺. The hormone itself is the first messenger and never enters the cell.

Full definition, symbols and units
G-protein-coupled receptor
Seven transmembrane helices; activation exchanges GDP for GTP on the Gα subunit. Gs stimulates adenylyl cyclase (cAMP ↑ → PKA); Gi inhibits it; Gq activates phospholipase C (→ IP₃ releases SR/ER Ca²⁺, and DAG activates PKC).
Receptor tyrosine kinase
The receptor is the enzyme. Ligand binding dimerises it, the two halves phosphorylate each other, and phosphotyrosines recruit adaptors — PI3K/Akt and Ras/MAPK. Insulin and the growth factors work this way, which is why insulin's receptor is not a GPCR and cAMP has nothing to do with it.
JAK–STAT
Receptor with no intrinsic kinase; an associated Janus kinase phosphorylates STAT proteins, which dimerise and go to the nucleus themselves — an unusually short route to transcription. Growth hormone, prolactin, leptin, erythropoietin and most cytokines.
Nuclear receptor superfamily
Ligand-activated transcription factors with a DNA-binding zinc-finger domain and a ligand-binding domain. They bind hormone response elements and recruit coactivators. The two hormones that break the pattern you were expecting

Thyroid hormone. It is built from tyrosine, so the obvious classification is "amine, like adrenaline, therefore a surface receptor". Wrong. Adding four iodines makes T4 strongly lipophilic, and it behaves in every respect like a steroid: carried on TBG, half-life of days, nuclear receptor already sitting on DNA, effect through transcription, active orally. It is the single most reliable trap in this section, and it is worth stating the rule that survives it — solubility predicts the receptor; biosynthetic family does not. (It does need a transporter, MCT8, to cross the membrane efficiently, which is a nuance rather than a contradiction.)

Insulin. A peptide, so a surface receptor — correct — but it is a receptor tyrosine kinase, not a GPCR, and cAMP plays no part in its action. In fact glucagon is the cAMP hormone of that pair, and much of insulin's effect on the liver is exerted by lowering cAMP. Any option that says "insulin acts through cAMP" is wrong twice over.

Do not memorise that comparison table — learn one column and generate the rest

There is no mnemonic here and you do not need one, because a single physical property predicts every row. The lipid-soluble set is not a list of a dozen hormones, it is four families: the steroids (cortisol, aldosterone, oestrogen, testosterone, progesterone), thyroid hormone, vitamin D as calcitriol, and vitamin A as retinoic acid. Everything else on the page is water-soluble. Hold those four, then read the consequences off rather than off a table: a hormone that can cross a membrane needs no surface receptor and no second messenger, so its receptor is inside, it works by transcription, it is slow to start and slow to stop, it dissolves poorly in plasma so it travels mostly bound to a carrier protein, its half-life is long because that carrier protects it, and it survives the gut and can be given by mouth. Each of those follows from the one before; none of them is a separate fact.

The single case where the rule and the intuition disagree is thyroid hormone, and that is the one worth holding as an explicit exception rather than trying to fold into a hook: it is built from tyrosine like adrenaline, and it behaves like a steroid anyway, because four iodines make it lipophilic. Solubility predicts the receptor; biosynthetic family does not.

Amplification, and why signalling must be switched off actively

Four catalytic steps in series, each turning over roughly a hundred substrate molecules, gives a gain of about 100⁴ = 10⁸ — which is why a hormone at 10⁻¹⁰ M can dominate a cell's metabolism. But a system with that gain is dangerous unless it can be shut down fast, so every step has a dedicated off-switch: the Gα subunit hydrolyses its own GTP, phosphodiesterases destroy cAMP, phosphatases reverse the kinases, and receptors are desensitised by GRK phosphorylation and β-arrestin binding and then internalised. Two classic diseases are failures of exactly those brakes — cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gsα so it can no longer hydrolyse GTP, leaving adenylyl cyclase permanently on in the gut epithelium and causing massive secretory diarrhoea; pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates Giα instead and locks it in its inactive GDP-bound form, so it can never inhibit — the mirror-image target, but note that the end result is the same direction, unopposed cAMP, not the opposite one. And receptor down-regulation is why continuous GnRH treatment suppresses the gonadal axis while pulsatile GnRH stimulates it. Full mechanistic treatment of these cascades is in biochemistry note 09; this section is the physiological face of the same chemistry.
