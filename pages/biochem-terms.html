§ 01

The shorthand: P, Pi, ~P, NAD⁺, CoA

The ideaBiochemistry is written in a compressed notation, and every symbol in it hides exactly one fact. A circled P hides a double negative charge, which is why a phosphorylated sugar cannot leave the cell. A squiggle hides a large free energy of hydrolysis, which is why one reaction can pay for another. A single Greek letter on a phosphate hides which phosphate is attacked, which decides whether the leaving group is ADP or pyrophosphate. The shorthand is not decoration; it is the exam question, written small. Every symbol below with a dotted underline can be clicked for its own panel.

Definition & the notation

Ⓟ — a phosphoryl group
The circled P of a pathway map is –PO₃²⁻ esterified to an oxygen. It carries about two negative charges at pH 7.4, so the molecule it is attached to is inside the cell — no facilitative transporter will carry it. Phosphorylation is how a cell keeps what it has taken up.
Pi and PPi
is free phosphate in solution, attached to nothing. is two phosphates joined head to head, P–O–P. The subscript i stands for inorganic, and it is there precisely to stop you reading it as a phosphate already on a molecule.
~P — the squiggle
A : an acyl phosphate, an enol phosphate or a phosphoanhydride. It is a statement about free energy, not about the bond. See § 07 for why the phrase “high-energy bond” is misleading.
ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ and ATP → AMP + PPi is −45.6, which is why the second route is used when a reaction must not run back

Three shorthand traps

NAD⁺ and NADP⁺ are not interchangeable in an answer. They differ by one phosphate that does no chemistry, but the cell holds NAD⁺/NADH oxidised to collect electrons from catabolism and NADP⁺/NADPH reduced to hand them to biosynthesis. Same hydride, opposite job. An option that has fatty-acid synthesis using NADH is wrong for that reason alone.

Pi is not Ⓟ. “Adds a phosphate from Pi” and “adds a phosphate from ATP” are different reactions run by differently named enzymes (§ 03). Glycogen phosphorylase uses Pi and no ATP; answering “kinase” there is a standard loss.

PPi is not “two Pi”. It becomes two Pi, and that second, separate hydrolysis by inorganic pyrophosphatase is the step that makes DNA synthesis, RNA synthesis, fatty-acid activation and glycogen synthesis irreversible. If a stem asks what commits one of those reactions, this is usually the answer.

§ 02

The three things α, β and γ label

The ideaThe same three Greek letters are used for three completely unrelated jobs, and the paper's favourite vocabulary trick is to slide between them inside one stem. There is no deep connection to find: α simply means “the first one, counting from an agreed starting point”, and biochemistry has three different agreed starting points. Fix the starting point and the letter becomes unambiguous. On a phosphate chain you count outward from the sugar. On a sugar ring the letter is not a count at all but a face — which side the new hydroxyl ended up on when the ring closed. Along a carbon chain you count from the carboxyl. Three conventions, three pictures, and after that no stem can catch you.

① α β γ · phosphates on a nucleotide

Counted outward from the sugar: α sits on the 5′ carbon, then β, then γ at the tip. A kinase hands over γ, leaving ADP. Attack at α releases PPi instead — DNA and RNA synthesis, fatty-acid activation, UDP-glucose.

② α β · the anomeric carbon of a sugar

Not a count — a face. When the ring closes, C1 becomes a new stereocentre and its hydroxyl ends up on one side or the other. There is no γ. This is the α of α-D-glucose and of the α(1→4) and α(1→6) links of glycogen.

③ α β γ · position along a carbon chain

Counted from the carboxyl (or thioester) carbon: C2 is α, C3 is β, C4 is γ. This is the sense in α-amino acid, α-keto acid, β-oxidation and vitamin-K-dependent γ-carboxylation.

The three swaps the paper actually makes

“β-oxidation cleaves a β-glycosidic bond.” Two different senses in one sentence. β-oxidation is sense ③ and happens to a fatty acyl chain; a β-glycosidic bond is sense ② and happens to a sugar. Nothing connects them.

“A kinase transfers the α phosphate.” It transfers γ. Reactions that go for α are the ones that release PPi, and they are the irreversible ones — nucleic-acid synthesis, acyl-CoA formation, UDP-glucose.

“α-amylase cuts α bonds and β-amylase cuts β bonds.” Both cut α(1→4) bonds. The prefix names the anomeric configuration of the product each releases — β-amylase inverts the centre and releases β-maltose. This is a genuinely confusing legacy name and is worth knowing precisely because it looks like it ought to follow the rule.

§ 03

Enzyme names, where the marks are lost

The ideaAn enzyme's name is meant to be a compressed description of its reaction, and mostly it is. That makes the names derivable rather than memorisable — but only if you read them as descriptions of chemistry instead of as labels. Four questions settle almost every naming item on a paper: is water a reactant? is a nucleotide spent? are electrons moved? does the molecular formula come out unchanged? Answer those and the EC class falls out, and the class usually names the enzyme. The handful of names that break the rule — ATP synthase, fatty-acid synthase, trypsin — break it in known ways, so they are worth learning as exceptions rather than as evidence that the rule is useless.

Definition & the decision rule

EC number
Four levels, as in EC 1.1.1.27 for lactate dehydrogenase. Only the first digit — the class — is examinable, and it is examined by giving you a reaction and asking which class it is.
water is a reactant → hydrolase (3)protease, lipase, phosphatase, nuclease, glycosidase
an NTP is spent → ligase (6)synthetase, carboxylase, DNA ligase
electrons move → oxidoreductase (1)dehydrogenase, oxidase, oxygenase, reductase, peroxidase
same formula out → isomerase (5)isomerase, mutase, epimerase, racemase
a group moves between molecules → transferase (2)kinase, transaminase, phosphorylase, glycosyltransferase
none of the above → lyase (4), or translocase (7) if it crossed a membranedecarboxylase, aldolase, hydratase, cyclase

The pairs, and the rule that decides each one

Confusable enzyme names with the rule that separates them
Pair or trioWhat each one doesThe rule that decides
synthase vs synthetase Synthase joins two things without spending a nucleotide (citrate synthase). Synthetase joins them and spends ATP or another NTP (glutamine synthetase, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase). The extra t is the T of NTP: a synthetase spends one, a synthase does not. That fixes synthetase as a ligase (class 6) but says nothing about a synthase's class — citrate synthase is a transferase (EC 2.3.3.1), glycogen synthase a transferase, tryptophan synthase a lyase, ATP synthase a translocase. Classify a synthase from its reaction, never from its ending.
kinase vs phosphorylase vs phosphatase Kinase transfers a phosphoryl group between a nucleotide and a substrate — usually the γ-phosphoryl of ATP onto the substrate (hexokinase, PFK-1, PKA), but the ending names the enzyme, not the direction: phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase are kinases that in the cell run the other way and make ATP (§ 07). Phosphorylase cleaves a bond using free Pi, so one fragment leaves already phosphorylated. Phosphatase removes a phosphate using water. Ask where the phosphate comes from: a nucleotide (class 2), free Pi (class 2), or — for removal — water (class 3). Glycogen phosphorylase uses no ATP; that is the trap.
mutase vs isomerase Isomerase is the whole class: any rearrangement that leaves the molecular formula alone. Mutase is the subset that moves a functional group from one atom to another within the same molecule — phosphoglycerate mutase, phosphoglucomutase, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Every mutase is an isomerase; not every isomerase is a mutase. If a group has changed position, say mutase; if a carbonyl has merely shifted or a centre has inverted, say isomerase or epimerase.
dehydrogenase vs oxidase vs oxygenase Dehydrogenase removes hydrogen to NAD⁺, NADP⁺ or FAD. Oxidase uses O₂ as the electron acceptor but puts no oxygen atom into the product — O₂ ends as H₂O or H₂O₂. Oxygenase puts oxygen atoms from O₂ into the substrate. Follow the oxygen atoms. Cytochrome c oxidase makes water and adds nothing; cytochrome P450 is a monooxygenase, one O into the substrate and one into water, which is why it needs NADPH; a dioxygenase puts both in.
reductase vs peroxidase vs catalase All class 1. Reductase is simply an oxidoreductase named in the reducing direction. Peroxidase uses H₂O₂ as the oxidant. Catalase uses H₂O₂ as both oxidant and reductant, giving H₂O and O₂. Direction is a naming choice, not chemistry: HMG-CoA reductase and its reverse would be one enzyme either way. The peroxide enzymes are told apart by what reduces the H₂O₂ — another substrate, or a second molecule of H₂O₂.
lyase vs hydrolase Hydrolase uses water to break the bond. Lyase breaks or makes a bond by elimination or addition, generating or consuming a double bond. Water appearing in the equation is not enough. Fumarase adds water across a C=C and is a lyase; enolase removes water and is a lyase. Ask whether the water attacked the bond that broke.
epimerase vs racemase Both invert a stereocentre. Racemase is used when the molecule has only one centre, so inverting it gives the enantiomer. Epimerase is used when there are several and only one is inverted. Count the stereocentres. Alanine racemase (one centre) versus UDP-galactose 4-epimerase (four centres, one inverted).
carboxylase vs decarboxylase vs carboxykinase Carboxylase adds CO₂ — usually biotin-dependent and ATP-driven, hence a ligase. Decarboxylase removes CO₂, a lyase. Carboxykinase (PEPCK) does both at once: it decarboxylates oxaloacetate and phosphorylates the product with GTP. Adding CO₂ costs energy, so look for ATP and biotin; removing it releases energy and needs neither. PEPCK is a lyase (EC 4.1.1.32) despite the “kinase” in its name — a second reason not to trust endings alone.
protease vs peptidase; endo- vs exo- Protease and peptidase are synonyms in ordinary use. Endopeptidase cuts an internal bond; exopeptidase nibbles from an end — aminopeptidase from the N terminus, carboxypeptidase from the C. The endo/exo prefix says where, never what. The same distinction is used for nucleases, and there it decides whether a fragment or a single nucleotide is released.
transaminase vs aminotransferase The same enzymes under two names — ALT is alanine aminotransferase, AST is aspartate aminotransferase. Class 2, PLP-dependent. No discrimination to make; the pair exists only to look like one. If a stem offers both, they are not the answer to a “which differs” item.
-ase on a substrate vs on a reaction Lactase, urease and ATPase are named for what they act on; hydrolase, transferase and oxidoreductase for what they do. Substrate-named enzymes tell you nothing about class — lactase is a hydrolase, ATPase may be a hydrolase or a translocase pump. Read the reaction.
Names with no -ase at all Trypsin, chymotrypsin, pepsin, thrombin, plasmin, papain, ficin — all proteases; lysozyme is a glycosidase. These predate the naming system. A stem that says “all enzymes end in -ase” or “all enzymes are proteins” is false as written — ribozymes catalyse real chemistry, including peptide-bond formation in the ribosome.

Synthase or synthetase

synthe-T-ase needs a Triphosphate

The standard hook, and the one worth carrying verbatim: the extra t is the T of NTP, so a synthetase couples joining to ATP or GTP hydrolysis and is a ligase, class 6, while a synthase needs no nucleotide — but its EC class is not fixed by the name; citrate synthase itself is EC 2.3.3.1, a transferase. Two cautions it does not cover, both of them examinable. First, the ending is a naming convention rather than a law: ATP synthase is a translocase (class 7), and fatty-acid synthase is a multi-enzyme complex whose name describes the product, not one reaction. Second, when a stem gives you the reaction instead of the name, classify from the chemistry and ignore the ending entirely. The longer treatment, with the same caveats, is in note 03 § 01.

§ 04

Enzyme vocabulary: parts, kinetics, inhibition, control

The ideaThis vocabulary splits cleanly into four questions, and knowing which question a word answers is half the battle. What is the enzyme made of? gives apoenzyme, holoenzyme, cofactor, coenzyme, prosthetic group. How fast does it go? gives Km, Vmax, kcat and the specificity constant. How is it slowed? gives the four inhibition patterns, which are told apart by geometry alone. How is it controlled on purpose? gives allostery, covalent modification and zymogen activation, which differ in timescale and in whether they can be undone. Words from different questions are never alternatives to each other, which quietly eliminates options.

Definition & the four questions

holoenzyme = apoenzyme (protein) + cofactor the apoenzyme alone is catalytically dead; “holo” is the working form
v₀ = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])Michaelis–Menten; at [S] = Km, v₀ = ½Vmax
Vmax = kcat[E]tso kcat is a property of one molecule, Vmax of the tube
1/v₀ = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/VmaxLineweaver–Burk: y-intercept 1/Vmax, x-intercept −1/Km

The three the paper actually swaps

Un-competitive is not non-competitive. One syllable, opposite plots. Uncompetitive binds ES only and gives parallel lines; noncompetitive binds E and ES equally and gives lines meeting on the x-axis. If the two prefixes blur, hang each on a word starting with its own letter: Untouched slope, No change in Km.

“Km decreased and Vmax decreased” is not a misprint. It is the uncompetitive signature. Removing ES from the equilibrium pulls E and S together, so half-maximal velocity arrives at a lower [S] even though the enzyme is less active overall. Rejecting that option on instinct loses the mark.

An irreversible inhibitor produces the pure non-competitive pattern. It deletes enzyme molecules; the survivors have a normal Km and there are fewer of them. Two things a kinetic plot cannot show separate them: activity does not return on dialysis, and inhibition deepens with pre-incubation time.

Mechanism-based (suicide) inhibitorA substrate analogue the enzyme itself converts into a reactive species that then kills itDistinguished from an ordinary irreversible inhibitor by needing the enzyme's own chemistry to work. 5-fluorouracil on thymidylate synthase
§ 05

Structure and stereochemistry

The ideaAlmost all of this vocabulary answers one question — how many stereocentres differ between these two molecules? All of them, and the molecules are mirror images: enantiomers. Some but not all: they are diastereomers. Exactly one: epimers. Exactly one, and it happens to be the carbon created when the sugar closed its ring: anomers. That is a nested hierarchy, not four unrelated definitions, and drawing it once as a hierarchy is worth more than memorising the four sentences. The second thread is that a molecule can carry two charges and still be neutral overall, which is why an amino acid is soluble in water, migrates in an electric field, and stops moving at exactly one pH.

Definition & the hierarchy

Chiral centre (stereocentre)
A carbon carrying four different substituents. A molecule containing one is chiral: not superimposable on its mirror image. n stereocentres give at most 2ⁿ stereoisomers — an aldohexose has four, hence sixteen, of which eight are D and eight are L.
Anomeric carbon
The carbonyl carbon that becomes a new stereocentre when the sugar cyclises — C1 of an aldose, C2 of a ketose. It did not exist as a stereocentre in the open chain, which is exactly why anomers can interconvert.
isomers → constitutional | stereoisomersdifferent connectivity, vs same connectivity and different arrangement in space
stereoisomers → enantiomers | diastereomersmirror images, vs everything else
diastereomers → epimers → anomersone centre differs → and that centre is the anomeric one

Is an anomer an epimer? — a place where usage is genuinely loose

Two anomers differ at exactly one stereocentre, so on the plain definition they are epimers, and Lehninger's own wording treats them that way. Some courses nevertheless reserve “epimer” for pairs differing at a non-anomeric centre, so that the two words never overlap. Both usages circulate. The safe answer under exam conditions is to name the more specific term: if the difference is at the anomeric carbon, say anomers; if it is anywhere else, say epimers. That answer is correct under either convention, and it is what a well-constructed option will say.

§ 06

Bonds and linkages

The ideaNearly every linkage in biology is made by squeezing out one water molecule and broken by putting it back, and each one is named after the atom the acyl carbon ended up bonded to. Land on nitrogen and it is an amide; on oxygen, an ester; on sulfur, a thioester. That single rule generates most of the table below, and it also explains the one entry that behaves differently: a thioester holds far more free energy than an ester, not because its bond is stronger but because sulfur is bad at stabilising the molecule it is part of. Two entries break the condensation pattern entirely and are worth flagging now — a disulfide is an oxidation, and a phosphoanhydride is charged on both sides of the join.

Definition & the generating rule

acyl carbon lands on N, O or S → amide, ester, thioester three rows of the table are one rule; the name follows the atom
Condensation vs hydrolysis
Condensation joins two groups and releases one H₂O; hydrolysis is the exact reverse. Almost every biological polymer is built and dismantled this way, which is why the classes of enzyme that do it — hydrolases — outnumber everything else in the digestive tract.
The two exceptions on this page
A disulfide is formed by oxidation of two thiols, not by condensation — no water leaves, and it is undone by reduction, not by a hydrolase. A phosphoanhydride is a condensation, but between two phosphates, and the product is destabilised by the charge repulsion between them.
Biological linkages: what forms each one and what breaks it

Three linkage traps

“Phosphodiester” does not mean two phosphates. It is one phosphate esterified twice. Two phosphates joined to each other is a phosphoanhydride, and the distinction decides whether the linkage is low energy (backbone) or high energy (ATP).

A disulfide is not made by condensation. It is an oxidation — no water leaves — and it is undone by a reducing agent, not by a hydrolase. Options that group it with the peptide and glycosidic bonds as “all formed by loss of water” are wrong on exactly this point.

Hemiacetal and acetal are the whole of “reducing sugar”. One –OR and a free –OH on the same carbon: it can reopen, so it reduces Benedict's reagent. Two –OR groups: it cannot, so it does not. Sucrose is non-reducing because the glycosidic bond runs anomeric-to-anomeric and caps both ends at once.

§ 07

Energetics

The ideaTwo symbols do almost all the work here, and confusing them is the commonest energetics error on a paper. ΔG°′ is a tabulated constant: it is the free-energy change measured with every participant at 1 M and pH 7, and it is nothing more than another way of writing the equilibrium constant — where the reaction settles if left alone, whatever concentrations it started from. ΔG is what is actually happening in the cell right now, and it depends on the concentrations the cell is holding. A reaction with a large positive ΔG°′ can run forwards all day if the cell keeps draining the product — and several important ones do. Everything else in this section is downstream of that one distinction, including why “high-energy bond” is a bad phrase and why a spontaneous reaction can be immeasurably slow.

Definition & the two free energies

ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q the tabulated value plus a correction for the concentrations actually present
ΔG°′ — standard transformed free-energy change
Measured with every solute at 1 M, at pH 7, 25 °C and 1 atm, with [H⁺] and [H₂O] left out of Q by convention (that is what the prime means). It carries exactly the same information as K′eq: ΔG°′ = −RT ln K′eq.
ΔG — actual free-energy change
The only one that decides direction. ΔG < 0 means the reaction proceeds as written under those conditions; at equilibrium ΔG is zero, and ΔG°′ is not.
Constants and the arithmetic shortcut
R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ · RT = 2.48 kJ·mol⁻¹ at 25 °Cand 2.58 at 37 °C
ΔG ≈ ΔG°′ + 5.71 × log₁₀ Q (kJ·mol⁻¹, 25 °C)every factor of ten in Q is worth 5.71 kJ·mol⁻¹
ΔG°′ = −nF ΔE°′ · F = 96.5 kJ·mol⁻¹·V⁻¹with ΔE°′ = E°′(acceptor) − E°′(donor)

§ 08

Pathway vocabulary

The ideaThese words describe a pathway's role rather than its chemistry, and each answers a specific question about the map. Which direction is it going — building or breaking? Does it do both at once? Does it top a cycle up, or drain it? Where is it controlled, and is that the same place as the slowest step? The pair that costs the most marks is committed against rate-limiting, because in glycolysis they happen to be the same enzyme and students then assume they always are. They are defined differently, and a stem can separate them.

Definition & the two that get conflated

Committed step
The first reaction whose product has no fate other than this pathway — the first irreversible step past the last branch point. It is a statement about the shape of the map. PFK-1 for glycolysis; acetyl-CoA carboxylase for fatty-acid synthesis.
Rate-limiting step
The step with the greatest control over flux — in practice the slowest, usually far from equilibrium. It is a statement about rates. Often the same enzyme as the committed step, because evolution puts the control where commitment happens, but the two are not the same idea and need not coincide.
control lives where a reaction is irreversiblea near-equilibrium step slides either way and cannot be a useful switch
opposing pathways must use different enzymes at those stepsotherwise one signal cannot switch the two directions independently

Committed, rate-limiting, regulated — three claims, not one

A stem will often name an enzyme and then offer all three words as separate options. Committed is about position on the map; rate-limiting is about flux control; regulated merely says something modulates it. PFK-1 is all three, which is why it is the example everyone learns and also why it is a poor guide. Hexokinase is regulated (product inhibition by glucose 6-phosphate) but not committed to glycolysis, because glucose 6-phosphate has four exits. Read which of the three claims the option is actually making.
