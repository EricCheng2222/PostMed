Physics · Note 02

Newton’s Laws, Forces and Friction

Note 01 described motion. This note explains it. The organising idea is small and ruthless: acceleration is caused by the vector sum of the forces that other bodies exert on this body, and by nothing else. Every result below — the incline, the Atwood machine, terminal velocity, the banked curve — is what you get when you isolate one body, add up the arrows, and set the sum equal to ma. The formulas are consequences of that one habit, not facts to memorise.

§ 01

Inertia and the inertial frame

The ideaNothing needs a push to keep going. It needs a push to change how it is going — that is the break with common sense, and it is why a puck sliding across ice eventually stops: the ice is rubbing on it, not because motion wears out. The rest of the section is about where you are standing while you watch. Some vantage points are honest ones, in that everything you see start, stop or turn was genuinely made to do so by something you could point at. Others — a bus braking hard, a spinning roundabout — throw things about for no visible reason, and every rule in this note is written for the honest kind.

Definition & formula

Force
An interaction between two bodies. It is a vector, it always has an identifiable other body as its source, and its effect is to change momentum.
ΣF = 0 ⟺ v = constant first law; true only in an inertial frame
Full definition, symbols and units
Inertia (mass)
The property of a body that quantifies how strongly it resists a change in its velocity. Operationally: apply the same force to two bodies and the accelerations are inversely proportional to their masses.
Inertial reference frame
A frame in which a body subject to no interactions moves in a straight line at constant speed. Newton’s first law asserts that such frames exist.
m₁ / m₂ = a₂ / a₁same force applied to each — the operational definition of mass
1 N = 1 kg·m·s⁻²the newton is a derived unit, not a fundamental one

Newton’s first law. There exist reference frames — call them inertial — in which a body with no net force on it keeps a constant velocity. Constant velocity means constant speed and constant direction; standing still is only the special case v = 0.

The conceptual break with Aristotle is worth stating explicitly, because it is exactly what an exam question probes: motion needs no cause; only a change of motion does. A puck gliding across ice slows down not because motion "runs out" but because friction is a force. Remove the force and the motion persists indefinitely.

Why the first law is not a special case of the second

Read as an equation, "ΣF = 0 ⇒ a = 0" really is a trivial corollary of ΣF = ma. But that reading throws away the law’s actual content, which is existential: it claims that a particular class of reference frames exists, and it is only in those frames that the second law is true.

Why does that matter? Because ΣF = ma on its own is unfalsifiable. Observe any acceleration you like and you can always "explain" it by declaring that some force must have been present. The first law closes that loophole. It says: there is a frame in which the only accelerations you ever have to account for are traceable to identifiable interactions with identifiable other bodies. Pick a frame that fails this test — a braking bus, a spinning carousel — and objects accelerate with no source body in sight, and ΣF = ma simply gives wrong answers unless you patch it (§ 11).

So the logical order is: first law defines the arena → second law is the law of motion inside it → third law constrains what forces are allowed to look like.

Trap · "no net force" ≠ "no motion"

A body moving in a circle at constant speed has changing velocity, so it is not in equilibrium and the net force on it is not zero — see § 10. Equally, a lift moving upward at a steady 2 m·s⁻¹ has zero net force on its passenger; the scale reads their full weight. Velocity and acceleration are independent.

Exam point · is the Earth’s surface inertial?

Almost. A laboratory on the rotating Earth accelerates toward the rotation axis at ω²R = (7.29×10⁻⁵)² × 6.37×10⁶ ≈ 0.034 m·s⁻² at the equator — about 0.35 % of g. For every problem in this note that is negligible, so we treat the ground as inertial. It stops being negligible for long-range ballistics, ocean currents and weather systems, where the Coriolis effect shows up.

§ 02

The second law as a vector equation

The ideaThis is the rule that turns a list of pushes and pulls into a prediction. Add up everything acting on one chosen object, directions included, and the total tells you how quickly its motion is changing and which way. Three things carry all the difficulty. The adding has to be done the way you combine directions on a map, not the way you add up a bill; only what is acting on that one object counts, never what it does to something else; and a heavier object changes its motion less for the same total, which is exactly what mass measures.

Definition & formula

Newton’s second law
The net force on a body is the rate of change of its momentum. For a body of constant mass this is mass times acceleration, and the acceleration is parallel to the net force.
ΣF = ma inertial frame, constant mass
Full definition, symbols and units
Net force
The vector sum of every force exerted on one chosen body by other bodies. Forces on different bodies never appear in the same sum.
ΣF = dp/dtgeneral form; the one to keep when the mass changes — but p must be that of a fixed set of particles (a rocket plus its exhaust)
ΣFx = max ; ΣFy = mayone independent scalar equation per axis

Everything hard about the second law is contained in three words: net, on one body, and vector.

  • Net. You add the arrows, not the magnitudes. Two 10 N forces at right angles give 14.1 N, not 20 N.
  • On one body. Choose the body first, then list only the forces other things exert on it. Mixing in the forces it exerts on other things is the single most common way dynamics problems go wrong (§ 3).
  • Vector. In two dimensions this is two scalar equations, which is why two unknowns are solvable. Choosing the axes intelligently — one axis along the known direction of a — is most of the work (§ 7).

Note also that a is parallel to ΣF, and to nothing else. It is not parallel to v. A ball thrown upward has a pointing down throughout, including at the instant it is momentarily at rest at the top. "Deceleration" is not a separate concept — it is simply a antiparallel to v.

Trap · ΣF = ma is not a definition of force

If it were, it would be empty. Its physical content comes from the fact that the forces on the left-hand side are given independently by force laws — mg for gravity, μkN for kinetic friction, −kx for a spring — and those same laws predict the motion of every other body too. The equation is a testable claim connecting two independently measurable things, not a naming convention.

Exam point · units and magnitude sense

1 N = 1 kg·m·s⁻². Useful anchors: a 1 kg object weighs 9.8 N; an adult of 70 kg weighs about 690 N; a 1 N force accelerates a 1 kg mass at 1 m·s⁻². Any answer that gives a person a weight of 70 N or an acceleration of 500 m·s⁻² is a sign-or-substitution error, not physics.

§ 03

The third law: interaction pairs

The ideaA push is never one-sided. Lean on a wall and the wall leans back on you just as hard, at the same instant, whether it is solid brick or about to fall down. That sounds as though nothing could ever get moving, since every push is matched by an equal one. The escape is that the two halves land on two different objects, so they never appear in the same tally and never cancel each other. It is also the answer to a question most people have never thought to ask: what actually pushes you forward when you walk? You push the floor backwards, and the floor pushes you forwards.

Definition & formula

Newton’s third law
Forces exist only as pairs belonging to a single interaction between two bodies: if A pushes or pulls B, then B pushes or pulls A with equal magnitude in the opposite direction.
FA→B = − FB→A always exactly true; no conditions, no exceptions in mechanics
Full definition, symbols and units
Third-law pair
The two halves of one interaction. They act on different bodies, are of the same type, and are exactly equal in magnitude at every instant, no matter what either body is doing.
FA→B + FB→A = 0but they never cancel, because they act on different bodies
FA→Bforce exerted by body A on body B (N)
FB→Aforce exerted by body B on body A (N)

A third-law pair shares four things and differs in one. Same magnitude, same line of action, same interaction type (gravity pairs with gravity, contact with contact), same instant — and different bodies. That last item is the whole story: because the two forces act on different bodies, they can never cancel in a single free-body diagram, and the world is not frozen.

The normal force is not the reaction to weight

This is the classic trap, and it is worth doing slowly. A book of mass m rests on a table. Two forces act on the book: gravity W = mg downward and the normal force N upward. They are equal and opposite. They are not a third-law pair.

Two independent tests separate the cases, and either one is enough:

  1. Whose diagram? A third-law pair never appears on the same free-body diagram. N and W both act on the book, so they cannot be a pair. They balance because of the second law: the book is not accelerating.
  2. Does the equality survive? A third-law pair is equal in magnitude under all circumstances. N = mg survives almost nothing: press down on the book and N > mg; put the table in a descending lift and N < mg; tilt the table and N = mg cos θ; let both fall and N = 0 while W is unchanged.

Two paradoxes that dissolve immediately

"If the cart pulls back on the horse as hard as the horse pulls the cart, nothing can ever move." Those two forces act on different bodies, so they belong to different equations. To find whether the cart accelerates, draw the cart only: the harness pulls it forward, friction and drag oppose. To find whether the horse accelerates, draw the horse only: the ground pushes it forward by friction, the harness pulls it back. The system accelerates because the ground’s friction on the horse’s hooves exceeds the resistance on the cart.

"What force pushes me forward when I walk?" You push backward on the ground with your foot; the ground pushes forward on you. The forward force on a walking human is friction from the floor. This is why a frictionless floor is unwalkable, and why the force that accelerates a car forward is a forward-pointing static friction force at the tyre contact patch.

One-line test

Write every force as "X on Y". The third-law partner is obtained by swapping the two names: "Y on X". If you cannot name X, it is not a force.

§ 04

Free-body diagrams, step by step

The ideaThis is the working method the rest of the note runs on, and it is deliberately dull. Choose one object. Draw it on its own, with everything around it rubbed out. Then add one arrow for each other thing that is touching it or pulling on it — and nothing else whatsoever: no arrow for where it is heading, none for how fast it is already going. What survives is a picture you can turn straight into equations, and most mistakes in this topic are mistakes in that picture rather than in the algebra that follows.

Definition & formula

Free-body diagram (FBD)
A drawing of one chosen body, removed from its surroundings, showing every force exerted on it by something else — and nothing else at all.
one body → one diagram → two equations in 2-D; solvable when unknowns ≤ equations
Full definition, symbols and units
System
Whatever you draw the boundary around. It may be one block, or several bodies treated as a single object, in which case all forces internal to the boundary drop out in pairs.
ΣFx = maxchoose x along the known direction of a
ΣFy = may = 0perpendicular to a; this is what determines N
Nnormal (contact) force (N)
ffriction force (N)
Tstring tension (N)
Wweight, mg (N)
θangle to a chosen axis (degrees or rad)

The recipe below is mechanical on purpose. Follow it and the algebra becomes bookkeeping.

  1. Choose the body. Say out loud which object you are drawing. If a problem has two blocks, you will usually need two diagrams.
  2. Redraw it alone, as a box or a dot, with the surroundings erased.
  3. Contact first. Walk around the boundary and ask what touches it? Every contact contributes a normal force perpendicular to the surface and, if the surfaces are rough, a friction force parallel to it. Every attached string contributes a tension along the string, away from the body.
  4. Then action-at-a-distance. In this note that means gravity: one mg arrow, straight down, from the centre of mass.
  5. Choose axes. Put one axis along the direction you know a lies in. On an incline that means tilting the axes; in circular motion it means radial and tangential.
  6. Write and count. ΣFx = max, ΣFy = may. Count unknowns. If there are more unknowns than equations, you are missing a constraint (an inextensible string, a surface the body stays on, a wheel that does not slip).

Trap · arrows that must never appear on an FBD

  • ma — that is the right-hand side of the equation, not a force. Drawing it is double counting.
  • mv²/r or "centripetal force" — a role played by real forces, never an extra one (§ 10).
  • "The force of motion" or "the force that keeps it going" — there is no such thing. That is the first law.
  • Centrifugal force — only legitimate in a rotating frame, and then it is a pseudo-force (§ 11). Never mix it with a ground-frame diagram.
  • Forces the body exerts on other things — those belong on the other body’s diagram.

Exam point · the axis choice is the marks

Graders and multiple-choice distractors both key on the same decision: which way did you point x? Point it along a. On an incline the acceleration is along the surface, so tilt the axes and resolve mg; in circular motion the acceleration is radial, so use radial/tangential axes. Resolving the weight instead of the normal force is what keeps the algebra to one line.

§ 05

Weight, normal force and tension

The ideaThree arrows turn up on almost every one of those pictures, and they behave very differently. The Earth’s pull on an object is steady and predictable. A table or a rope is not: neither has a set strength that it always supplies. Each gives back exactly as much as is needed to stop the object sinking through the surface, or to keep the rope taut, and no more. That is why a bathroom scale in a lift keeps changing its mind while the person standing on it has not changed at all — the scale is reporting the support, not the pull of the Earth. And because a table can only push and a rope can only pull, there are moments when they supply nothing at all: contact is lost, or the rope goes slack.

Definition & formula

Weight
The gravitational force the Earth exerts on a body: a vector of magnitude mg directed toward the Earth’s centre. It is a force, measured in newtons.

The component of a contact force perpendicular to the contacting surface. It is a constraint force: it takes on whatever value is required to stop the two bodies interpenetrating, and it can only push (N ≥ 0).
Tension
The pull a stretched string, rope or cable transmits along its own length, always directed away from the body and along the string.
Apparent weight
The magnitude of the normal (or supporting) force acting on a body — what a scale reads, and what your body’s pressure receptors report.

Mass versus weight

These are different kinds of quantity, and the confusion is baked into everyday language ("I weigh 62 kilos"). In physics, mass is the resistance to acceleration and the source of gravity; weight is the force gravity actually exerts here.

Note the phrasing in the last row of the table: an astronaut in orbit is not weightless in the sense of gravity being absent — at the altitude of the ISS, g is still about 89 % of its surface value. They are in free fall, so nothing supports them, so N = 0 and their apparent weight is zero.

Why the normal force is a constraint force

There is no force law for N. You cannot look it up the way you look up mg or μkN. Instead, N is whatever it has to be so that the body does not sink into the surface — that is, so that the acceleration perpendicular to the surface is zero. You therefore solve for it from ΣF⊥ = ma⊥, every single time.

That has one important consequence. Surfaces push but cannot pull, so N ≥ 0. The moment your algebra says the required N is negative, the physical constraint has failed and contact is lost — the body leaves the surface. That single condition, N = 0, is the entire content of the "minimum speed at the top of a loop" problem in § 10 and of a car going airborne over a hump.

Worked example 1 · The lift and the bathroom scale

A 70 kg person stands on a scale in a lift. The scale reads the normal force it exerts. Find the reading when the lift (a) is at rest or moving at constant velocity, (b) accelerates upward at 2.0 m·s⁻², (c) accelerates downward at 2.0 m·s⁻², (d) has its cable cut. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. One body: the person. Two forces on them — N up (from the scale) and mg down (from the Earth). Take up as positive.
  2. Second law, vertical: N − mg = may, so N = m(g + ay). The person’s acceleration is the lift’s acceleration, because they move together.
  3. (a) ay = 0: N = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N. Constant velocity gives the same answer as rest — speed is irrelevant.
  4. (b) ay = +2.0: N = 70 × (9.8 + 2.0) = 70 × 11.8 = 826 N, a 20 % increase. This is the pressed-into-the-floor sensation as a lift starts upward.
  5. (c) ay = −2.0: N = 70 × (9.8 − 2.0) = 70 × 7.8 = 546 N.
  6. (d) free fall, ay = −9.8: N = 70 × 0 = 0. Weight is unchanged at 686 N; it is the support that has vanished.
686 N · 826 N · 546 N · 0 N

Ideal strings and ideal pulleys

Two idealisations do a great deal of work, and each earns its keep for a reason you should be able to state:

  • Massless string ⇒ the tension is the same everywhere along it. Apply the second law to any short segment: T₂ − T₁ = msegmenta = 0 when the segment has no mass. If the rope is heavy — a climbing rope, a lift cable — the tension genuinely varies along its length, being largest at the top.
  • Inextensible string ⇒ a kinematic constraint. Bodies joined by a taut string have equal speeds along the string and equal acceleration magnitudes. This is the extra equation that makes two-body problems solvable.
  • Ideal pulley (massless, frictionless) ⇒ it redirects the tension without changing it. A real pulley has rotational inertia, so accelerating it requires a net torque, which requires T₁ ≠ T₂ on the two sides.
  • A string can only pull. Like N ≥ 0, this is a one-sided constraint: T ≥ 0. When the algebra demands negative tension, the string goes slack.

Trap · "the tension equals the hanging weight"

Only if the system is in equilibrium. Hang a 5.0 kg mass from a string and let it accelerate downward at 2.0 m·s⁻²: T = m(g − a) = 5.0 × 7.8 = 39 N, not 49 N. Similarly, a rope over a pulley with unequal masses has a tension strictly between the two weights (§ 8).

Exam point · g has two readings, and both are correct

g = 9.8 m·s⁻² is the free-fall acceleration; g = 9.8 N·kg⁻¹ is the gravitational field strength. They are the same number with the same dimensions, and using the second reading makes W = mg read naturally as "force per kilogram, times kilograms". Clinical aside: this is why traction, splint and sling calculations are done in newtons, while the patient’s chart records kilograms.

§ 06

Friction: static, kinetic, angle of repose

The ideaFriction has two moods, and this whole section is about telling them apart. While the two surfaces are still gripping one another, friction simply follows: push gently and it resists gently, push harder and it matches you exactly — up to a ceiling set by how hard the surfaces are pressed together. Push past that ceiling and things start to slide; friction stops following and settles at a roughly fixed value, usually a little below the ceiling it has just lost, which is why a stuck drawer flies open. So the first question to ask is never how big friction is, but whether it is sliding yet.

Definition & formula

Friction
The component of a contact force parallel to the surface, arising from adhesion and interlocking between the microscopic asperities that make up the real contact area.
fs ≤ μsN  ·  fk = μkN note the inequality on the left and the equality on the right
Full definition, symbols and units
Static friction
The friction acting when the surfaces are not sliding on one another. Like the normal force it is a constraint force: it takes whatever value prevents relative sliding, up to a ceiling.
Kinetic friction
The friction acting when the surfaces are sliding. Its magnitude is approximately constant and it is directed opposite to the relative sliding velocity of the surfaces.
Angle of repose
The steepest incline angle at which a body still does not slide.
fs,max = μsNonly at the instant of impending slipping
tan θr = μsangle of repose; independent of mass
μk ≤ μstrue for almost all pairs of surfaces

fkkinetic friction force (N)

Why one is an inequality and the other is not

Static friction is not a force law; it is a constraint, exactly like N. Push a heavy crate with 5 N and it does not move, so friction is 5 N. Push with 20 N and it still does not move, so friction is now 20 N. Friction has no opinion — it supplies whatever is needed to keep a = 0. What it cannot do is supply more than μsN. Hence fs ≤ μsN, and hence the correct procedure:

  1. Assume the body does not slide. Solve ΣF = 0 for the friction force required.
  2. Compare with the ceiling μsN. Compute N from the perpendicular equation — it is frequently not mg.
  3. If required ≤ ceiling, the assumption holds: the body is static and friction equals the required value. If required > ceiling, the body slides, and only now do you switch to fk = μkN and solve for a.

The two approximate laws, and why they work at all

Amontons–Coulomb friction says f ∝ N, independent of the apparent contact area and (roughly) of the sliding speed. Both claims look wrong until you realise that surfaces touch only at asperity tips. The real contact area is a tiny fraction of the apparent area, and it is that real area which grows in proportion to N. Doubling the footprint of a tyre halves the pressure and leaves the real contact area, and therefore the friction, unchanged. These are empirical approximations, not fundamental laws: they fail for very clean surfaces, very high pressures, and lubricated contacts.

Worked example 2 · Same force, opposite outcome — push versus pull

A 12 kg crate sits on a floor with μs = 0.50 and μk = 0.35. A 60 N force is applied at 20° to the horizontal. (a) Directed 20° below the horizontal (a push). (b) Directed 20° above the horizontal (a pull). Use sin 20° = 0.342, cos 20° = 0.940, g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Common quantities. mg = 12 × 9.8 = 117.6 N; F cos 20° = 60 × 0.940 = 56.4 N (horizontal); F sin 20° = 60 × 0.342 = 20.5 N (vertical).
  2. (a) Push. Vertical equation first: the vertical component points down, so N = mg + F sin 20° = 117.6 + 20.5 = 138.1 N. Ceiling: fs,max = 0.50 × 138.1 = 69.1 N.
  3. Compare: the required friction is 56.4 N, which is below 69.1 N. The crate does not move; friction is 56.4 N and a = 0.
  4. (b) Pull. Vertical equation: the vertical component now points up, so N = mg − F sin 20° = 117.6 − 20.5 = 97.1 N. Ceiling: fs,max = 0.50 × 97.1 = 48.6 N.
  5. Compare: 56.4 N required > 48.6 N available, so it slides. Switch to kinetic friction: fk = 0.35 × 97.1 = 34.0 N.
  6. Horizontal second law: a = (56.4 − 34.0)/12 = 22.4/12 = 1.87 m·s⁻².

The identical 60 N force leaves the crate motionless when pushed and accelerates it when pulled, purely because the vertical component changes N, and N sets the friction ceiling. This is why you pull a suitcase rather than push it.

(a) stationary, f = 56 N · (b) a = 1.9 m·s⁻²
Worked example 3 · Angle of repose

A coin rests on a flat board. The board is tilted slowly; the coin begins to slip at 22°. What is μs, and does the answer depend on the coin?

  1. Axes along and perpendicular to the board. At the instant of slipping the coin is still static but friction is at its ceiling.
  2. Perpendicular: N = mg cos θ.
  3. Along the slope: mg sin θ = fs,max = μsN = μs mg cos θ.
  4. Divide. m and g both cancel: tan θr = μs.
  5. Substitute: μs = tan 22° = 0.404.

Because the mass cancels, a heavy coin and a light coin of the same material slip at exactly the same angle. This is the cheapest possible measurement of μs, and it is also why a heap of dry sand always settles to the same cone angle regardless of how much sand you pour.

μs = tan 22° ≈ 0.40 · independent of mass

Trap · friction opposes relative sliding, not motion

The force that accelerates a car forward is static friction from the road, pointing forward. The tyre’s contact patch tries to slide backward, so friction acts forward on it. Likewise the friction on a box sitting on an accelerating truck bed points forward — it is the only horizontal force available to accelerate the box. Asking "which way would the surfaces slide if friction vanished?" gets the direction right every time.

Exam point · the three sentences worth memorising verbatim

(1) fs ≤ μsN — an inequality; equality holds only at impending slipping. (2) N is almost never mg unless the surface is horizontal and no other vertical force acts. (3) μ is dimensionless and can exceed 1 (racing tyres reach ≈ 1.5).

§ 07

The inclined plane

The ideaA slope is where everything so far gets its first serious workout. What makes it feel harder than flat ground is only this: gravity no longer points neatly along the direction of travel or square across it, but somewhere in between, so it has to be split into a share that drags the object down the slope and a share that presses it into the surface. Line your bookkeeping up with the slope rather than with the horizon and the problem falls apart into two easy halves. Steepen the slope and the dragging share grows while the pressing share shrinks, which is precisely why there is an angle at which things begin to slip.

Definition & formula

Inclined plane
A flat surface making an angle θ with the horizontal. The physical content is a constraint: a body in contact with it can only accelerate parallel to the surface, so the perpendicular acceleration is zero.
a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ) block sliding down a rough incline; positive means down-slope
Full definition, symbols and units
Resolution along the surface
Choosing the x-axis along the slope and the y-axis along the surface normal, so that ay = 0 and the two equations decouple.
N = mg cos θperpendicular equation; no other perpendicular forces
mg sin θthe down-slope driving component of the weight
a = g sin θfrictionless limit
it slides ⟺ tan θ > μsreleased from rest, no other applied force
a = g(sin θ + μk cos θ)block moving up the slope: friction now acts down-slope as well, so the (still down-slope-positive) acceleration is larger — a deceleration
θincline angle from the horizontal (°)
Nnormal force (N)
μs, μkfriction coefficients (dimensionless)
aacceleration along the slope (m·s⁻²)
g9.8 m·s⁻²

Why the axes get tilted

You already know the direction of the acceleration: the block stays on the plane, so a is parallel to the surface. Point the x-axis that way and the y-equation immediately becomes ΣFy = 0, which hands you N for free. Use horizontal/vertical axes instead and both components of a are non-zero, both equations couple, and you do three times the algebra for the same answer.

The price is that the weight, which is not along either axis, must be resolved. The geometry that everyone half-remembers is this: the angle between mg and the inward surface normal is the same θ as the incline angle, because tilting the surface by θ tilts its normal by θ. Hence the component perpendicular to the surface carries the cosine and the component along it carries the sine.

Sanity checks that catch a sine/cosine swap instantly

  • θ → 0 (flat). N = mg cos 0 = mg ✓ and the driving force mg sin 0 = 0 ✓. If your formula gives N = 0 on flat ground, you have swapped them.
  • θ → 90° (vertical wall). N = 0 (the wall cannot support it) and a = g ✓ — free fall.
  • Mass cancels in a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ). If m survives in your acceleration, you have made an algebra error.
Worked example 4 · Block released on a rough 30° incline

A 4.0 kg block is placed on a 30° incline with μs = 0.40 and μk = 0.30 and released from rest. (a) Does it slide? (b) Find N and a. (c) How fast is it moving after sliding 2.0 m? cos 30° = 0.866, g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. (a) Slip test. Compare tan θ with μs: tan 30° = 0.577 > 0.40, so the required static friction exceeds the ceiling and the block slides. (Equivalently: the angle of repose here is arctan 0.40 = 21.8°, and 30° is steeper.)
  2. (b) Perpendicular equation. N = mg cos θ = 4.0 × 9.8 × 0.866 = 33.9 N. Note this is well below mg = 39.2 N.
  3. Kinetic friction. fk = μkN = 0.30 × 33.9 = 10.2 N, acting up the slope.
  4. Along-slope equation. mg sin θ − fk = ma → 19.6 − 10.2 = 4.0 a → a = 2.35 m·s⁻². Check against the formula: g(sin θ − μkcos θ) = 9.8(0.500 − 0.260) = 2.35 ✓.
  5. (c) Constant acceleration (friction and the slope are both constant, so the note-01 kinematics apply): v² = 2as = 2 × 2.35 × 2.0 = 9.41, so v = 3.07 m·s⁻¹.
N = 34 N · a = 2.4 m·s⁻² down-slope · v = 3.1 m·s⁻¹

Trap · friction flips when the direction of travel flips

Throw the same block up the slope and friction now acts down-slope, giving a deceleration of g(sin θ + μk cos θ) = 9.8(0.500 + 0.260) = 7.45 m·s⁻² — more than three times the magnitude of the downward acceleration. So the trip up is much shorter than the trip down, and the block returns to the bottom slower than it left. A separate question is whether it comes back at all: once it stops it will only restart if tan θ > μs, which here it does.

Exam point · the incline in three numbers

N = mg cos θ · mg sin θ is the driving component · tan θr = μs is the slip threshold. Almost every incline question in a first-year paper is one of these three wearing a costume, and the mass cancels in the last two.

§ 08

Connected bodies and the Atwood machine

The ideaTwo objects tied together are not free to do as they please: a rope that will not stretch forces them to speed up and slow down in step. That one extra fact is what makes these problems solvable, and leaving it out is what makes them look impossible. There are two ways in. Treat the whole assembly as a single lump and the rope stops mattering, which gets you the change of motion quickly but tells you nothing about the rope itself. Or draw each object separately, which takes longer but is the only route to finding out how hard the rope is being pulled.

Definition & formula

Connected system
Two or more bodies linked by an inextensible string, so that their motions are related by a kinematic constraint rather than being independent.
a = (m₂ − m₁)gm₁ + m₂ Atwood machine, ideal string and pulley; m₂ descends
Full definition, symbols and units
Atwood machine
Two masses hanging from the ends of a light string passed over an ideal pulley. Its purpose is to produce an acceleration much smaller than g, so that it can be measured with a clock.
System method
Treating several connected bodies as one object. Every internal force (the tension) appears twice, in a third-law pair, and cancels — leaving only external forces.
T = 2m₁m₂gm₁ + m₂same tension throughout the ideal string
a = ΣFexternalΣmsystem method; gives a but never gives T
|a₁| = |a₂|the inextensibility constraint — the extra equation you need
a = m₂g − μkm₁gm₁ + m₂m₁ on a rough horizontal table, m₂ hanging
m₁, m₂the two connected masses (kg)
acommon acceleration magnitude (m·s⁻²)
Tstring tension (N)
μkcoefficient of kinetic friction at the table (dimensionless)

Two methods, and when each is the right tool

The system method draws one boundary around everything. Internal tensions are third-law pairs inside the boundary, so they cancel, and you are left with a = ΣFext/Σm in a single line. It is fast, and it is the only sensible way to get a. Its limitation is exactly its strength: because the tension cancelled, the system method can never tell you what the tension is.

The per-body method draws a separate FBD for each mass, giving one equation each, plus the constraint |a₁| = |a₂|. Use it whenever a question asks for a tension, a contact force between two blocks, or "what does the string have to withstand".

The sign bookkeeping is where marks are lost. The clean approach: define one positive sense that runs along the string — say, "up on the left-hand side, over the pulley, down on the right-hand side" — and use it consistently for both bodies. Then a single scalar a describes the whole system, and no absolute-value signs are needed.

Worked example 5 · Atwood machine, then the same string on a rough table

Part A. An ideal Atwood machine carries m₁ = 3.0 kg and m₂ = 5.0 kg. Find a, T, and the force the pulley’s axle must supply. g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Positive sense: m₂ down, m₁ up. Then both bodies have the same scalar acceleration a.
  2. System method for a: the external forces along that sense are m₂g forward and m₁g backward, so a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁+m₂) = (2.0 × 9.8)/8.0 = 2.45 m·s⁻².
  3. Per-body for T — use the lighter block: T − m₁g = m₁a → T = m₁(g + a) = 3.0 × 12.25 = 36.75 N.
  4. Cross-check on the heavier block: m₂g − T = m₂a → T = 5.0 × (9.8 − 2.45) = 5.0 × 7.35 = 36.75 N ✓. The tension lies strictly between the two weights, 29.4 N and 49.0 N, as it must.
  5. The axle holds up two string segments, so it supplies 2T = 73.5 N — which is less than the total weight of 8.0 × 9.8 = 78.4 N. That is not an error: the system’s centre of mass is accelerating downward, so the net external force on it must point down.

Part B. Now put m₁ = 2.0 kg on a horizontal table with μk = 0.25, connected over a pulley at the table edge to m₂ = 3.0 kg hanging. Find a and T.

  1. Normal force on the table block: N = m₁g = 19.6 N, so fk = 0.25 × 19.6 = 4.9 N, opposing its motion.
  2. System method: external driving force m₂g = 29.4 N, external resisting force fk = 4.9 N, total mass 5.0 kg → a = (29.4 − 4.9)/5.0 = 4.90 m·s⁻².
  3. Tension from the table block: T − fk = m₁a → T = 4.9 + 2.0 × 4.9 = 14.7 N.
  4. Check with the hanging block: m₂g − T = 29.4 − 14.7 = 14.7 N, and m₂a = 3.0 × 4.9 = 14.7 N ✓.
A: a = 2.45 m·s⁻², T = 36.8 N, axle 73.5 N · B: a = 4.90 m·s⁻², T = 14.7 N

Limits worth checking

Equal masses

m₁ = m₂ = m gives a = 0 and T = mg — static equilibrium, as it must be.

One mass vanishing

m₁ → 0 gives a → g and T → 0: the heavy block is in free fall with a slack string.

One mass enormous

m₂ ≫ m₁ gives a → g and T → 2m₁g — the light block is yanked upward at g, needing twice its own weight.

Trap · a real pulley breaks the equal-tension rule

"The tension is the same on both sides" follows from the pulley being massless and frictionless. Give the pulley a moment of inertia and you need a net torque to angularly accelerate it, which requires T₂ > T₁. Any question that mentions the pulley’s mass or radius is signalling that this idealisation has been withdrawn — that is a rotational-dynamics problem, not this one.

Exam point · the constraint is a real equation

Two bodies give two equations and typically three unknowns (a₁, a₂, T). The inextensible string supplies the third: |a₁| = |a₂|. If a problem seems to have one unknown too many, the missing equation is almost always a geometric constraint — a string that does not stretch, a surface the body stays on, or a wheel that rolls without slipping.

§ 09

Drag and terminal velocity

The ideaAir and water push back on anything moving through them, and unlike the rubbing of the earlier sections, that push grows the faster you go. A falling object is therefore in a race it always loses: it speeds up, the resistance climbs, and it goes on climbing until it exactly matches the object’s weight — after which nothing changes and the fall continues at a steady speed. Finding that steady speed is the point of the section, and it needs no difficult mathematics, because at that moment nothing is speeding up at all. Which description of the resistance applies depends on how big and how fast the object is: a skydiver and a blood cell settling in a test tube sit at opposite ends of the same story.

Definition & formula

Drag
The force a fluid exerts on a body moving through it, directed opposite to the body’s velocity relative to the fluid. Unlike friction between solids, its magnitude depends strongly on speed.
m dv/dt = mg − Fd(v) → 0 as v → vt terminal velocity is where the drag force equals the weight

Drag is the first force in this note whose magnitude depends on the velocity. That has an immediate consequence you must internalise: the acceleration is not constant, so the constant-acceleration kinematics of note 01 do not apply. You cannot write v = v₀ + at for a falling raindrop. What you can do without any calculus is find the terminal velocity, because that is a statics problem: set the net force to zero.

Terminal velocity is an attractor, not a speed limit reached at a particular instant. Start below it and drag is too small, so you speed up; start above it (a diver pulling out of a dive) and drag exceeds the weight, so you slow down. In the linear regime the approach is exponential with time constant τ = m/b, so after 3τ you are within 5 % of vt.

Worked example 6 · A skydiver, and a red cell settling in plasma

Part A — quadratic regime. A 75 kg skydiver falls spread-eagle with A = 0.70 m², Cd = 0.70, in air of density ρ = 1.2 kg·m⁻³. Find the terminal speed.

  1. Set drag equal to weight: ½ρCdAvt² = mg, so vt = √(2mg / ρCdA).
  2. Numerator: 2mg = 2 × 75 × 9.8 = 1470 N. Denominator: ρCdA = 1.2 × 0.70 × 0.70 = 0.588.
  3. Divide and take the root: 1470/0.588 = 2500, so vt = √2500 = 50 m·s⁻¹ — about 180 km·h⁻¹, which is the standard belly-to-earth figure (real skydivers measure 50–55 m·s⁻¹).
  4. Scaling check: because vt ∝ √(m/A), a head-down dive that halves A multiplies the terminal speed by √2, not by 2.

Part B — linear regime, and why haematologists care. Model a red blood cell as a sphere of equivalent radius r = 2.8 μm with density excess ρs − ρf = 75 kg·m⁻³ over plasma of viscosity η = 1.5×10⁻³ Pa·s. Estimate its settling speed.

  1. Balance weight minus buoyancy against Stokes drag; the standard result is vt = 2r²(ρs − ρf)g / 9η.
  2. Numerator: 2 × (2.8×10⁻⁶)² × 75 × 9.8 = 2 × 7.84×10⁻¹² × 735 = 1.15×10⁻⁸.
  3. Denominator: 9 × 1.5×10⁻³ = 1.35×10⁻².
  4. Divide: vt = 8.5×10⁻⁷ m·s⁻¹. Multiply by 3600 s: 3.1×10⁻³ m·h⁻¹ = 3.1 mm·h⁻¹.
  5. Interpret. That is the erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and 3 mm·h⁻¹ sits squarely in the normal range. Because vt ∝ r², inflammation raises the ESR dramatically: acute-phase proteins such as fibrinogen make cells stack into rouleaux, and an aggregate of effective radius three times larger settles roughly nine times faster.
A: vt ≈ 50 m·s⁻¹ · B: vt ≈ 3.1 mm·h⁻¹

Trap · scaling in the quadratic regime

Doubling the mass does not double the terminal speed; it multiplies it by √2 ≈ 1.41, because drag grows as v². The same square root is why a mouse survives a fall that kills a horse: mass scales as L³ while frontal area scales as L², so vt ∝ √(L³/L²) = √L — small animals simply have low terminal speeds.

Exam point · which drag law, and why it matters clinically

Anything cell-sized, micron-sized or slow is Stokes; anything person-sized or fast is quadratic. The Stokes branch underwrites three things you will meet again: the ESR, centrifugal separation of blood components (§ 11), and aerosol deposition in the airways — particles above ~5 μm sediment out in the upper airway, while particles of 1–3 μm settle slowly enough to reach the alveoli, which is precisely the target window for inhaled drugs.

§ 10

Dynamics of uniform circular motion

The ideaGoing round a bend at a steady speed still counts as changing your motion, because your direction keeps changing. Something has to be doing that, and the real content of this section is working out what: the grip of a road, the pull of a string, the tilt of a banked track, gravity itself. There is no extra going-round-in-a-circle push to add to the list — the ordinary ones are simply taking on the job. And because you know in advance that the path is a circle, you know how strong the total inward pull has to be before you have written anything down, which is the reverse of every other problem in this note.

Definition & formula

Uniform circular motion
Motion on a circular path at constant speed. The velocity changes direction continuously, so the body is accelerating even though the speed is constant.
ΣFradial = m v²/r = m ω²r toward the centre; uniform circular motion
Full definition, symbols and units

The acceleration of a body in circular motion, directed toward the centre of the circle, of magnitude v²/r.
Centripetal force
Not a kind of force. It is the name for the role played by the net radial component of the real forces — tension, gravity, normal force, friction — that happen to point toward the centre.

The banked curve

A car turns because something pushes it toward the centre of the turn. On a flat road that something is friction, and friction is unreliable — rain, ice, worn tyres. Banking the road makes the normal force do the job instead, and the normal force never runs out.

Set up axes horizontally and vertically (not along the slope — the acceleration is horizontal here, which is exactly why the incline’s tilted axes are the wrong choice). With no friction there are only two forces:

vertical: N cos θ − mg = 0 ⇒ N = mgcos θno vertical acceleration
horizontal: N sin θ = mv²/rthe whole net force
divide: tan θ = v²rgmass cancels — the design speed is the same for a lorry and a motorbike
Go deeper · the conical pendulum, and why the string can never reach horizontal

The conical pendulum

A bob on a string of length L swept round a horizontal circle at half-angle θ is the same algebra with T in place of N: T cos θ = mg vertically and T sin θ = mv²/r radially, with r = L sin θ. Dividing gives v² = gr tan θ, and substituting for r and converting to a period gives Tperiod = 2π√(L cos θ / g). Two features are examinable: the mass cancels, and θ can approach but never reach 90°, because T cos θ must always find enough vertical component to hold up the weight.

The vertical loop, and the meaning of "minimum speed"

At the top of a vertical circle both the weight and the string tension (or the track’s normal force, for a rider on the inside of a loop) point downward — toward the centre. So

T + mg = mv²/r  ⇒  T = m(v²/r − g)top of the loop

A string can only pull and a track can only push, so T ≥ 0 and N ≥ 0. Setting the limiting value to zero gives vmin = √(gr). Below that speed the required centripetal force is less than the weight gravity is already supplying, so the body leaves the circular path — the string goes slack, the rider falls away from the track. Note the physical meaning: at vmin gravity alone provides exactly the needed mv²/r, and the rider is momentarily in free fall.

Two important riders. First, this applies to a string or an inner track surface, which can only pull inward or push inward. A rigid rod or a tube can also push outward, so a bead in a tube can go round arbitrarily slowly, even stopping at the top. Second, at the bottom of the loop the two forces oppose: N − mg = mv²/r, so N = m(g + v²/r) and the apparent weight is larger than mg.

Worked example 7 · Three circular-motion problems with one method

(a) Banked curve. A bend of radius 80 m is banked at 15°. What is the design speed, and what is the fastest safe speed if μs = 0.35? tan 15° = 0.268.

  1. v² = rg tan θ = 80 × 9.8 × 0.268 = 784 × 0.268 = 210, so v = 14.5 m·s⁻¹ = 52 km·h⁻¹. At exactly this speed no friction at all is needed.
  2. With friction available up the slope: vmax² = rg(tan θ + μs)/(1 − μstan θ) = 784 × 0.618/0.906 = 535, so vmax = 23.1 m·s⁻¹ = 83 km·h⁻¹. Friction more than doubles the kinetic energy the bend can tolerate.

(b) Conical pendulum. A 0.20 kg bob on a 1.2 m string sweeps a cone of half-angle 30°. Find the tension, the speed and the period. cos 30° = 0.866, tan 30° = 0.577.

  1. T = mg/cos θ = (0.20 × 9.8)/0.866 = 1.96/0.866 = 2.26 N.
  2. r = L sin θ = 1.2 × 0.500 = 0.60 m, and v² = gr tan θ = 9.8 × 0.60 × 0.577 = 3.39 → v = 1.84 m·s⁻¹.
  3. Tperiod = 2π√(L cos θ/g) = 2π√(1.039/9.8) = 2π × 0.326 = 2.05 s. Cross-check: 2πr/v = 2π × 0.60/1.84 = 2.05 s ✓.

(c) Vertical loop. A ball on a string is whirled in a vertical circle of radius 5.0 m. Find the minimum speed at the top, and the tension at the bottom if the ball only just makes it round (ignore air resistance).

  1. vtop = √(gr) = √(9.8 × 5.0) = √49 = 7.0 m·s⁻¹.
  2. Energy from top to bottom, falling a height 2r = 10 m: vbot² = vtop² + 2g(2r) = 49 + 196 = 245 m²·s⁻².
  3. At the bottom: T − mg = mvbot²/r → T = m(9.8 + 245/5.0) = m(9.8 + 49) = 58.8m = 6.0 mg.

That last result is worth remembering: a minimum-speed vertical loop produces exactly 6 g at the bottom, independent of the radius and the mass. It is why loop-the-loop rollercoasters are built as teardrop shapes (a smaller radius at the top) rather than circles — a circular loop fast enough to be safe at the top is punishing at the bottom, and sustained +6 Gz pushes a rider toward greyout and loss of cerebral perfusion.

(a) 14.5 → 23.1 m·s⁻¹ · (b) T = 2.3 N, v = 1.8 m·s⁻¹, period 2.0 s · (c) 7.0 m·s⁻¹, T = 6mg

Trap · never draw mv²/r on the diagram

mv²/r belongs on the right-hand side of ΣF = ma. Adding it as an arrow among the forces counts the same physics twice and usually produces the wrong sign as well. Ask instead: which real body is pushing or pulling this one toward the centre? If you cannot name it, the object is not going in a circle.

Exam point · the top of the loop, in one line

At the top, both forces point down because down is toward the centre: N + mg = mv²/r. Setting N = 0 gives vmin = √(gr). The common wrong answer is √(2gr), which comes from an energy argument through a height r and answers a different question entirely.

§ 11

Non-inertial frames and pseudo-forces

The ideaEverything up to here has been described by someone standing on solid ground. Sometimes it is far easier to describe things from inside the lift, the turning car or the spinning rotor — but seen from in there, objects lurch about with nothing visible causing it. You can go on using the rules of this note in such a place, at one price: to every object you must add an extra invented push, pointing the opposite way to how the whole room is speeding up. Nothing is exerting it, and it disappears the moment you step outside — but it is honest bookkeeping, and it is what lets a centrifuge behave like a very powerful version of gravity.

Definition & formula

Non-inertial frame
A reference frame that is itself accelerating (linearly or rotationally) with respect to an inertial frame. In it, a body with no forces on it appears to accelerate.
ΣFreal + (−mA) = ma′ frame accelerating with A; a′ is measured in that frame
Full definition, symbols and units
Pseudo-force (inertial force, fictitious force)
The term −mA that must be added to the real forces to make ΣF = ma′ come out right in an accelerating frame. It is a correction for the frame’s own acceleration, not an interaction between bodies.
Centrifugal force
The pseudo-force appearing in a rotating frame, directed radially outward, of magnitude mω²r.

Everything so far has been written in a frame fixed to the ground, which we argued in § 1 is inertial to about 0.35 %. Sometimes it is far more convenient to work in a frame that is accelerating — inside the lift, inside the turning car, inside the centrifuge rotor — because in that frame the object of interest is at rest. The price of that convenience is that ΣF = ma stops being true, and you have to buy it back by adding a term.

The rule is short. If the frame accelerates with A, then for every body of mass m in that frame you add a pseudo-force −mA, and then proceed exactly as usual. Nothing else changes.

The lift, done again from inside

Take worked example 1 again, but stand in the lift. The lift accelerates upward with A = +2.0 m·s⁻², so we add a downward pseudo-force of magnitude mA to the person. In the lift frame they are at rest, so a′ = 0:

N − mg − mA = 0 ⇒ N = m(g + A) = 70 × 11.8 = 826 Nidentical to the ground-frame answer, as it must be

The two frames must agree, because N is a physical reading on a physical scale. What the pseudo-force buys is a change of viewpoint: inside the lift it feels exactly as if gravity had strengthened to geff = 11.8 m·s⁻². That is a genuine and useful equivalence — plumb lines hang along geff, water surfaces lie perpendicular to it, and pendulums swing through small angles with period 2π√(L/geff).

Push the idea to the limit: in free fall A = −g, so the pseudo-force is +mg upward and cancels the weight exactly, for every body, regardless of mass. That exact cancellation is possible only because gravitational force is proportional to the same mass that appears in ma. It is the observation Einstein elevated to the equivalence principle, and it is why "zero gravity" in orbit is really "free fall with a good view".

Why the centrifugal force is bookkeeping, not an interaction

Sit in a car taking a left-hand bend and you are thrown against the right-hand door. It is tempting to say an outward force pushed you. In the ground frame there is no such force. What happens is: your body tends to continue in a straight line (first law), the car curves left into you, and the door pushes you inward — that inward push is the only horizontal force on you, and it supplies your mv²/r. The sensation of being thrown outward is the sensation of your own inertia being overcome.

In the car’s rotating frame you are motionless, which requires the forces to balance, so we add an outward mω²r to cancel the door’s inward push. That term is a bookkeeping entry that restores the form of the second law in a frame where it does not naturally hold. Test it against the criteria for a real force:

Trap · centripetal and centrifugal are not a third-law pair

They are the same physics described in two different frames, and they must never appear together. Choose one: either work in the ground frame, where the net inward force equals mv²/r and there is no centrifugal force at all; or work in the rotating frame, where the body is in equilibrium because the real inward force is balanced by the outward pseudo-force. Writing "the centripetal force balances the centrifugal force" is the single most common conceptual error in this whole topic — if they balanced, the object would travel in a straight line.

The centrifuge: a machine built out of a pseudo-force

Inside the rotor, the pseudo-force per unit mass is ω²r, which plays exactly the role of g — it drives sedimentation in the same way gravity does, only thousands of times harder. Laboratory practice quotes it as the relative centrifugal field, RCF = ω²r/g, in multiples of g, precisely because the rate law is the Stokes result from § 9 with g replaced by ω²r.

Worked example 8 · Relative centrifugal field

(a) A benchtop centrifuge spins at 3000 rpm with a rotor radius of 0.10 m. What is the RCF? (b) A high-speed rotor protocol calls for 15 000 × g at a radius of 0.080 m. What rotor speed is needed?

  1. Convert rpm to rad·s⁻¹: ω = 3000 × 2π/60 = 100π = 314 rad·s⁻¹. (Forgetting this conversion is the classic error; degrees and revolutions have no place in a = ω²r.)
  2. Centripetal acceleration: ω²r = (314)² × 0.10 = 9.87×10⁴ × 0.10 = 9.87×10³ m·s⁻².
  3. Divide by g: RCF = 9.87×10³ / 9.8 ≈ 1.0×10³ — a field of about 1000 g, enough to pack red cells in a few minutes.
  4. (b) Invert: ω = √(RCF × g / r) = √(15 000 × 9.8 / 0.080) = √(1.84×10⁶) = 1.36×10³ rad·s⁻¹.
  5. Back to rpm: 1355 × 60/(2π) ≈ 1.3×10⁴ rpm.

Note the strong dependence on speed: RCF ∝ ω², so doubling the rpm quadruples the field. This is why protocols always specify × g rather than rpm — the same rpm in a rotor of different radius gives a different field.

Go deeper · the rotating Earth, and where the Coriolis term actually matters

The Earth, honestly

The ground beneath you is a rotating frame, so a strictly correct ground-frame analysis carries two pseudo-forces. The centrifugal one contributes ω²R = 0.034 m·s⁻² outward at the equator, which is why measured g is about 9.780 m·s⁻² at the equator against 9.832 m·s⁻² at the poles (the remainder of that difference comes from the equatorial bulge). Because the centrifugal term is simply folded into the measured value of g, it never has to be written down separately, which is exactly why we could ignore it for ten sections.

The Coriolis term −2mω × v′ cannot be absorbed that way, because it depends on the body’s velocity. It is negligible on the scale of a laboratory but it dominates on the scale of the atmosphere, deflecting winds to the right in the northern hemisphere and producing the rotation of cyclones. It does not determine which way your sink drains — that is set by the basin’s shape and residual swirl, which are orders of magnitude larger.

Exam point · decide the frame before you draw

Write at the top of the diagram which frame you are in, and stay in it. Ground frame: real forces only, and the acceleration is v²/r or alift. Accelerating frame: real forces plus −mA, and the body of interest is usually at rest. Both give identical, correct answers. Mixing them gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of two or by a sign, which is precisely what the distractors are built from.

The whole note in one sentence

Pick one body, list only the forces other bodies exert on it, point an axis along the acceleration, and set the sum equal to ma — N and fs are whatever the constraints demand, everything else you look up.
