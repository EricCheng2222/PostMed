Physics · Note 01

Kinematics: Motion in One and Two Dimensions

Kinematics has exactly one organising idea: position, velocity and acceleration are linked by slope and area, and that link runs in both directions. Differentiate downward (x → v → a), integrate upward (a → v → x). Every formula on this page — including all four constant-acceleration equations, the whole of projectile motion, and centripetal acceleration — is that one relationship applied to a particular case. Learn the relationship and the formulas become consequences; learn the formulas alone and you will misuse them the first time the acceleration is not constant.

§ 01

Position, displacement, and the sign convention

The ideaPhysics has to answer two different questions about a moving thing, and it is easy to mistake them for one. The first is where it ended up compared with where it began; the second is how much ground it covered getting there. Walk one lap of a running track and you have covered a lot of ground but you are standing exactly where you started, so the two answers are not even close. This section sets up the bookkeeping that keeps them apart — and it starts with a decision you have to make yourself, before any sums: which way along your line counts as forward.

Definition & formula

Position x
A signed coordinate of an object measured from a chosen origin along a chosen axis. It is a label for where, not for how far travelled.
Δx = xfinal − xinitial always; the axis direction must be fixed first
Full definition, symbols and units
Displacement Δx
The vector from the starting position to the finishing position: final minus initial. It depends only on the two endpoints.
Distance (path length)
The scalar total length of the path actually traced out. It can only increase.
distance ≥ |Δx|equality only if the motion never reverses
|Δr| = Δx² + Δy²two dimensions
xposition along the axis (m)
Δxdisplacement, signed (m)
Δrdisplacement vector in 2-D (m)

One-dimensional motion works because a vector along a line needs only a sign to specify its direction. That is the whole trick: choose an axis, declare which way is positive, and every vector quantity in the problem — displacement, velocity, acceleration, later force and momentum — collapses to a signed number. Every one of them must then be read against the same axis. Mixing them (up-positive for velocity, down-positive for gravity) is the single most common source of wrong answers in this chapter.

Notice what the definition of displacement excludes. It does not know about the route, the detours or the time taken. Walk a 400 m lap of a running track and your displacement is zero while your distance is 400 m. That asymmetry propagates: average velocity is built on displacement, average speed on distance, and they are therefore genuinely different numbers, not two names for one thing.

Trap · the axis is a choice, and it is yours

There is no physically correct direction for "positive". Down-positive is perfectly legal for a falling object and makes a = +g. What is not legal is changing convention halfway through, or writing a = −9.8 while also writing the initial downward velocity as a positive number. Write the axis on your page before you write any equation.

§ 02

Velocity and speed: average vs instantaneous

The ideaThere are two honest answers to "how fast was it going", and they are answers to different questions. One is the whole-journey summary: this much progress in this much time, which deliberately forgets everything that happened in between. The other is what a speedometer shows at one moment. A car that crawls through town and then flies down the motorway has a single unremarkable summary number and a very eventful moment-by-moment story, and swapping one for the other is where most of the marks in this section are lost.

Definition & formula

Average velocity
Displacement divided by the elapsed time — the single constant velocity that would have produced the same net displacement in the same time.
v̄ = Δx / Δt    v = limΔt→0 Δx/Δt = dx/dt definitions; valid for any motion whatsoever
Full definition, symbols and units
Instantaneous velocity
The limit of the average velocity as the time interval shrinks to zero; equivalently, the slope of the tangent to the position–time graph.
Speed
The magnitude of the instantaneous velocity (a scalar). Average speed, however, is path length over time — which is not the magnitude of the average velocity.
average speed = total path lengthΔtscalar; ≥ |v̄|
Δx = ∫ v dtthe area under the v–t curve
v̄average velocity (m·s⁻¹)
vinstantaneous velocity (m·s⁻¹)
Δxdisplacement (m)
Δtelapsed time (s)

Average velocity is a summary; it deliberately throws information away. Two journeys with identical endpoints and identical durations have identical average velocities however wildly they differed in between. Instantaneous velocity is what a speedometer and a radar gun report, and it is what every dynamics equation later in the course actually uses.

The most commonly examined consequence: average speed is not the arithmetic mean of the speeds unless equal times are spent at each. Travel a route out at 60 km·h⁻¹ and back at 40 km·h⁻¹ and you spend longer at the slow speed, so the average is dragged below 50 — it is the harmonic mean, 2v₁v₂/(v₁+v₂) = 48 km·h⁻¹. Equal distances → harmonic mean. Equal times → arithmetic mean.

Worked example 1 · displacement, distance, and the two averages

A jogger runs 300 m due east in 100 s, then turns and runs 100 m due west in 50 s. Find the average speed and the average velocity for the whole run.

  1. Set the axis. East positive. Leg 1: Δx₁ = +300 m. Leg 2: Δx₂ = −100 m.
  2. Totals. Net displacement Δx = +300 − 100 = +200 m. Path length = 300 + 100 = 400 m. Total time Δt = 100 + 50 = 150 s.
  3. Average speed = 400 m ÷ 150 s = 2.667 m·s⁻¹ ≈ 2.67 m·s⁻¹.
  4. Average velocity = +200 m ÷ 150 s = 1.333 m·s⁻¹ ≈ 1.33 m·s⁻¹ east.
  5. Sanity check. The runner reversed, so the path length exceeds |Δx| and the average speed must exceed the magnitude of the average velocity. 2.67 > 1.33 ✓
average speed 2.67 m·s⁻¹ · average velocity 1.33 m·s⁻¹ due east

Exam point

Any question containing the words "returns to the starting point" is testing the same reflex: average velocity = 0, average speed ≠ 0. Read the question for which one it asks for, then answer that one. Examiners write the plausible arithmetic mean into the distractor list on purpose.

§ 03

Acceleration — and why "deceleration" is not a separate thing

The ideaAcceleration is not how fast something is going; it is how quickly its going is changing. That distinction does real work. Because going somewhere involves a direction as well as an amount, a car taking a bend at a steady speed is accelerating too. And there is no separate thing called slowing down: there is one change, and whether the object gets faster or slower depends only on whether that change pushes the same way it is already travelling or against it. Get comfortable with that and half the sign errors in this chapter disappear.

Definition & formula

Acceleration a
The rate of change of the velocity vector with time. Because velocity is a vector, an acceleration exists whenever the speed changes, or the direction changes, or both.
ā = Δv/Δt    a = dv/dt = d²x/dt² average (left) and instantaneous (right); both valid for any motion
Full definition, symbols and units
Uniform acceleration
Acceleration that is constant in both magnitude and direction over the interval considered — the only case the four equations of §04 describe.
speeding up ⟺ a and v have the same sign1-D test
slowing down ⟺ a and v have opposite signs1-D test
āaverage acceleration over Δt (m·s⁻²)
ainstantaneous acceleration (m·s⁻²)
Δvchange in velocity (m·s⁻¹)
Δttime interval (s)
gfree-fall acceleration, 9.8 m·s⁻² near the Earth's surface

"Deceleration" is a description of a relationship, not a quantity. There is exactly one vector a; whether the motion is speeding up or slowing down depends on how that vector sits relative to v. A car moving in the −x direction at −20 m·s⁻¹ with acceleration a = +4 m·s⁻² is slowing down, even though the acceleration is positive. A ball thrown upward has a = −9.8 m·s⁻² throughout — on the way up (slowing), at the top (momentarily at rest), and on the way down (speeding up). The acceleration never changes; only its relationship to the velocity does.

Trap · v = 0 does not imply a = 0

At the top of a vertical throw the velocity passes through zero, but it is changing as fast as ever — that is precisely why the ball does not hover there. If a were zero at the apex the ball would stay at the apex forever. Velocity is the value; acceleration is the rate of change of the value, and the two are logically independent at any instant.

Memory hook

Same signs → faster. Opposite signs → slower. Nothing else to remember, in any number of dimensions: the component of a along v changes the speed, the component perpendicular to v changes only the direction (§10).

§ 04

The four constant-acceleration equations

The ideaWhen the rate of change stays steady, you never have to follow a journey moment by moment — the whole thing can be written down in one line. Five things describe such a journey: how far it went, how fast it was at the start, how fast at the end, how strongly it was changing, and how long it lasted. Know any three of them and the remaining two are already decided. The four equations below are simply the four useful ways of saying that, each one arranged so a different quantity drops out, and the skill is picking the one that leaves out whatever you neither know nor need.

Definition & formula

The kinematic equations (SUVAT)
Four algebraic relations among the five quantities Δx, v₀, v, a, t that hold if and only if the acceleration is constant over the whole interval. Each equation omits exactly one of the five — choose the one that omits the quantity you neither know nor want.
v = v₀ + at constant a · omits Δx
Full definition, symbols and units
Δx = v₀t + ½at²constant a · omits v

Δx = ½(v₀ + v)tconstant a · omits a
Δxdisplacement over the interval (m)

vvelocity at time t (m·s⁻¹)

Do not memorise these four as four facts. They are one picture — the v–t graph of constant acceleration is a straight line — read four ways.

Go deeper · deriving all four equations from that one graph

Where each one comes from

  1. v = v₀ + at. The slope of the line is Δv/Δt = a by definition, so Δv = at and v = v₀ + at. This equation is the definition of constant acceleration, rearranged.
  2. Δx = ½(v₀ + v)t. The area of a trapezoid is (mean of the two parallel sides) × width. Since the line is straight, the mean height is the average velocity, so v̄ = ½(v₀+v) — true for constant a and for nothing else.
  3. Δx = v₀t + ½at². Cut the same trapezoid into the rectangle v₀ × t and the triangle ½ × t × (at). Interpretation: the distance you would have gone at the original speed, plus the bonus the acceleration bought you.
  4. v² = v₀² + 2aΔx. Eliminate t. From (1), t = (v − v₀)/a; substitute into (2): Δx = ½(v₀+v)(v−v₀)/a = (v² − v₀²)/(2a). Rearranged, that is the equation. It is the one to reach for whenever the question mentions no time at all — and it is the ancestor of the work–energy theorem.

Worked example 2 · stopping distance, and why speed is the dangerous variable

A car travels at 25 m·s⁻¹ (90 km·h⁻¹). The driver's reaction time is 0.70 s and the brakes then produce a constant a = −5.0 m·s⁻². Find the total stopping distance. Then repeat at half the speed.

  1. Reaction phase — no acceleration yet, so Δx = vt = 25 × 0.70 = 17.5 m.
  2. Braking phase — no time is asked for, so use v² = v₀² + 2aΔx with v = 0:
    0 = 25² + 2(−5.0)Δx → Δx = 625 / 10 = 62.5 m.
  3. Total = 17.5 + 62.5 = 80.0 m. (Braking time, if wanted: t = (0 − 25)/(−5.0) = 5.0 s.)
  4. At 12.5 m·s⁻¹: reaction distance halves to 8.75 m, but the braking distance is 156.25/10 = 15.6 m — a quarter, because Δx ∝ v₀². Total 24.4 m.
  5. Read the physics. Halving the speed cut the total stopping distance by a factor of 3.3, not 2. The quadratic term is why speed limits near hospitals and schools are set where they are, and why pedestrian injury severity climbs so steeply with impact speed.
80.0 m at 25 m·s⁻¹ · 24.4 m at 12.5 m·s⁻¹

Trap · these four equations are useless if a is not constant

A skydiver whose air drag grows with speed, a car whose engine power (not force) is constant, a mass on a spring, a charge in a non-uniform field — none of these may be fed into SUVAT. If the acceleration changes during the interval, you need the graph (area under v–t) or calculus. Watch also for problems where a is constant in each phase but different between phases: split the timeline and apply the equations phase by phase, carrying the final velocity of one phase into the next as v₀.

§ 05

Free fall: the special case a = −g

The ideaTake the air out of the picture and gravity turns out to be remarkably even-handed: it changes the motion of everything at the same steady rate, whatever the object is and however heavy it is. A feather really does keep up with a hammer once there is no air to hold it back. And "falling" is the wrong word for what this section covers — a ball you throw upward is under exactly the same influence on the way up, at the top, and on the way down, so it counts from the moment it leaves your hand. Nothing new has to be learned here; it is the previous section with one number filled in.

Definition & formula

Free fall
Motion under gravity alone, with air resistance neglected. "Falling" is a misnomer: an object thrown upward is in free fall from the moment it leaves the hand.
ay = −g = −9.8 m·s⁻² up chosen positive; air resistance neglected
Full definition, symbols and units
g
The magnitude of the free-fall acceleration near the Earth's surface, 9.8 m·s⁻². It is a positive number by definition; the direction is supplied by the sign convention, not by g itself.

Free fall is not a new topic; it is §04 with the acceleration filled in. The one physical fact worth stating is Galileo's: with air resistance neglected, the acceleration is independent of mass. A feather and a hammer released together in vacuum land together. In air they do not, because drag — roughly proportional to cross-sectional area — is a much larger fraction of the weight for the feather. This is why "heavier things fall faster" survives as an intuition: it is a correct observation about air, and a wrong one about gravity.

Two symmetries save time in exams. For a projectile launched and caught at the same height: the time up equals the time down, and the speed at any given height on the way up equals the speed at that height on the way down (opposite in direction). Both follow from the fact that the v–t graph is a straight line crossing zero — the geometry above and below the axis is a mirror image. Neither holds if the landing height differs from the launch height.

Worked example 3 · thrown upward from a cliff

A stone is thrown vertically upward at 15 m·s⁻¹ from the edge of a cliff 25 m above the sea. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻² and neglect air resistance. Find (a) the maximum height above the launch point, (b) the time to reach the water, (c) the impact speed.

  1. Axis. Up positive, origin at the launch point. So v₀ = +15 m·s⁻¹, a = −9.8 m·s⁻², and the sea is at y = −25 m.
  2. (a) Apex. At the top v = 0, and no time is asked, so 0 = v₀² − 2g·h → h = 15²/(2 × 9.8) = 225/19.6 = 11.5 m above the launch point (36.5 m above the sea). It gets there at t = 15/9.8 = 1.53 s.
  3. (b) Time to the water. Use y = v₀t − ½gt² with y = −25:
    −25 = 15t − 4.9t² → 4.9t² − 15t − 25 = 0.
    t = [15 ± √(225 + 4·4.9·25)] / (2 × 4.9) = [15 ± √715] / 9.8 = (15 + 26.74)/9.8 = 4.26 s. (The negative root is discarded: it is the time the stone would have left the water had the parabola been extended backwards.)
  4. (c) Impact speed. Fastest route is the time-free equation: v² = 15² + 2(9.8)(25) = 225 + 490 = 715 → v = 26.7 m·s⁻¹ downward. Cross-check with (b): v = 15 − 9.8(4.26) = −26.7 m·s⁻¹ ✓
  5. Symmetry check. Passing the launch height on the way down, the speed is again 15 m·s⁻¹, at t = 2 × 1.53 = 3.06 s — and the remaining 25 m of fall then takes 4.26 − 3.06 = 1.20 s. ✓
(a) 11.5 m · (b) 4.26 s · (c) 26.7 m·s⁻¹ downward

Exam point

For a throw that lands below the launch point, the quadratic has two positive-looking roots only if you make a sign slip; done correctly there is exactly one positive root. And do not use "time up = time down" here — the fall is longer than the rise. That shortcut is licensed only by equal launch and landing heights.

§ 06

Motion graphs: slope and area are inverses

The ideaWhere something is, how fast it is going, and how quickly that is changing are three pictures of one journey, and each picture can be recovered from the one next to it. Move one way along the chain and you are asking how steeply a line tilts. Move back the other way and you are asking how much space is trapped underneath it. Those are the only two moves there are, so once you can tell which one a question wants, reading motion graphs stops being a separate skill and becomes the same skill you already have.

Definition & formula

Slope–area duality
Moving down the chain x → v → a is differentiation, read off a graph as a slope. Moving up the chain a → v → x is integration, read off a graph as a signed area. Every motion-graph question is one of those two operations.

Four readings that carry most of the marks. (1) On an x–t graph a horizontal segment is at rest, not "at the origin". (2) On an x–t graph, a peak is where the velocity is zero and reverses sign — it is not where the object is going fastest. (3) On a v–t graph, a line sloping down through zero means the object slowed, stopped and reversed; the area is then positive before the crossing and negative after, so displacement and distance separate. (4) "Which graph shows an object moving backwards while speeding up?" means: v below the axis and getting further from it.

Trap · the steepest x–t slope, not the highest x–t point

Students routinely pick the highest point of an x–t graph as the fastest moment. Height is position. Speed is steepness, and the sign of the slope is the direction. An object at x = 200 m sitting still has a bigger position and a smaller velocity than one at x = 5 m sprinting.

§ 07

Vectors, components and vector addition

The ideaAnything that points somewhere can be broken into two pieces at right angles to each other — how much of it goes across, and how much of it goes up. The reason this is worth doing is that the two pieces never interfere: whatever is happening sideways has no say in what is happening vertically, and the other way round. That single fact is what lets a genuinely two-dimensional problem be taken apart into two separate, much easier one-dimensional problems, and it is why the next three sections are easier than they look.

Definition & formula

Vector
A quantity with both magnitude and direction that adds by the parallelogram (tip-to-tail) rule. Written bold here: A. Its magnitude is written A or |A|.
Ax = A cosθ  ·  Ay = A sinθ θ measured from the +x axis
Full definition, symbols and units
Components
The projections of a vector onto the chosen axes. Because the axes are perpendicular, the components are independent — this is the fact that makes 2-D kinematics tractable at all.

Everything in two-dimensional kinematics rests on one theorem: perpendicular components do not talk to each other. Adding a horizontal velocity to a falling stone changes where it lands but not when. Choosing your axes well is therefore a real decision, not bookkeeping — on an incline, tilting the axes so x runs along the slope turns a two-component problem into a one-component one.

Two habits prevent most errors. First, cos goes with the component adjacent to the stated angle — if the angle is measured from the vertical, then it is the vertical component that carries the cosine. Do not memorise "x gets cos". Second, when you take arctan, check the quadrant against the signs of the components: a calculator returns an angle between −90° and +90° and will happily report a north-west vector as though it pointed south-east.

Worked example 4 · adding two displacements

A hiker walks 5.0 km in a direction 30° north of east, then 4.0 km due north. Find the resultant displacement.

  1. Axes. +x east, +y north.
  2. Components of leg 1. x₁ = 5.0 cos30° = 5.0 × 0.8660 = 4.330 km; y₁ = 5.0 sin30° = 5.0 × 0.5000 = 2.500 km.
  3. Components of leg 2. x₂ = 0; y₂ = 4.000 km.
  4. Add component-wise. x = 4.330 km, y = 2.500 + 4.000 = 6.500 km.
  5. Magnitude. R = √(4.330² + 6.500²) = √(18.75 + 42.25) = √61.0 = 7.81 km.
  6. Direction. θ = arctan(6.500 / 4.330) = arctan(1.501) = 56.3°; both components are positive, so it is in the first quadrant — 56.3° north of east.
  7. Sanity check. The answer must lie between |5.0 − 4.0| = 1.0 km and 5.0 + 4.0 = 9.0 km. 7.81 ✓ And it must be steeper than 30° but shallower than 90°. 56.3° ✓
R = 7.81 km at 56.3° north of east

Trap · magnitudes do not add

5.0 km + 4.0 km gives 9.0 km only if the legs are parallel. In general the resultant magnitude lies in the range |A − B| ≤ |A+B| ≤ A + B, with the maximum at φ = 0° and the minimum at φ = 180°. Any multiple-choice option outside that band can be eliminated before you calculate anything.

§ 08

Relative velocity: rivers and closing speed

The ideaHow fast something is moving is never an absolute fact — it is always how fast it is moving compared with something else. Someone walking down the aisle of a moving train is strolling as far as the other passengers are concerned and hurtling along as far as a person on the platform is concerned, and neither of them is wrong. This section is about choosing that point of view deliberately, because picking the right one turns an awkward question — a boat pushed sideways by a current, two cars racing toward each other — into an ordinary one.

Definition & formula

Relative velocity vA/B
The velocity of object A as measured in the reference frame of object B — that is, what B would say A is doing. Every velocity is relative to something; "the velocity of the boat" is meaningless until you say relative to the water or relative to the bank.
vA/C = vA/B + vB/C non-relativistic (v ≪ c); inner subscripts cancel

The subscript rule is the whole method: write the chain so the inner subscripts match and cancel. vboat/bank = vboat/water + vwater/bank. Then resolve into components and treat each axis separately.

The physically important result for a river crossing is that the current does not affect the crossing time when the bow is aimed straight across. The current is entirely along the bank, so it has no component across the river; the across-river velocity is still the full boat speed, and t = w/vboat. All the current does is add a downstream drift. This is the independence-of-components theorem of §07 in a different costume, and it is the same reason a bullet fired horizontally and one dropped hit the ground together.

Closing-speed problems are simpler still. Two cars approaching head-on at 25 and 15 m·s⁻¹ close the gap at 25 + 15 = 40 m·s⁻¹; 800 m of separation therefore lasts 20 s. Two cars travelling the same way at those speeds close at only 10 m·s⁻¹. Sit in one frame and the problem becomes one-dimensional with a single moving object.

Worked example 5 · crossing a river, two ways

A river 120 m wide flows east at 1.5 m·s⁻¹. A boat can move at 2.0 m·s⁻¹ relative to the water. (a) If the bow is aimed due north (straight across), find the crossing time, the drift and the path length over the ground. (b) What heading lands the boat directly opposite, and how long does that take?

  1. (a) Across-axis. The current is purely east, so the northward velocity is the full 2.0 m·s⁻¹: t = 120 / 2.0 = 60 s.
  2. (a) Along-axis. Drift = 1.5 × 60 = 90 m downstream.
  3. (a) Ground velocity. √(2.0² + 1.5²) = √6.25 = 2.5 m·s⁻¹, so the path over the ground is 2.5 × 60 = 150 m (the 3–4–5 triangle: 90, 120, 150).
  4. (b) Cancel the drift. The boat must aim upstream (west of north) by θ where the westward component cancels the current: 2.0 sinθ = 1.5 → sinθ = 0.750 → θ = 48.6° west of north.
  5. (b) Remaining northward speed. 2.0 cos(48.6°) = 2.0 × 0.6614 = 1.323 m·s⁻¹ (= √(2.0² − 1.5²) = √1.75). Crossing time = 120 / 1.323 = 90.7 s.
  6. Read the physics. Steering to land opposite costs 51 % more time, because part of the boat's capability is spent fighting the current instead of crossing. If the current exceeded 2.0 m·s⁻¹ no heading could land the boat opposite at all — but it could still reach the far bank, just further downstream.
(a) 60 s, 90 m drift, 150 m path · (b) 48.6° upstream, 90.7 s

Exam point

Distinguish the two river questions by their target. "Minimum time" → aim straight across and accept the drift. "Land directly opposite" / "minimum drift" → aim upstream at sinθ = vriver/vboat and accept the longer time. They are different optimisations and cannot both be satisfied.

§ 09

Projectile motion: two independent 1-D problems

The ideaThrow something and it is doing two completely separate things at the same time. Sideways it simply keeps going at whatever rate it left your hand, because once it is in the air nothing is pushing it along or holding it back. Up and down, it behaves exactly as it would if you had thrown it straight up and let it drop back — gravity takes no notice of the fact that it is also travelling sideways. Slide a coin off the edge of a table at the same moment you drop another one beside it and the two hit the floor together, which is this whole section in a single demonstration: the only thing the sideways story and the up-and-down story share is the clock.

Definition & formula

An object moving under gravity alone after launch, with air resistance neglected. Its horizontal acceleration is zero and its vertical acceleration is −g at every instant, including the apex.

Why 45° maximises the range

On level ground the range is R = v₀² sin2θ / g. Everything except sin2θ is fixed, and sin peaks at 1 when its argument is 90°, i.e. when θ = 45°. The causal story matters more than the algebra: the range is (horizontal speed) × (time of flight) = v₀cosθ × 2v₀sinθ/g. A shallow launch gives plenty of horizontal speed but almost no hang time; a steep launch gives lots of hang time but little horizontal speed. 45° is the balance point of that trade-off. It also follows immediately that complementary angles give equal ranges — 30° and 60° land in the same place, one with a flat fast trajectory and one with a high slow one, because sin60° = sin120°.

Two conditions on that result. It assumes level ground: launching from a height, the optimum angle is less than 45° (extra hang time is already provided by the drop, so it pays to buy horizontal speed instead). And it assumes no air resistance: real thrown objects optimise nearer 35–40°.

Worked example 6 · a full projectile, level ground

A ball is kicked from level ground at 20 m·s⁻¹, 30° above the horizontal. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻² and neglect air resistance. Find the time of flight, the maximum height, the range, and the velocity 1.0 s after launch.

  1. Split the launch velocity. vx = 20 cos30° = 20 × 0.8660 = 17.32 m·s⁻¹ (constant); vy0 = 20 sin30° = 20 × 0.5000 = 10.00 m·s⁻¹.
  2. Time of flight. Vertical only. The ball returns to y = 0, and by symmetry the time up equals the time down: tup = 10.00/9.8 = 1.020 s, so T = 2.04 s.
  3. Maximum height. Vertical only, no time needed: H = vy0²/(2g) = 100/19.6 = 5.10 m.
  4. Range. Horizontal only, using T as the bridge: R = 17.32 × 2.041 = 35.4 m. Check against the formula: R = 20² sin60° / 9.8 = 400 × 0.8660 / 9.8 = 346.4/9.8 = 35.35 m ✓
  5. Velocity at t = 1.0 s. vx = 17.32 m·s⁻¹ still; vy = 10.00 − 9.8(1.0) = +0.20 m·s⁻¹ (just short of the apex). Speed = √(17.32² + 0.20²) = √(299.98 + 0.04) = 17.32 m·s⁻¹, at 0.7° above the horizontal.
  6. Sanity check. A 60° launch at the same speed would give the same 35.4 m range but H = 400 × 0.75/19.6 = 15.3 m and T = 3.53 s — same landing spot, three times the height.
T = 2.04 s · H = 5.10 m · R = 35.4 m · v(1.0 s) = 17.3 m·s⁻¹ nearly horizontal

Trap · T = 2v₀sinθ/g and R = v₀²sin2θ/g are not general

Both assume the projectile lands at the launch height. Fire from a cliff, into a valley, or onto a raised platform and neither applies. In those cases go back to y = (v₀sinθ)t − ½gt², set y to the (negative) landing height, solve the quadratic for t, and only then multiply by vx. Also note that a projectile launched horizontally has θ = 0, so vy0 = 0 and the fall time is simply t = √(2h/g) — independent of how fast it was thrown.

Memory hook

Vertical decides when; horizontal decides where. Time is the only thing the two axes are allowed to share.

§ 10

Uniform circular motion, kinematically

The ideaGoing round a bend at a perfectly steady speed still counts as changing how you are moving, because the direction you are heading in is every bit as much a part of it as the number on the speedometer. That needle can sit rock-still all the way round a roundabout while you are pressed hard against the door, and being pressed against the door is the giveaway that something is being changed after all. What is changing is direction, and at every single moment the change is aimed straight at the centre of the circle — not forward along the path, and certainly not outward. All this section does is work out how big that inward change is, from nothing more than the shape of a circle and ideas you already have.

Definition & formula

Uniform circular motion
Motion on a circular path at constant speed. The velocity is nonetheless changing at every instant, because its direction changes continuously — so the acceleration is not zero.
ac = v²/r = ω²r uniform circular motion; directed toward the centre
Full definition, symbols and units
Centripetal acceleration ac
The acceleration of a body in uniform circular motion: constant in magnitude, always directed toward the centre, always perpendicular to the velocity. "Centripetal" names a direction, not a new kind of acceleration.

This is the clearest test of whether you believe that velocity is a vector. The speedometer reads constant, so speed is constant; but the velocity vector is swinging round through 360° every period, so Δv ≠ 0 and therefore a ≠ 0. Since the speed does not change, the acceleration can have no component along v (§03) — so it must be entirely perpendicular, which on a circle means pointing at the centre.

The two forms of the formula are not interchangeable in practice. Use ac = v²/r when the linear speed is fixed — a car on a bend, blood in an aortic arch. Use ac = ω²r when the angular speed is fixed — anything rigid and spinning, where every point shares ω but the rim moves faster than the hub. Note the opposite dependence on r: at fixed v, a larger radius means gentler acceleration; at fixed ω, a larger radius means fiercer acceleration. That is exactly why a centrifuge is built with the longest rotor arm the machine can spin.

Worked example 7 · a clinical centrifuge

A benchtop centrifuge separates plasma from packed cells. The sample sits at a radius of 8.0 cm and the rotor turns at 3000 revolutions per minute. Find the tangential speed, the centripetal acceleration, and express that acceleration in multiples of g.

  1. Convert to SI. r = 0.080 m. f = 3000 / 60 = 50 rev·s⁻¹ = 50 Hz.
  2. Angular speed. ω = 2πf = 2π(50) = 100π = 314.2 rad·s⁻¹.
  3. Tangential speed. v = ωr = 314.2 × 0.080 = 25.1 m·s⁻¹.
  4. Centripetal acceleration. ω is the fixed quantity, so use ac = ω²r = (314.2)² × 0.080 = 98 700 × 0.080 = 7.90 × 10³ m·s⁻².
    Cross-check with v²/r = 25.1² / 0.080 = 630 / 0.080 = 7.9 × 10³ m·s⁻² ✓
  5. In units of g. 7.90 × 10³ / 9.8 = ≈ 8.1 × 10² g — about 806 times gravity, which is why a few minutes of spinning does what hours of settling could not.
  6. Read the physics. Sedimentation rate scales with the effective acceleration, so this is the quantity clinical protocols specify (as "relative centrifugal force", RCF, in units of g) rather than rpm — rpm alone is meaningless without the rotor radius.
v = 25.1 m·s⁻¹ · ac = 7.9 × 10³ m·s⁻² ≈ 806 g toward the axis

Exam point

Three things are examined again and again. (1) Constant speed with nonzero acceleration is not a contradiction — direction is half of a vector. (2) The acceleration is toward the centre; there is no outward acceleration. (3) Nothing here needs Newton's laws: ac is a kinematic fact about circular paths. The force that supplies it — friction, tension, gravity, the normal force — is the subject of note 02, and "centripetal force" is a job description, never an extra force to add to a free-body diagram.

Trap · degrees, revolutions and radians

v = ωr and ac = ω²r are true only if ω is in rad·s⁻¹. Revolutions per minute must be multiplied by 2π/60. Forgetting the 2π under-reports the acceleration by a factor of about 39. Likewise, the vestibular semicircular canals respond to angular acceleration (rad·s⁻²), not to ac — which is why you can be spun at constant ω and, after a few seconds, feel nothing at all.
