Amino Acids, Peptides and Protein Structure

A polypeptide has far less freedom than it looks. The repeating backbone unit is flat and rigid, so each residue contributes exactly two rotatable angles and nothing else; and the only chemistry that varies along the chain is the side chain hanging off one carbon. Everything this paper asks about proteins falls out of those two facts. The charge on a peptide at a stated pH is arithmetic on side-chain pKa values. Which residues can sit in a helix, a sheet or a collagen triple helix is a question about which of those two angles they are allowed to adopt. Why haemoglobin is sigmoid and myoglobin is not is a question about whether the two angles can move together. And every purification column is just a device for sorting on charge, on size or on surface. Learn to derive those, and you will not need to memorise much at all.

§ 01

One carbon, four things attached

The ideaEvery one of the twenty building blocks is the same molecule with one part swapped out. A single central carbon carries an acid group on one side, a base group on the other, a hydrogen, and one variable piece. Because an acid and a base are bolted to the same carbon, they react with each other: in water the acid has already given its proton away and the base has already taken one, so the molecule sits there carrying a positive and a negative charge at the same time. That double-charged state is not an oddity — it is the normal condition, and it is the reason amino acids behave like salts rather than like organic acids.

Definition & key facts

α-Amino acid
A carboxylic acid bearing an amino group on the carbon adjacent to the carboxyl — the α-carbon. That α-carbon also carries a hydrogen and a variable side chain R.
⁺H₃N–CαH(R)–COO⁻ the zwitterion — the form that actually exists in water at any pH between about 3 and 8

Why the zwitterion, and what follows from it

The α-carboxyl is a fairly strong organic acid (pKa ≈ 2.3, well below acetic acid's 4.76, because the neighbouring –NH₃⁺ pulls electron density away and stabilises the carboxylate). The α-amino group is a reasonably strong base (its conjugate acid has pKa ≈ 9.6). Put them on the same carbon and, at any pH between roughly 3 and 8, the first is already deprotonated while the second is still protonated. There is nothing left to decide: the molecule must carry both charges.

That single structural fact explains a set of properties the exam likes to test as a group. Amino acids are crystalline solids that decompose rather than melt, typically above 200 °C, because the crystal is held by full ionic forces, not by dipoles. They are freely soluble in water and nearly insoluble in ether. They have no measurable vapour pressure. And their solutions have a large dipole moment and a high dielectric increment. If a question offers you "amino acids are volatile liquids" or "they dissolve well in nonpolar solvents", the zwitterion is the reason both are wrong.

Two stereochemistry traps

L is not the same as (S), and L is not the same as (+). The D/L system is a relative configuration, referred to glyceraldehyde; R/S is the absolute CIP assignment; and (+)/(−) is an experimental measurement of optical rotation. Nineteen L-amino acids happen to be (S); L-cysteine is (R), purely because its side-chain sulfur outranks the carboxyl carbon under CIP rules. Nothing about its three-dimensional shape differs from the others.

D-amino acids do exist in biology. D-Ala and D-Glu are structural elements of bacterial peptidoglycan (which is precisely why they resist host proteases, and why vancomycin binds the D-Ala–D-Ala terminus); D-Ser is a co-agonist at the NMDA receptor in mammalian brain; several peptide antibiotics such as gramicidin contain them. What is true is that ribosomal protein synthesis uses only L.

Reading a peptide name

Ala–Gly–Ser means alanine's free α-amino group is the N-terminus and serine's free α-carboxyl is the C-terminus; the middle residue has both of its α groups locked into peptide bonds and therefore contributes no charge. Every net-charge calculation in § 04 starts by recognising that only the two ends plus the side chains are still titratable.

§ 02

The twenty side chains, by what they do

The ideaLearning twenty structures as twenty separate facts is the slow way and it does not survive exam pressure. There are really only about five questions you ever ask of a side chain: does it carry a charge at body pH, can it make a hydrogen bond, is it greasy, is it bulky, and can it do something chemically special such as form a bridge or be switched on and off. Sort the twenty by the answers and the groups almost draw themselves — and each group then behaves as one thing, so a question about "the residue most likely to be buried in the core" or "the residue most likely to be phosphorylated" has an obvious short list.

The three that break the pattern

Glycine has no side chain at all — just a second hydrogen. It is therefore achiral, the smallest residue by a wide margin, and the most conformationally free: with no Cβ to collide with the backbone it can adopt φ/ψ combinations that are forbidden to every other residue (§ 05). That freedom is why glycine appears wherever a chain must turn sharply or pack impossibly tightly — the tight turns of § 06, and the every-third-residue position in collagen.

Proline is the only one whose side chain loops back and bonds to its own backbone nitrogen. The consequences are entirely structural and they are the most reliably examined facts on this page: the nitrogen is a secondary amine, so in a peptide bond proline has no N–H to donate a hydrogen bond; and the five-membered ring pins φ at about −60° so the residue cannot adopt most backbone conformations. Together these make proline the classic helix and sheet breaker, and the residue of choice in turns and in collagen.

Cysteine is the only side chain that will form a covalent bond to another side chain spontaneously — the lysine-derived cross-links of collagen and elastin (§ 06) are the other case, and they need a dedicated enzyme to make them. Two thiols oxidise — they do not condense — to a disulfide, and the resulting dimer is called cystine. Because the reaction needs an oxidising environment, stable disulfides are a feature of secreted and ER-lumen proteins (insulin, immunoglobulins, ribonuclease) and are rare in the reducing cytosol. Cysteine's thiol is also unusually acidic for a "neutral" side chain (pKa 8.18), and in an enzyme active site that value can fall to 4 or below, giving a permanently reactive thiolate nucleophile.

Short lists worth having ready

Sulfur-containing: Cys and Met — only two. Amide side chains: Asn and Gln (uncharged; do not confuse with Asp and Glu). Alcohol side chains: Ser, Thr, Tyr — and therefore the phosphorylation set. β-branched: Val, Ile, Thr — sterically awkward, so they disfavour α-helix and favour β-sheet.

One-letter codes that catch people out: W = Trp, Y = Tyr, F = Phe, K = Lys, R = Arg, Q = Gln, N = Asn, E = Glu, D = Asp. Remember them as pairs: D/N are the four-carbon acid and its amide, E/Q the five-carbon pair.

The nine essential amino acids

PVT TIM HaLL — Phe, Val, Thr, Trp, Ile, Met, His, Leu, Lys. Add arginine when the question says growth, pregnancy or trauma; add tyrosine and cysteine when the question says phenylketonuria or a diet lacking methionine.

§ 03

pKa, Henderson–Hasselbalch and buffering

The ideaEvery ionisable group has a number that says how tightly it holds its proton. Compare that number with the pH of the solution and you know, without any calculation at all, which way the group is sitting: below the number it keeps the proton, above the number it has lost it. The only time you need arithmetic is when the two numbers are close together, and then a single equation converts the gap between them into a ratio. Everything in the next section — charge, isoelectric point, which way a protein moves in an electric field — is this one comparison repeated for each group in turn.

Definition & formula

pKa
The negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant, and — far more usefully — the pH at which the group is exactly half dissociated. A low pKa means a strong acid, which means it gives its proton away readily.
pH = pKa + log10[A⁻][HA] Henderson–Hasselbalch — conjugate base on top, always
Full definition, symbols and units
Acid dissociation
HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, with Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. Take −log₁₀ of both sides and rearrange to get Henderson–Hasselbalch; the equation contains no new physics.
Buffer
A mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base in comparable amounts. It resists pH change because added H⁺ is mopped up by A⁻ and added OH⁻ by HA — but only while both partners are still present in quantity.
Buffer capacity
Moles of strong acid or base absorbed per unit pH change. It is maximal at pH = pKa, where the ratio is 1:1 and the titration curve is flattest, and it collapses beyond about one pH unit either side.
fraction deprotonated = 11 + 10(pKa − pH)the form to use for a net-charge calculation on an acidic group
fraction protonated = 11 + 10(pH − pKa)use for a basic group — a protonated amine carries the charge
pH = pKa ± 1 ⇒ ratio 10:1 or 1:10 ⇒ ≈9 % / 91 %the working definition of the buffering range

The only sentence you need to internalise is this: the pH tells the group what to do. Write the pH and the pKa next to each other and subtract. If pH is below pKa, the proton stays on — a carboxyl is –COOH (neutral), an amine is –NH₃⁺ (positive). If pH is above pKa, the proton has gone — carboxyl is –COO⁻ (negative), amine is –NH₂ (neutral). Notice that losing a proton always makes the group more negative by one, whether that means 0 → −1 or +1 → 0. That is the whole of § 04 in one sentence.

How far the pH has to be from the pKa before you can treat the group as fully switched is worth fixing quantitatively, because examiners exploit the grey zone. One unit gives 10:1 — that is 91 % switched, so treating it as complete introduces a 9 % error. Two units gives 100:1 (99 %), and three gives 1000:1 (99.9 %). So for a calculation at pH 7, glutamate (4.25) is 2.75 units away and is 99.8 % ionised — safely counted as a full −1. Histidine (6.00) is only one unit away and is 9 % protonated — which is why the honest answer to "what charge does His carry at pH 7?" is "about +0.1, so call it zero, but know that it is the one residue where that matters."

The two rankings that recur every year

Highest side-chain pKa: arginine (12.48). The tempting wrong answer is lysine, because "amines are basic and lysine is the obvious base". The discriminator is the guanidinium resonance argument above — and the practical consequence is that arginine is the one residue you can assume is positively charged under every condition a living cell ever sees, which is why arginine-rich patches are what bind DNA and phosphates.

Full ranking, low to high: Asp 3.65 < Glu 4.25 < His 6.00 < Cys 8.18 < Tyr 10.07 < Lys 10.53 < Arg 12.48. Note the two near-misses examiners like: Cys is a stronger acid than Tyr (thiol beats phenol), and Tyr is a stronger acid than Lys's conjugate acid by only half a unit.

Why histidine does all the work

Look again at the table and notice how empty the middle is. Between glutamate at 4.25 and cysteine at 8.18 there is exactly one side chain, and it sits at 6.00. A group buffers — and, more importantly, can act as both a proton donor and a proton acceptor within a single catalytic cycle — only when its pKa is within about a unit of the working pH. At pH 7.4, aspartate and glutamate are locked as anions and lysine and arginine are locked as cations; none of them can pick a proton up and put it down again. Histidine can.

That is why imidazole turns up wherever a proton has to be moved: the His57 of the chymotrypsin catalytic triad, the proximal and distal histidines of the globins, the zinc ligands of carbonic anhydrase, the His146 that produces the Bohr effect in § 09. It is also why histidine residues are the dominant protein buffer in blood — haemoglobin, with 38 histidines per tetramer, does roughly as much buffering in whole blood as bicarbonate does in plasma.

pKa values are not constants

The tabulated numbers are for the free amino acid in dilute aqueous solution. Inside a folded protein the local environment shifts them, sometimes enormously, and this is a genuine exam theme rather than a technicality. Burying a carboxylate in an apolar pocket removes the water that stabilised the anion and raises its pKa; placing a lysine next to another positive charge destabilises its cation and lowers its pKa. The classic examples are the active-site lysine of acetoacetate decarboxylase (pKa ≈ 5.9 instead of 10.5) and the catalytic cysteine of papain (≈4 instead of 8.2, because an adjacent histidine stabilises the thiolate). The lesson: a pKa is a property of a group in its environment, and enzymes tune it deliberately.

Worked example 1 — three Henderson–Hasselbalch problems, done the fast way

(a) What fraction of histidine side chains (pKa 6.00) is protonated at blood pH, 7.40? (b) What fraction of glutamate side chains (4.25) is ionised at pH 7.00? (c) You need 1.00 L of 0.100 M phosphate buffer at pH 7.40, using the H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻ pair, pKa = 6.86. How much of each? And what happens to the pH when 5.0 mmol of HCl is added?

  1. (a) The gap is pH − pKa = 7.40 − 6.00 = +1.40. A positive gap means the base form dominates, by 101.40 = 25.1 : 1.
  2. So the protonated (charged) form is 1/(1 + 25.1) = 0.0383 — 3.8 % protonated. Histidine is therefore essentially neutral in blood, but nearly 4 % of it is available as a proton donor at any instant, which is exactly what a catalyst needs.
  3. (b) Gap = 7.00 − 4.25 = +2.75, ratio 102.75 = 562 : 1, so ionised fraction = 562/563 = 0.9982 — 99.8 %. Count glutamate as a clean −1.
  4. (c) Gap = 7.40 − 6.86 = +0.54, so [HPO₄²⁻]/[H₂PO₄⁻] = 100.54 = 3.467.
  5. Split the 0.100 M in that proportion: [HPO₄²⁻] = 0.100 × 3.467/4.467 = 0.0776 M and [H₂PO₄⁻] = 0.0224 M. Check: 6.86 + log(0.0776/0.0224) = 7.40 ✓
  6. Adding 5.0 mmol H⁺ converts 5.0 mmol of base to acid: 77.6 → 72.6 mmol and 22.4 → 27.4 mmol.
  7. New pH = 6.86 + log(72.6/27.4) = 6.86 + 0.424 = 7.28 — a fall of only 0.12 units. The same 5 mmol of HCl added to a litre of pure water at pH 7 would give [H⁺] = 5 × 10⁻³ M, i.e. pH 2.30. That five-order-of-magnitude difference is what a buffer buys, and it is why blood pH is defended to ±0.05.

§ 04

Titration curves, pI and net charge

The ideaIf you add base to an amino acid solution drop by drop and plot the pH, the graph does not climb steadily. It rises, stalls, rises, stalls again — one stall for each acidic group, each stall centred on that group's own number. Somewhere between two of the stalls the molecule's positives and negatives exactly cancel, and at that pH it will not move in an electric field at all. Finding that pH is not a new skill: it is just noticing which two stalls the neutral form sits between, and taking their average.

Definition & formula

Isoelectric point, pI
The pH at which a molecule carries zero net charge. It is a property of the molecule, not of the solution, and at that pH the molecule does not migrate in an electric field — and, having no charge to repel its neighbours, is at its least soluble.
pI = ½ (pKa below + pKa above) the two values that flank the species of zero net charge — never simply "the first two"

Computing pI without guessing which values to average

Students lose marks here by memorising three separate formulas and then applying the wrong one. There is only one rule, and it always works: write out the ionisation states in order of increasing pH, find the one with net charge zero, and average the pKa values immediately on either side of it.

Work it for aspartate. Fully protonated at pH 0 it is ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₂COOH)–COOH, net +1. Deprotonate the α-carboxyl (pK 1.88) and you reach net 0. Deprotonate the side-chain carboxyl (pK 3.65) and you reach net −1. So the zero-charge species is flanked by 1.88 and 3.65: pI = ½(1.88 + 3.65) = 2.77. The α-amino group at 9.60 never enters the calculation, because by the time you reach it the molecule is already at −1 and moving further negative. Do the same for lysine and you will find the zero species flanked by 8.95 (α-NH₃⁺) and 10.53 (ε-NH₃⁺), giving 9.74 — and this time the α-carboxyl at 2.18 is the value that never enters.

The pattern that emerges is the one worth carrying: an extra acidic group drags pI down, an extra basic group drags it up. A protein rich in Asp and Glu (pepsin, pI ≈ 1) is acidic; one rich in Lys and Arg (histones, cytochrome c, lysozyme, pI ≈ 10–11) is basic. That single sentence answers most ion-exchange questions in § 10 before you have looked at a column.

Net charge of a peptide — the method that does not fail

  1. List the ionisable groups: N-terminal α-NH₃⁺ (pK ≈ 8–9 in a peptide, lower than the 9.6 of the free amino acid; this page uses 9.0 throughout, and a question will always hand you the value), C-terminal α-COOH (pK ≈ 3.1, higher than the free acid's 2.3), and every ionisable side chain. Ignore every internal α group — they are in peptide bonds.
  2. For each group compare pH with pKa. Below → protonated. Above → deprotonated.
  3. Assign the charge: a protonated amine is +1, a deprotonated amine 0; a protonated carboxyl is 0, a deprotonated carboxyl −1.
  4. Add them up. If any group is within one unit of the pH — in practice this means histidine — use the fraction rather than a whole number, or state the answer as "approximately".

Because the two termini contribute +1 and −1 at any pH between about 4 and 8, they usually cancel. That is a useful shortcut but a dangerous habit: by pH 9 the N-terminus is at best half protonated, above that it is largely neutral, and the cancellation fails. Check, do not assume.

Three things pI does not tell you

It is not the pH of the solution. A protein of pI 4.5 sitting in pH 7.4 buffer is not at its pI; it is 2.9 units above it and therefore substantially negative.

It is not a measure of how much charge a protein carries. Two proteins can share a pI of 7.0 while one has four ionisable groups and the other four hundred. pI locates the zero crossing; it says nothing about the slope through it.

A protein is least soluble at its pI, not most. With no net charge there is no electrostatic repulsion between molecules, so they aggregate and precipitate — the principle behind isoelectric precipitation, behind casein curdling as milk sours, and behind the practical rule that you do not store a protein at its own pI.

§ 05

The peptide bond and the two angles left over

The ideaJoining two amino acids does not just make a longer molecule; it makes a stiffer one. The link that forms is not a plain single bond — it is partly a double bond, and double bonds cannot be twisted. So each joint in the chain arrives already locked flat, and the only places the chain can bend are the two bonds either side of each central carbon. A protein backbone is therefore not a floppy string but a chain of rigid plates connected by two hinges apiece. Once you know that, the whole of protein architecture becomes a question of which hinge settings are physically possible.

Definition & geometry

Peptide bond
The amide link formed by condensation of one amino acid's α-carboxyl with the next one's α-amino group, releasing water. Formation is thermodynamically unfavourable in water (ΔG°′ ≈ +21 kJ·mol⁻¹), which is why the cell activates the carboxyl first — as an aminoacyl-tRNA ester.

Why the amide is flat, and why it is trans

The nitrogen lone pair is not content to sit on the nitrogen. It delocalises into the adjacent carbonyl π* orbital, producing a resonance form with C=N⁺ and O⁻. The real bond is a weighted average of the two, so C–N comes out at 1.32 Å — measurably shorter than the 1.49 Å of the Cα–N single bond in the same molecule — and C=O comes out slightly longer than a ketone's. Partial double-bond character means a rotational barrier, and 80 kJ·mol⁻¹ is far beyond what thermal collisions supply. The plate stays flat.

Flat leaves two arrangements: trans, with the two Cα atoms on opposite sides of the C–N bond, and cis, with them on the same side. In cis the two side chains are forced within van der Waals contact of each other, which costs roughly 18 kJ·mol⁻¹ — and at 310 K a gap that size is exactly what makes trans outnumber cis by about a thousand to one. Proline is the exception, and for a reason worth understanding rather than memorising: because proline's side chain loops back onto its own nitrogen, the "substituent" clashing with the preceding residue in the cis form is the ring's Cδ — and a Cδ is about as bulky as the Cα it would clash with in the trans form. The two arrangements are therefore nearly equal in energy, and roughly 10 % of X–Pro bonds in real proteins are cis.

Peptide-bond facts that get asked directly

The C–N bond has partial double-bond character (≈40 %) and therefore no free rotation; the peptide group is planar and comprises six atoms; it is uncharged but strongly polar, with a dipole of about 3.5 D, and it is both a hydrogen-bond donor (N–H) and acceptor (C=O) — which is precisely what makes backbone-only secondary structures possible. The configuration is trans except at X–Pro. Free rotation exists only about N–Cα (φ) and Cα–C (ψ).

The Ramachandran plot is not a lookup table; it is a steric argument you can reconstruct. Ask, for a given (φ, ψ), whether any two non-bonded atoms would come closer than the sum of their van der Waals radii. Do that for every combination and three islands survive. Their positions then predict secondary structure rather than describe it: the lower-left island is the α-helix, the upper-left is the β-sheet, and the small upper-right island is a mirror-image left-handed helix that is essentially glycine-only, since any side chain at positive φ runs into the preceding residue's carbonyl.

Two practical readings follow. First, a real structure whose residues fall in the white area is probably wrong — the Ramachandran plot is the standard validation tool for crystal structures, and outliers other than glycine are treated as errors. Second, glycine and proline are the two residues that break every rule below, and now you can see why: one has too much freedom, the other too little. Everything in § 06 about helix breakers and turn residues is a corollary of this single plot.

Go deeper · cis-proline, prolyl isomerases and why cyclosporin is an immunosuppressant

Because trans → cis isomerisation of an X–Pro bond requires breaking the 80 kJ·mol⁻¹ resonance barrier, it is slow — seconds to minutes at 37 °C, while the rest of a small protein folds in milliseconds. For any protein that needs a cis-proline in its native state, this one step becomes the rate-limiting event of folding, producing the characteristic slow phase seen in refolding kinetics.

Cells solve it with peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerases (PPIases), which catalyse the rotation by stabilising the twisted transition state. Two families matter clinically:

  • Cyclophilins — the receptor for cyclosporin A.
  • FKBPs (FK506-binding proteins) — the receptor for tacrolimus (FK506) and for sirolimus (rapamycin).

The clinically important twist is that these drugs do not work by inhibiting isomerase activity. The drug–protein complex acquires a new surface: cyclosporin–cyclophilin and tacrolimus–FKBP12 both bind and inhibit calcineurin, the phosphatase that activates NFAT and hence IL-2 transcription in T cells. Rapamycin–FKBP12 instead inhibits mTOR. The isomerase is a carrier, not the target — a genuinely elegant example of gain-of-function inhibition, and a favourite of examiners because the naive answer ("it blocks protein folding") is wrong.

Proline's other structural role follows from the same rigidity: proline-rich sequences cannot form regular secondary structure at all, so they adopt the extended polyproline II conformation, which is exactly what SH3 domains and WW domains recognise in signalling, and what collagen exploits in § 06.

§ 06

Secondary structure: helix, sheet, turn, collagen

The ideaThe backbone comes with a hydrogen-bond donor and an acceptor on every unit, and leaving them unsatisfied in water is wasteful. So the chain finds regular repeating shapes in which every donor is pointed at an acceptor. There are only a few such shapes, because the hinges can only take a few settings. One coils the chain into a spring and satisfies the bonds within the coil; another stretches it flat and satisfies them between neighbouring stretches. A third makes a tight hairpin so the chain can double back. Which one a stretch of sequence chooses is a question about which residues are in it.

Why proline and glycine both break helices, for opposite reasons

This is the single most reliably examined distinction in the section, and the two halves must be kept apart. Proline fails on two counts simultaneously: in a peptide bond its nitrogen carries no hydrogen, so a proline residue simply cannot donate the i→i+4 hydrogen bond the helix requires; and its ring fixes φ near −60°, which is close to the helical value but not free to adjust, so the residue cannot follow the helix's slight variations. Add the steric clash of the ring's Cδ with the carbonyl of residue i−4 and a proline in mid-helix costs several kJ·mol⁻¹.

Glycine fails for the opposite reason: it has nothing wrong with it in a helix, but it is equally happy almost everywhere else. Constraining a residue to one (φ, ψ) costs conformational entropy, and the more conformations you take away, the higher the cost. Glycine has the most to lose, so ordering it into a helix is entropically expensive and it is found preferentially in turns and loops where its freedom is an asset rather than a liability.

Four helix traps

i→i+4, not i→i+3. The i→i+3 pattern describes the 3₁₀ helix, which is tighter, less stable and mostly confined to single turns and helix termini. If an option says "hydrogen bond between residue n and residue n+3" it is describing a different structure.

Proline is tolerated in the first turn. The first four N–H groups of an α-helix have no i−4 partner anyway, so a proline there costs nothing — which is why proline is statistically common at helix N-termini and in transmembrane helices where it introduces a functional kink. "Proline is never found in a helix" is false as written.

The bonds are backbone-to-backbone. Side chains do not make the helix; they only vote on whether it forms. A question that attributes helix stability to side-chain hydrogen bonds is wrong.

The helix has a dipole and a direction. An α-helix is not symmetric end-to-end: N-terminus δ+, C-terminus δ−.

β-sheets, turns and the arithmetic of a hairpin

A β-strand is a nearly fully extended chain (φ, ψ near −120°, +130°) whose backbone donors and acceptors point sideways rather than along the chain. It therefore cannot satisfy itself and must pair with another strand — which is why β-strands never occur alone and why a lone hydrophobic β-strand is an aggregation risk (§ 08). Two arrangements are possible. Antiparallel strands run in opposite directions, so the N–H and C=O of facing residues line up straight at each other; the hydrogen bonds are close to linear and therefore stronger. Parallel strands run the same way, so each hydrogen bond is angled and slightly weaker, and the connection between two parallel strands must cross the whole sheet, giving the characteristic right-handed β-α-β crossover.

Because the side chains alternate above and below the sheet, an alternating polar/nonpolar sequence (…Ser-Val-Thr-Ile-Ser-Val…) produces a strand with one polar and one greasy face — the design principle behind β-barrels and porins. Between strands the chain must turn round in as few residues as possible, and the standard device is the β-turn: four residues, with a hydrogen bond from the C=O of residue 1 to the N–H of residue 4. Proline is common at position 2 and glycine at position 3 — the type II turn requires glycine at position 3 because no other residue can take the necessary positive φ. Note how both "helix breakers" are, in the turn, exactly the right residues.

Collagen: a structure built out of the exceptions

Collagen takes the two residues that ruin ordinary secondary structure and makes them obligatory. The repeating unit is (Gly–X–Y)n, with X most often proline and Y most often 4-hydroxyproline. Each chain adopts the extended, left-handed polyproline II conformation — which proline enforces and which cannot make internal hydrogen bonds at all — and three such chains wind round one another into a right-handed superhelix. In that arrangement one residue from each chain is jammed against the central axis every third position, and the available space there is the size of a hydrogen atom. Only glycine fits. That is the whole reason for the repeat, and it is why substituting even a single glycine is catastrophic.

The hydroxyprolines are made after translation by prolyl 4-hydroxylase, which requires Fe²⁺, molecular O₂, α-ketoglutarate and — critically — ascorbate (vitamin C) to keep the iron reduced. Without them the triple helix is unstable at body temperature and collagen is degraded before it can be secreted, which is scurvy: bleeding gums, perifollicular haemorrhage, poor wound healing, re-opening of old scars. Downstream, lysyl oxidase — a copper enzyme — converts certain lysines to allysine so that covalent cross-links can form between tropocollagen molecules; this step fails in Menkes disease (copper transport) and is inhibited by β-aminopropionitrile in lathyrism.

Collagen diseases map onto specific steps

Osteogenesis imperfecta — a glycine substitution in COL1A1/COL1A2. Because one defective chain poisons the whole trimer, the inheritance is dominant-negative: blue sclerae, recurrent fractures, dentinogenesis imperfecta, hearing loss. Scurvy — failure of hydroxylation (vitamin C). Ehlers–Danlos — many types, but note the two the exam likes: the vascular type is COL3A1 (arterial and bowel rupture) and the kyphoscoliotic type is lysyl hydroxylase (PLOD1) deficiency. Menkes — copper, therefore lysyl oxidase, therefore cross-linking. Match the clinical picture to the biochemical step and these stop being a list to memorise.

§ 07

Tertiary and quaternary structure

The ideaHelices and strands are local; they say nothing about what is next to what once the chain folds back on itself. The next level is the complete three-dimensional shape of one chain, and it is decided almost entirely by one thing: greasy side chains hate water and are driven into the middle, while charged ones stay on the wet outside. Once a chain has folded, several folded chains may then stick together, and that last step buys something a single chain cannot have — the ability for one part of the assembly to tell another part what has just happened to it.

The ranking above is worth arguing over rather than memorising, because the exam's favourite question is "which force is most important in stabilising tertiary structure?" and the answer is the hydrophobic effect — but for a reason that is easy to state wrongly. Hydrogen bonds and salt bridges cost almost nothing net, because in the unfolded state those same donors, acceptors and charges were already hydrogen bonded or ion-paired with water; folding merely swaps one partner for another. The hydrophobic effect has no such cancellation: the ordered water shell around an exposed apolar surface is released into bulk water, and that entropy gain is pure profit. (Note 01 develops this in full.)

Motifs, domains and why the distinction is examined

A motif is geometry: β-α-β, a Greek key, a helix–turn–helix. It may be too small to fold on its own, and the same motif appears in proteins with unrelated jobs. A domain is a folding unit: cut it out of the chain and it will still fold, and it usually does one job — bind ATP, bind DNA, catalyse a reaction. The practical test is independence, and the practical consequence is modularity: a multidomain protein is a set of functions bolted together, and evolution rearranges them by exon shuffling rather than by inventing new folds. Fewer than about a thousand distinct folds account for almost all known structures.

What each structural level survives

Primary structure is covalent along the backbone and survives everything short of a protease or acid hydrolysis. Heat, urea, guanidinium chloride, SDS and extremes of pH destroy secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure and leave the sequence intact. That is the mechanism behind Anfinsen's experiment (§ 08) and behind SDS-PAGE (§ 10), and the statement "denaturation breaks peptide bonds" is a guaranteed wrong option. Be careful with the disulfide, which is the one place the levels blur: Lehninger defines primary structure as all the covalent bonds linking residues, so a disulfide belongs to primary structure, even though what it does is stabilise a tertiary or quaternary arrangement — and a reducing agent breaks it without touching a single peptide bond. If the question asks which level a disulfide is part of, answer primary; if it asks what a disulfide stabilises, answer tertiary.

Coiled coil

Two or more amphipathic α-helices wound round each other. Driven by a heptad repeat (abcdefg)n with hydrophobic residues at a and d — a 3,4-spacing, which is exactly the helical-wheel periodicity of § 06. Found in α-keratin, tropomyosin, myosin tails, and the leucine zipper of bZIP transcription factors. Because 3.5 residues per turn is slightly less than 3.6, the hydrophobic stripe slowly winds round the helix — which is precisely why the two helices must supercoil to stay in contact.

β-α-β and the Rossmann fold

Two parallel strands joined by a helix packed against them. Repeated, it gives the Rossmann fold — the nucleotide-binding module of essentially every NAD(P)-dependent dehydrogenase, with a glycine-rich loop at the C-terminal edge of the strands where the helix dipole's δ+ end meets the phosphates.

Greek key and β-barrel

Four antiparallel strands folded over one another. The immunoglobulin fold is the classic example, and a closed barrel of β-strands with an alternating polar/apolar sequence gives the porins — the only common membrane proteins that are not helical.

DNA- and Ca²⁺-binding motifs

Helix–turn–helix (the recognition helix lies in the major groove), zinc finger (Zn²⁺ held by Cys and His — structural, not catalytic), leucine zipper and basic helix–loop–helix. The EF hand is a helix–loop–helix that chelates Ca²⁺ through carboxylate oxygens — calmodulin, troponin C, parvalbumin.

Fibrous versus globular is the other axis worth holding. Fibrous proteins (α-keratin, collagen, elastin, silk fibroin) are dominated by a single repeating secondary structure, are insoluble, and are structural. Globular proteins fold back on themselves, are soluble, and are functional — enzymes, transporters, antibodies. Membrane proteins are a third class whose "inside" and "outside" are swapped relative to a globular protein, since their solvent is lipid.

Quaternary structure ≠ "more than one domain"

A single polypeptide with four domains has no quaternary structure at all; a dimer of two identical 100-residue chains has quaternary structure and no multidomain architecture. The test is whether there is more than one chain. This matters because allostery and cooperativity are quaternary phenomena: myoglobin has one chain and cannot be cooperative no matter how many domains you give it, while haemoglobin's four chains are exactly what makes § 09 possible.

§ 08

Folding, chaperones and misfolding disease

The ideaA folded protein is only just more stable than an unfolded one. Two enormous opposing effects — the gain from burying greasy surfaces and making contacts, and the loss of all the shapes the chain could otherwise have taken — very nearly cancel, and what is left over is about as much as a handful of hydrogen bonds. That is why proteins are so easy to destroy with heat or a change of solvent, why a single wrong residue can be enough to unpick one, and why cells keep a permanent staff of helpers whose only job is to stop half-folded chains from sticking to each other.

Definition & thermodynamics

Anfinsen's principle
The native conformation is determined entirely by the amino-acid sequence, and it is the thermodynamically most stable structure accessible under physiological conditions. Folding needs no external information — only, sometimes, help going fast enough.

Why the stability is so small, and what follows from it

Folding buries hundreds of square ångströms of apolar surface and makes hundreds of van der Waals contacts and hydrogen bonds — a large favourable term. It also freezes every one of the chain's φ and ψ angles, an enormous unfavourable conformational entropy term. Both are of order hundreds of kJ·mol⁻¹, and the residue is 20–65 kJ·mol⁻¹: the equivalent of two to ten hydrogen bonds for an entire protein.

Three consequences follow directly, and each is examinable. First, proteins denature easily — a modest change in temperature, solvent or pH can flip the sign of a number that small. Second, a single point mutation can be enough to unfold a protein, which is why so many genetic diseases are misfolding diseases rather than active-site diseases. Third, marginal stability is not an accident of chemistry but a design requirement: a protein must be degradable and, for enzymes and receptors, must be able to move. A perfectly rigid protein would be useless.

Anfinsen's experiment, and the detail the question turns on

Ribonuclease A (124 residues, four disulfides) was denatured with 8 M urea plus β-mercaptoethanol — the urea unfolds it, the thiol reduces the disulfides. Removing both together let it refold with full catalytic activity: the sequence alone had specified the structure.

The control is where marks are won. If β-mercaptoethanol is removed first, while the chain is still unfolded in urea, the eight cysteines re-oxidise at random — and with 7 × 5 × 3 × 1 = 105 possible pairings, only about 1 % of the molecules get all four bridges right. Adding protein disulfide isomerase (or simply a trace of thiol) then lets the wrong bonds be reshuffled and activity climbs back to 100 %. The moral is that disulfides lock in a conformation; they do not choose it.

Misfolding: one wrong shape, reached by many routes

The pathological alternative to the native fold is almost always the same structure: amyloid, a fibril of β-sheets in which the strands run perpendicular to the fibril axis and the sheets stack along it. Amyloid is extracellular or intracellular depending on the protein, is resistant to proteolysis, binds Congo red and shows apple-green birefringence under polarised light — the histological answer the exam wants.

Prions take this one step further and make it transmissible. What chaperones do and do not do

Chaperones do not contain folding information and do not make an incorrect sequence fold correctly. They bind the exposed hydrophobic surfaces that a partly folded chain presents, preventing those surfaces from finding each other and aggregating, and they release the chain in ATP-driven cycles to give it repeated chances to reach the native state. This is why the heat-shock response exists at all: raising the temperature increases the population of partly unfolded protein, so the cell makes more Hsp70, Hsp60 and Hsp90. Two useful specifics: GroEL/GroES is a barrel that encloses a single substrate — folding "in a cage" where there is no partner to aggregate with; and Hsp90 is specialised for holding signalling proteins (steroid-hormone receptors, kinases) in a ligand-ready state, which is why Hsp90 inhibitors are anticancer agents.

§ 09

Haemoglobin: cooperativity and its two effectors

The ideaA carrier that grabs oxygen tightly is good at picking it up and useless at putting it down. The trick haemoglobin uses is to change its own mind: it starts out reluctant, and each oxygen it does manage to bind makes the next one easier, so the molecule switches from reluctant to eager over a narrow range of oxygen pressure. Arrange for that switch to happen between the pressure in the lung and the pressure in the tissue and you get a carrier that fills completely in one place and empties substantially in the other. Everything else in this section — acid, carbon dioxide, the red-cell phosphate, fetal haemoglobin — is a way of moving where the switch happens.

Definition & formula

Binding of a ligand at one site changes the affinity of the other sites on the same molecule. It requires multiple binding sites on one assembly — which is why myoglobin, with one chain and one heme, cannot be cooperative under any circumstances.

The mechanism, in the order the molecule performs it

In deoxyhaemoglobin the Fe²⁺ is slightly out of the porphyrin plane, pulled toward the proximal histidine (His F8) that is its fifth ligand. When O₂ binds at the sixth position, the iron's electronic state changes and it moves about 0.6 Å into the plane. It drags His F8 with it; His F8 drags helix F; helix F shifts the α₁β₂ interface; and the ion pairs that held the T state break, letting the αβ dimer pairs rotate by about 15° into the R state. Every other subunit is now in the high-affinity conformation. That is cooperativity: a mechanical linkage, not magic — and its indispensable requirement is a subunit interface, which is why § 07 insisted on the distinction between domains and chains.

Two models describe it. The concerted (MWC) model says the whole tetramer flips between T and R together, so intermediate hybrids are rare; it explains positive cooperativity elegantly but cannot produce negative cooperativity. The sequential (KNF) model lets subunits change one at a time, which accommodates negative cooperativity but is less economical. Haemoglobin behaves mostly like MWC. Note the related definition the exam reuses: a homotropic effector is the substrate acting on its own binding (O₂ on haemoglobin), a heterotropic effector is a different molecule (H⁺, CO₂, 2,3-BPG).

Two distinctions that decide the mark

Saturation is not delivery. A left shift raises saturation at every pO₂ and yet reduces the amount handed over, because delivery is the difference between arterial and venous saturation. Almost every clinical scenario in this topic — stored blood, HbF, carbon monoxide, alkalosis — is testing whether you read the gap rather than the height.

The Hill coefficient is not the number of subunits. nH ≈ 2.8 for a four-site protein means strong but incomplete coupling; if the four sites were all-or-none, nH would be 4, and if they were independent it would be 1. Quoting "nH = 4 because haemoglobin has four chains" is a standard wrong option.

Two haemoglobins that are not simply "shifted"

Methaemoglobin has iron in the Fe³⁺ state, which cannot bind oxygen at all. Blood is chocolate-brown; the patient is cyanosed and does not improve with supplemental oxygen; the causes are congenital cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency or oxidant drugs (dapsone, nitrites, local anaesthetics). Treatment is methylene blue — except in G6PD deficiency, where it fails and can cause haemolysis, because it needs NADPH.

Sickle haemoglobin is a solubility problem, not an affinity problem. The β6 Glu→Val substitution (GAG→GTG) puts a hydrophobic valine on the surface, which fits a complementary hydrophobic pocket on an adjacent tetramer — but only in the T (deoxy) conformation, because only then is the pocket exposed. Hence: polymerisation is triggered by deoxygenation, so hypoxia, acidosis, dehydration and fever precipitate crises, and anything that keeps HbF high (hydroxyurea) is protective.

§ 10

Separating and measuring proteins

The ideaA cell contains thousands of different proteins mixed together, and every method for pulling one of them out works the same way: find a property on which your protein differs from the rest, and build a column or a gel that sorts on that property alone. There are only about four such properties worth using — how big it is, what charge it carries, how greasy its surface is, and what it specifically binds — so there are only about four families of method. Because each step sorts on something different, their effects multiply, and three or four steps chosen sensibly will do what a hundred repetitions of one step never could.

Definition & the figure of merit

Specific activity
Enzyme activity per milligram of total protein (U·mg⁻¹). It is the measure of purity: total activity can only fall as you purify, and total protein certainly falls, so the ratio rises. The two reversals people get wrong under time pressure

Gel filtration: big first. The intuition that says "small things go through faster" is describing a sieve; a gel-filtration column is the opposite of a sieve. The beads are the slow lane and only small molecules can enter them, so large proteins are excluded, travel the short route between beads, and come off in the void volume.

A cation exchanger binds cations. The resin is named for what it exchanges, i.e. what it binds and releases — so a negatively charged resin is a cation exchanger. Work from the protein instead: decide its sign from pI versus pH, then pick the resin of opposite sign.

Go deeper · sequencing, cleavage reagents and mass spectrometry

Edman degradation removes one residue at a time from the N-terminus: phenyl isothiocyanate reacts with the free α-amino group, mild acid cleaves off the derivatised residue as a phenylthiohydantoin, and the shortened peptide is ready for the next round. Two limitations are examinable: it fails on a blocked N-terminus (acetylated or pyroglutamate, which is common), and yields decay so that in practice it reads no more than about 50 residues. Longer chains must therefore be cut into pieces first — and with two different reagents, so that the overlapping fragments can be ordered.

Mass spectrometry has largely replaced Edman for identification. Proteins are ionised without fragmentation by MALDI (matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation, usually with a time-of-flight analyser) or ESI (electrospray, which produces a ladder of multiply charged ions and so brings large proteins within the range of a modest analyser). Digest with trypsin and the set of fragment masses is a peptide mass fingerprint that identifies the protein against a database; fragment a selected peptide inside the instrument (MS/MS) and the mass differences between successive fragment ions read out the sequence directly, one residue at a time. Mass spectrometry is also how post-translational modifications are located, since each adds a characteristic mass (phosphate +80, acetyl +42).

Quantitation. A₂₈₀ needs no reagent and is non-destructive, but depends on Trp and Tyr content — a protein with no tryptophan reads far too low, and nucleic acid contamination reads far too high (which is what the A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ ratio checks). The Bradford assay (Coomassie brilliant blue, binding mostly to Arg) and the BCA assay (Cu²⁺ reduction by peptide bonds) both need a standard curve, usually against bovine serum albumin, and both carry their own biases. A worked example: a 25 kDa protein with 2 Trp and 4 Tyr has ε₂₈₀ = 2(5500) + 4(1490) = 16 960 M⁻¹cm⁻¹, so A₂₈₀ = 0.85 in a 1 cm cell corresponds to 0.85/16 960 = 50.1 µM, i.e. 1.25 mg·mL⁻¹.

Design a purification in the order the exam expects

Start with a high-capacity, low-resolution step that tolerates a large dirty volume (ammonium sulfate), move to medium resolution with high capacity (ion exchange), and finish with high resolution and low capacity (affinity, then gel filtration as a polishing step that also removes aggregates and exchanges the buffer). Putting gel filtration first is the classic wrong answer: its capacity is limited to a few per cent of the column volume, so it is wasted on a crude extract. And remember that ion exchange and gel filtration sort on different properties, which is why using both multiplies the purification while running two ion-exchange columns barely improves on one.
