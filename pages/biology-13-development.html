Animal Reproduction and Development

A fertilised egg is a single cell with one genome, and an adult is a few tens of trillions of cells with the same genome arranged into organs. Development is therefore not a problem of information content — it is a problem of position. Every mechanism on this page exists to tell a cell where it is: an asymmetry already painted into the egg's cytoplasm, a signal arriving from the neighbour next door, a gradient of a diffusing protein, a transcription factor that records which segment a cell sits in. Reproduction is the front half of the same story — how the two haploid packages are made, matured, delivered and fused, and why the whole expensive apparatus of sex is worth keeping. Learn the positional logic and this topic stops being a list of stages with Greek names.

§ 01

Asexual and sexual reproduction, and the cost of sex

The ideaCopying yourself is cheap and reliable; mixing with a partner is expensive and unreliable. An animal that just copies itself passes on all of its own material to every offspring, needs no partner and wastes nothing on males. An animal that mixes gives each offspring only half of itself, must find a partner, and throws away half its reproductive capacity on individuals that cannot bear young. The interesting question is not "what are the advantages of sex" but why anything so wasteful survived at all — and the answer turns out to be about parasites and about mutations that accumulate when nothing is ever shuffled.

Definition & key facts

Sexual reproduction
Production of offspring by the fusion of two haploid gametes to form a diploid zygote. The genetic novelty comes from three sources, and only the third involves the partner: crossing over in prophase I, independent assortment of homologues in metaphase I, and random fusion of gametes.
asexual : sexual female-line growth = 2 : 1 the "two-fold cost of sex" — properly the cost of males, not the cost of meiosis
Full definition, symbols and units
Asexual reproduction
Production of offspring from a single parent without gamete fusion. Offspring are a clone — genetically identical to the parent except for new somatic mutations.
Parthenogenesis
Development of an unfertilised egg into an adult. Counted as asexual because no fusion occurs, but it is not automatically clonal: whether the offspring are identical to the mother depends on whether meiosis was bypassed (see the "Go deeper" panel).
Hermaphroditism
One individual makes both gamete types — simultaneously (earthworm, most land snails) or sequentially, changing sex with age or social rank.

Where the factor of two comes from

Take two females in the same population, each able to raise N offspring. The asexual female's offspring are all daughters, so her line multiplies by N each generation. The sexual female's offspring are half daughters and half sons, and sons do not bear young, so her line multiplies by N/2. The asexual lineage therefore doubles relative to the sexual one every single generation — an enormous advantage, and it should have driven sex extinct long ago.

Two things are worth noticing about that argument, because examiners test both. First, the cost is a cost of males, not a cost of meiosis: a simultaneous hermaphrodite that fertilises its partner's eggs while its own are fertilised pays no two-fold cost, because every individual bears young. An isogamous species — one where the two gametes are the same size, as in many algae — also escapes it. The cost appears the moment anisogamy appears, that is, the moment one gamete becomes large and provisioned and the other small and numerous. Second, the argument is about numbers of daughters, not about genes: the "each offspring gets only half my genome" version gives the same factor of two and is the usual textbook phrasing, but the demographic version is the one that survives scrutiny.

A textbook sentence that is not quite true

"Sexual reproduction is advantageous because it increases genetic variation." As written this is a distractor, not an explanation, and it is exactly the kind of sentence lifted verbatim into an option list. Variation is not intrinsically good. In a population already well adapted to a stable environment, recombination destroys the favourable combinations selection has just built up — this is the recombination load, and it is why highly successful clonal lineages exist (dandelions, bdelloid rotifers, many aphid populations in summer). Sex pays only under specific conditions: a fluctuating environment, coevolving parasites, or a high deleterious-mutation rate. Note also that the shuffling itself is done by meiosis — crossing over and independent assortment — and would happen even in a hermaphrodite fertilising itself; the partner adds outcrossing, which is a separate benefit.

The three explanations that are actually taught

  • Muller's ratchet. In a finite asexual population, the class of individuals carrying the fewest deleterious mutations can be lost by chance. With no recombination there is no way to reconstitute it, so the minimum mutation load ratchets upward, one click at a time, irreversibly. Recombination allows a lightly loaded genome to be reassembled from two heavily loaded parents, so the ratchet does not turn.
  • The Red Queen hypothesis. Parasites adapt to whatever host genotype is common. A clone is a standing target; a sexual population presents a moving one. The prediction is testable and has held up: in species with both modes (the New Zealand snail Potamopyrgus is the classic), sexual individuals are commonest exactly where parasite pressure is highest.
  • Fisher–Muller. Two beneficial mutations arising in different individuals can be combined into one genome by recombination. In an asexual population they must compete, and one is lost — "clonal interference". Sex therefore speeds adaptation whenever beneficial mutations are common enough to overlap.

Go deeper · parthenogenesis is two different things, and haplodiploidy is a third

Apomictic parthenogenesis suppresses meiosis altogether: the egg is produced by a mitotic division, is diploid from the start, and develops. The offspring are true clones. This is the aphid summer generation and the bdelloid rotifers.

Automictic parthenogenesis runs meiosis normally and then restores diploidy — by fusing the egg with a polar body, by duplicating the genome, or by suppressing one meiotic division. Crossing over has already happened, so the offspring are not clones: depending on which mechanism restores diploidy, heterozygosity is either halved each generation or preserved only near the centromeres. Parthenogenetic whiptail lizards (Aspidoscelis) get round this by pre-duplicating their chromosomes before meiosis, so each chromosome pairs with its own identical copy and heterozygosity survives intact.

Haplodiploidy in Hymenoptera is a different thing again, and confusing it with parthenogenesis-as-cloning is a classic error. A queen bee lays fertilised eggs, which are diploid and become females, and unfertilised eggs, which are haploid and become drones. The drone has no father and cannot have sons; his sperm are made by mitosis, so every sperm he produces is identical. This is why full sisters in a colony share on average r = 0.75 rather than 0.5, the standard kin-selection argument for eusociality.

Finally, note the terminology trap around sequential hermaphroditism. Protandry is male-first (clownfish: the largest fish in the anemone becomes the female); protogyny is female-first (bluehead wrasse: the largest becomes the territorial male). The direction is predicted by which sex gains more from being large. Options that swap the two are common.

External versus internal fertilisation, and what correlates with each

External fertilisation requires water, demands close synchrony (pheromones, photoperiod, lunar cues, courtship), produces very large numbers of small, cheap zygotes and typically little or no parental care — the r-selected end. Internal fertilisation frees the animal from water, allows sperm storage and female choice after mating, produces fewer and more expensive offspring, and is almost always accompanied by protection of the embryo — a shelled egg, a placenta, brooding. The examinable inference is directional: if a question gives you "lays 10⁶ eggs, no care", you can predict external fertilisation and aquatic habitat without being told. Note that internal fertilisation and internal development are separate: birds fertilise internally and develop externally, in a shelled amniote egg.

Exam technique · reproduction-strategy stems, answered from the numbers

  1. Read the offspring number first — it is the fastest single cue on this topic. Very many small offspring with no parental care means external fertilisation, water, and the r-selected end; few and expensive with protection means internal. You can give the habitat and the fertilisation mode from the brood size alone, without being told either.
  2. Then separate fertilisation from development — the standing trap is an option that treats them as one choice. Birds fertilise internally and develop externally in a shelled egg, and that single counter-example kills any option asserting that internal fertilisation implies live birth.
  3. If an option explains sex by "increasing genetic variation", suspect it — that is a sentence lifted from textbook prose, and it is a description rather than an explanation. Variation is not intrinsically good; recombination also breaks up favourable combinations, which is the recombination load and the reason successful clonal lineages exist. What the examiner wants named is a mechanism: Muller's ratchet, the Red Queen, or Fisher–Muller.
  4. Test any "cost of sex" option against a hermaphrodite — the two-fold cost is a cost of males. If the statement would still hold for a simultaneous hermaphrodite or an isogamous alga, it is not describing the two-fold cost and it is wrong. That one check disposes of the whole "cost of meiosis" family of distractors with no calculation at all.
  5. Ten seconds here, not sixty — §01 items are recognition, not reasoning. If you have not placed the stem in one of the four asexual modes or one of the three hypotheses within ten seconds, mark it and move on; the marks in this topic are cheap or they are not there.
§ 02

Gametogenesis: four sperm, one egg, two arrests

The ideaBoth sexes run the same meiosis, and yet one produces four small mobile cells from each starting cell and the other produces one huge stationary cell plus two scraps that are thrown away. That is not an accident of anatomy: sperm only have to deliver a nucleus, so making four is better than making one, while the egg has to supply the entire cytoplasm, the mitochondria, the stored messages and the food for the first days of the embryo, so all of the material must be kept in one cell. Everything else that differs between the two — when it starts, when it stops, how long it pauses — follows from that one difference in job description.

Definition & key facts

Gametogenesis
The production of haploid gametes from diploid germ cells: a mitotic proliferation phase, then meiosis, then a differentiation phase. The three phases are separated in time very differently in the two sexes.
1 primary spermatocyte → 4 spermatozoa · 1 primary oocyte → 1 ovum + 2–3 polar bodies equal cytokinesis versus deliberately unequal cytokinesis
Full definition, symbols and units
Spermiogenesis
The post-meiotic remodelling of a round spermatid into a spermatozoon. No division occurs. The Golgi becomes the acrosome, a centriole nucleates the flagellum, mitochondria wrap the midpiece, histones are replaced by protamines so the nucleus condenses, and most of the cytoplasm is shed as a residual body.
Meiotic arrest
A cell-cycle stop with a specific release signal. Human oocytes arrest twice: in prophase I (dictyate stage) from fetal life until the LH surge, and in metaphase II from ovulation until fertilisation.
Chromosome number vs chromatid number
Count centromeres for chromosomes and arms for chromatids. A cell that has replicated but not divided has 46 chromosomes and 92 chromatids; after meiosis I it has 23 chromosomes and 46 chromatids.

Spermatogenesis: continuous, symmetrical, and running on a conveyor

Spermatogenesis takes place in the seminiferous tubules and does not begin until puberty. A spermatogonium sitting on the basement membrane divides mitotically; one daughter stays behind as a stem cell and the other commits. The committed cell grows into a primary spermatocyte (46 chromosomes, 92 chromatids), completes meiosis I to give two secondary spermatocytes (23 chromosomes, 46 chromatids each), and each of those completes meiosis II to give two spermatids (23 chromosomes, 23 chromatids). Four haploid cells, all of them functional, all of them the same size. Then spermiogenesis reshapes each spermatid without any further division.

One structural detail explains something that otherwise looks impossible. Cytokinesis during spermatogenesis is incomplete: the daughter cells stay joined by cytoplasmic bridges until the very end, so a whole clone of developing spermatids shares one cytoplasm. That is why an X-bearing and a Y-bearing spermatid have identical phenotypes despite carrying different chromosomes — they are sharing gene products. It is also why male infertility caused by a single-gene defect is expressed uniformly across the whole sperm population rather than in half of it.

Which testicular cell answers which gonadotropin

LH → Leydig

The two Ls go together: LH acts on the Leydig cell, out in the interstitium, and the Leydig cell makes testosterone. Everything else then follows by exclusion — FSH must act on the Sertoli cell, inside the tubule, which nurses the germ cells, builds the blood–testis barrier and secretes inhibin B back onto FSH alone. This is the standard hook and it is worth carrying because the identical wiring is reused in the ovary — see the theca/granulosa pairing in §04. It says nothing about the spermatogonium, which responds to local signals and to neither gonadotropin.

Why the blood–testis barrier has to exist

Meiosis begins at puberty, long after the immune system has learned what "self" looks like. Haploid cells expressing recombined, previously unseen antigens are therefore foreign to their own owner. The Sertoli-cell tight junctions sequester every cell from the primary spermatocyte onward in an immunologically privileged compartment. Break the barrier — trauma, vasectomy reversal, mumps orchitis — and anti-sperm antibodies can appear. This is a favourite because it links development to immunology (note 12) and explains an anatomical fact from a functional requirement rather than by assertion.

Oogenesis: finite, asymmetric, and paused for decades

Oogonia proliferate only during fetal life. The pool peaks at roughly 6.8 million around 20 weeks of gestation, then falls continuously by atresia — 1–2 million at birth, 300 000–400 000 at puberty, and of those only about 450 are ever ovulated. Every one of the survivors entered meiosis in the fetus and stopped in prophase I. A cell ovulated at age 40 has been sitting in that arrest for four decades.

At each cycle, the LH surge releases the arrest in the dominant follicle. Meiosis I completes just before ovulation, and the division is deliberately unequal: essentially all the cytoplasm goes to the secondary oocyte and a sliver goes to the first polar body. The secondary oocyte then immediately arrests again, at metaphase II, and that is the cell that is ovulated. It stays arrested unless a sperm arrives; the Ca²⁺ signal at fertilisation is what releases it, and only then is meiosis II completed and the second polar body extruded.

Three sentences about the egg that are wrong, and are all in circulation

"The ovary releases an ovum." It releases a secondary oocyte arrested at metaphase II. The word ovum is correctly used only after meiosis II is finished, which happens after fertilisation. "The sperm nucleus fuses with the egg nucleus to form the zygote nucleus." In mammals the two pronuclear envelopes break down and the chromosomes assemble on a shared first mitotic spindle; there is no moment at which a single diploid nucleus is assembled by fusion, and the first true diploid nuclei appear in the 2-cell embryo. "Human females are born with all the eggs they will ever have." This one is nearly right and is the version to give on the exam, but the cells present at birth are primary oocytes in prophase-I arrest, not eggs, and the claim's real content is that the mitotic proliferation phase is over — which is the examinable point.

Which arrest is which, and what breaks it

P before ovulation, M after

Prophase I is the long wait, and it is broken by the LH surge, just before ovulation. Metaphase II is the short wait, and it is broken by the sperm's Ca²⁺ signal, after ovulation. So the cell sitting in the tube is a secondary oocyte at metaphase II — the one arrest that ovulation does not end. Holds for humans and most mammals. It does not hold for dogs and foxes, which ovulate a primary oocyte still in prophase I — a favourite five-option distractor.

Why maternal age raises trisomy risk — the mechanism, not just the graph

Sister-chromatid cohesion is established by cohesin complexes (meiotic kleisin REC8) loaded during fetal S phase and, crucially, never reloaded. Every year the oocyte spends in prophase-I arrest, cohesin is lost by attrition. When meiosis I finally runs, weakened cohesion allows premature separation of sister chromatids and mis-segregation of the bivalent. That is why the great majority of trisomy 21 conceptions arise from an error in maternal meiosis I, and why the risk curve is steep and late: roughly 1 in 1500 at age 20, 1 in 350 at 35, 1 in 100 at 40. It is also why paternal age raises point mutation rates but not aneuploidy — sperm are made fresh, from stem cells that have simply divided many more times.

Exam technique · ploidy items in under thirty seconds

  1. Read which quantity was asked — chromosomes, chromatids, or DNA content (C)? These are three different numbers for the same cell, and most lost marks on this topic are a correct answer to the wrong question. Underline the word before you look at the options.
  2. Count centromeres for chromosomes — replication doubles chromatids and DNA but leaves chromosome number untouched, because sister chromatids share one centromere. Only anaphase changes chromosome number, and only anaphase I halves it.
  3. Fix two anchors and derive the rest — primary spermatocyte / primary oocyte = 46 chr, 92 chromatids, 4C; secondary spermatocyte / secondary oocyte = 23 chr, 46 chromatids, 2C. Every other stage is one step from one of these.
  4. Use "primary" and "secondary" as the switch — "secondary" always means meiosis I is finished, so the cell is already haploid however many chromatids it has. That single word decides half of these items without any counting.
  5. Then eliminate on impossibility — "a primary oocyte has 92 chromosomes", "a secondary oocyte is diploid", "a spermatid has 46 chromatids" are all internally impossible and can go before you have worked anything out. With five options and negative marking, two dead options are enough to make answering strictly better than leaving it blank.
Worked example 1 · Ploidy bookkeeping through both pathways

State the chromosome number and the chromatid number for each cell, and how many gametes result

A human germ cell (2n = 46) is followed from before S phase to the finished gamete, in each sex. Give chromosomes and chromatids at: (a) before S; (b) primary spermatocyte / primary oocyte; (c) secondary spermatocyte / secondary oocyte; (d) spermatid / ovum. Then say how many functional gametes each starting cell yields.

  1. Before S phase. 46 chromosomes, each one chromatid → 46 chromosomes, 46 chromatids (2C).
  2. After S phase — primary spermatocyte or primary oocyte. Replication doubles chromatids but does not change chromosome number, because sister chromatids share one centromere → 46 chromosomes, 92 chromatids (4C). This is the cell that arrests in prophase I in the female.
  3. After meiosis I. Homologues separate. Each daughter gets one member of each pair, still as two attached chromatids → 23 chromosomes, 46 chromatids (2C). This is the ovulated cell in the female — the secondary oocyte at metaphase-II arrest.
  4. After meiosis II. Sisters separate → 23 chromosomes, 23 chromatids (1C).
  5. Yield. Male: 1 → 2 → 4, all equal, all functional. Female: 1 → 1 usable + 1 polar body → 1 usable + 1 more polar body; the first polar body may or may not divide, giving 2 or 3 in total.

46/92 → 23/46 → 23/23 · 4 sperm vs 1 ovum + 2–3 polar bodies

The trap is step 2. A stem that says "a primary oocyte contains 92 chromosomes" is false — it contains 92 chromatids. Count centromeres, always.

§ 03

The male and female reproductive systems

The ideaBoth tracts are the same shape of problem: make the gamete somewhere protected, mature it somewhere else, then move it. In the male everything downstream of the testis exists to finish and deliver a cell that leaves the body; in the female everything exists to receive, select, transport and then house. Almost every anatomical fact here is a solution to a physical constraint — temperature, pH, distance, immune privilege — so it is worth asking "what problem does this solve?" rather than memorising a list of tubes.

Definition & key facts

Semen
Sperm suspended in the combined secretions of the accessory glands. Sperm cells contribute under 5% of the volume; the fluid does the work of buffering, feeding and protecting them.

Ampulla

The male tract, read as a sequence of problems solved

Sperm leaving the seminiferous tubules are immotile and infertile. They acquire progressive motility and the ability to bind the zona during roughly two weeks of transit and storage in the epididymis. This is the fact behind a common exam item: the testis makes sperm, the epididymis finishes and stores them. The vas deferens is a muscular conduit, not a maturation site, which is why a vasectomy leaves ejaculate volume essentially unchanged — the sperm were less than 5% of it.

The scrotum exists because human spermatogenesis fails at core temperature. Three mechanisms hold the testis 2–3 °C cooler: the scrotum's position outside the abdomen, the cremaster and dartos muscles that raise or lower it, and the pampiniform plexus, a countercurrent heat exchanger in which venous blood leaving the testis cools the arterial blood entering it. Cryptorchidism — a testis retained in the abdomen — causes infertility and raises germ-cell tumour risk for exactly this reason.

Erection and ejaculation are opposite autonomic divisions — "point and shoot"

Erection is parasympathetic (pelvic splanchnic nerves, S2–S4): nitric oxide is released, activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, raises cGMP, and cGMP relaxes the smooth muscle of the helicine arteries so the sinusoids of the corpora cavernosa fill. That is why PDE5 inhibitors need arousal to work — they amplify a signal, they do not create one. Emission is sympathetic (L1–L2, moving semen into the urethra and closing the bladder neck), and expulsion is somatic (pudendal nerve, bulbospongiosus). Damage the sympathetic outflow and you get retrograde ejaculation into the bladder. The mnemonic — Parasympathetic Point, Sympathetic Shoot — is worth carrying because the two are routinely swapped in options.

Which autonomic division does which half of the act

Point and Shoot

Point = Parasympathetic (S2–S4, nitric oxide, cGMP, vasodilation → erection). Shoot = Sympathetic (L1–L2, emission of semen into the urethra and closure of the bladder neck). It covers only two of the three steps. The final expulsion is somatic — pudendal nerve to the bulbospongiosus — which is why the reflex survives sympathetic damage while emission does not, giving retrograde ejaculation.

The female tract, and the gap that matters

The ovary is not continuous with the uterine tube. At ovulation the oocyte is expelled into the peritoneal cavity and swept in by the beating cilia of the fimbriae. That anatomical gap is why an ectopic pregnancy can implant in the tube — where roughly 95% of ectopics occur, most of them in the ampulla — and, rarely, on peritoneal surfaces. Sperm swim up through the cervix, whose mucus is a filter whose properties are hormonally switched: thin, watery and aligned in the oestrogen-dominated late follicular phase (permissive), thick and tangled under progesterone (obstructive). Progestin-only contraception works largely by keeping the mucus in the obstructive state.

The uterine wall is three layers: perimetrium (serosa), myometrium (thick smooth muscle — the layer that contracts in labour and the site of fibroids), and endometrium. Only the endometrium's functional layer is shed at menstruation, which is what makes monthly regeneration possible at all.

Sexual differentiation — the default is female, and the two hormones do different jobs

Until week 7 the embryo has an indifferent gonad and both duct systems: Müllerian (paramesonephric) and Wolffian (mesonephric). SRY on the short arm of the Y drives the gonad to a testis. Two separate testicular products then act: Sertoli cells secrete anti-Müllerian hormone, which causes the Müllerian ducts to regress, and Leydig cells secrete testosterone, which maintains the Wolffian ducts as epididymis, vas deferens and seminal vesicle. Testosterone is converted by 5α-reductase to dihydrotestosterone in the target tissue, and it is DHT — not testosterone — that masculinises the external genitalia and the prostate. Remove SRY and nothing happens: no AMH, so the Müllerian ducts persist as tube, uterus and upper vagina; no androgen, so the Wolffian ducts regress. Two clinical consequences make excellent five-option items. In complete androgen insensitivity (46,XY, defective androgen receptor), AMH still works, so there is no uterus, and androgen does not, so the external phenotype is female with intra-abdominal testes and high testosterone. In 5α-reductase deficiency, internal male ducts are normal (testosterone acts) but external genitalia are undervirilised at birth (DHT does not), and virilise at puberty when testosterone concentrations rise high enough to act directly.

§ 04

The ovarian and uterine cycles, and their feedback

The ideaA control loop that only ever damps things down can hold a value steady, but it can never produce a sudden event. The ovarian cycle needs both: quiet, regulated growth of a follicle for two weeks, and then one explosive burst of hormone that ruptures it. Nature solves this by building a loop whose sign changes. For most of the cycle oestrogen tells the brain to slow down; once oestrogen has been high for long enough, the same hormone at the same receptors starts telling the brain to speed up, and the resulting runaway spike is the LH surge. Everything about cycle timing, every contraceptive, and every fertility drug is a manipulation of that one sign flip.

Walking the loop once

Early follicular phase. At the end of the previous cycle the corpus luteum died, so progesterone, oestradiol and inhibin A all fell. Removing that inhibition lets FSH rise — and that small intercycle FSH rise is what recruits the next cohort of antral follicles. Note the logic: the trigger for the new cycle is the death of the previous corpus luteum, not menstruation, which is merely the visible consequence.

Mid-follicular phase. Growing granulosa cells secrete oestradiol and inhibin B. Inhibin B suppresses FSH selectively — it acts on the pituitary gonadotrope and not on GnRH — so FSH falls while LH does not. Now only the follicle that has accumulated the most FSH receptors can keep growing in a falling-FSH environment. That is dominant follicle selection: the cohort is not chosen, it is starved down to one. It also explains why exogenous FSH in IVF produces many follicles — it removes the starvation.

Late follicular phase and the flip. The dominant follicle now produces oestradiol far faster than the cohort did. Once plasma oestradiol has been above roughly 200 pg·mL⁻¹ for about 50 hours, the response of the hypothalamic–pituitary unit reverses: GnRH pulse frequency rises, pituitary GnRH receptor number increases, gonadotrope sensitivity rises steeply, and LH is released as a surge rather than a trickle. Because more LH drives more oestradiol, this is genuine positive feedback — it runs away, and it stops only because the follicle ruptures and the source is removed.

Ovulation. About 36 hours after the surge begins, the follicle wall is digested by LH-induced proteases and prostaglandins and the cumulus–oocyte complex is expelled. The LH surge does three things at once, and questions like to separate them: it triggers rupture, it releases the oocyte's prophase-I arrest so meiosis I completes, and it luteinises the remaining granulosa and theca cells, switching them from oestrogen production to progesterone production.

Luteal phase. The corpus luteum secretes progesterone (with some oestradiol and inhibin A). Progesterone converts the endometrium to its secretory form, thickens cervical mucus, quiets the myometrium and raises the hypothalamic temperature set point by 0.3–0.5 °C — the basal body temperature shift, which is retrospective evidence of ovulation, not a prediction of it. Progesterone plus oestradiol together are strongly inhibitory at the hypothalamus and pituitary, so FSH and LH stay low and no new follicle can be recruited. With no hCG, the corpus luteum regresses after about 14 days, steroid support is withdrawn, spiral arteries constrict, the functional layer becomes ischaemic and is shed — and the loop restarts.

The two-cell model, if you already know the testis

Theca is Leydig; Granulosa is Sertoli

The ovary is the testis rewired, and each gonadotropin keeps its partner. Theca is the LH cell, the ovary's Leydig cell: it makes the androgen. Neither cell can make oestradiol alone, and that is the entire content of "two-cell, two-gonadotropin". The homology is real anatomy rather than a coincidence, so it will not let you down — but only the ovarian version ever produces a surge, because only the ovary drives oestradiol high enough for long enough to flip the sign of the feedback.

Four claims that sound like textbook sentences and are false

"Ovulation occurs on day 14." Only in a 28-day cycle. The luteal phase is the fixed one; ovulation is at cycle length minus 14. "Oestrogen inhibits FSH and LH." True for most of the cycle and false at exactly the moment that matters. "The corpus luteum degenerates because oestrogen levels fall." It degenerates on an intrinsic schedule of about 14 days unless hCG arrives; nothing has to actively kill it. "Menstruation is caused by the fall in oestrogen." It is caused by withdrawal of progesterone support from a progesterone-dependent secretory endometrium — which is why a progestin-withdrawal bleed can be induced pharmacologically and why continuous progestin can abolish bleeding entirely.

Exam technique · "what happens to FSH, LH, E₂ and P₄ if …"

  1. Draw the three boxes before reading the options — hypothalamus → pituitary → ovary, with the return arrow. Ten seconds, and it converts the whole question class into arrow-following.
  2. Find where the stem cuts the loop — remove the ovary, or exhaust it at menopause, and both return signals go, so FSH and LH rise. Block aromatase and only oestradiol goes, so gonadotropins rise but inhibin still restrains FSH. Give an exogenous steroid and you have added to the return arm, so FSH and LH fall.
  3. Then ask: constant or sustained-and-rising? Constant moderate oestrogen acts on the negative arm only — that is the combined pill. Oestradiol above ≈200 pg·mL⁻¹ held for ≈50 h flips the sign and gives the surge. Same molecule, same receptor, opposite answer, decided by the pattern. The identical trick runs on GnRH: pulsatile stimulates, continuous shuts the axis down.
  4. Use inhibin as a fingerprint — if the stem moves FSH alone, or asks why FSH and LH part company, inhibin is in the answer. It acts on the gonadotrope only, so it can never explain an LH change.
  5. Never answer "day 14" from reflex — ovulation is at cycle length − 14, because the luteal phase is the fixed one. If the stem bothered to state a cycle length other than 28, that is the whole question.
  6. Spend the 66 seconds on the direction, not the numbers — these items are almost always won or lost on one arrow's sign. If two options differ only in whether a hormone rises or falls, decide that first and the rest of the option usually decides itself.
Go deeper · how the sign flip is actually implemented, and what every contraceptive does to this loop

The reversal is not a property of the oestrogen receptor changing its mind. It is implemented by two anatomically separate populations of kisspeptin neurons upstream of the GnRH cells, both expressing oestrogen receptor α but responding oppositely. Neurons in the arcuate nucleus (the KNDy population — kisspeptin, neurokinin B, dynorphin) are inhibited by oestradiol and generate the tonic GnRH pulse; they are the negative-feedback arm. Neurons in the anteroventral periventricular nucleus are stimulated by sustained oestradiol and drive the surge; they are the positive-feedback arm and, in rodents, are markedly sexually dimorphic — which is why males do not surge. Loss-of-function mutations in KISS1R cause hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with a completely normal pituitary and ovary, which is the clean genetic proof that this layer exists. At the pituitary, sustained oestradiol simultaneously increases GnRH receptor expression and builds a releasable LH pool, so the same GnRH input produces a much larger output — amplification on top of the upstream switch.

Now read the pharmacology off the loop:

  • Combined oral contraceptive. A constant, moderate level of oestrogen plus progestin. Because it is constant, it never crosses the "high and rising for 50 h" condition, so it only ever acts on the negative arm. FSH never rises enough to recruit a dominant follicle, no endogenous oestradiol peak is generated, and there is no LH surge. This is the answer to "how can a pill containing oestrogen prevent ovulation?" — the sign of the feedback depends on the pattern, not the molecule.
  • Progestin-only methods. Ovulation suppression is inconsistent; the reliable effects are thick cervical mucus and a thin, unreceptive endometrium.
  • Clomiphene. A selective oestrogen receptor modulator that blocks the receptor at the hypothalamus. The brain reads oestradiol as low, releases FSH, and follicles are recruited — an induced ovulation by removing negative feedback.
  • Letrozole. Aromatase inhibitor: less oestradiol is made in the first place, same consequence, and now first-line for ovulation induction in polycystic ovary syndrome.
  • GnRH agonists. Given continuously they abolish pulsatility and shut the axis down after an initial flare. Given in pulses they do the opposite. Same drug, opposite effect, decided by the dosing pattern — a favourite item.
  • hCG "trigger". Given in IVF to imitate the LH surge, because it binds the same receptor and has a much longer half-life; egg retrieval is timed about 34–36 h later, exactly the natural surge-to-ovulation interval.

Menopause falls out of the same diagram. The follicle pool is exhausted, so oestradiol and inhibin production ceases; both negative-feedback signals disappear; FSH and LH rise and stay high, with FSH higher than LH because inhibin, the FSH-selective brake, is gone as well and because FSH is cleared more slowly. A raised FSH with a low oestradiol is therefore the biochemical signature, and its pattern tells you the lesion is in the ovary rather than in the pituitary.

Worked example 2 · Reading a 35-day cycle

A woman has regular 35-day cycles. Give the day of ovulation, the day the LH surge begins, the fertile window, the day for a mid-luteal progesterone, and the day the temperature shift appears.

  1. Ovulation. The luteal phase is fixed at 14 days, so ovulation is at 35 − 14 = day 21. The follicular phase is therefore 21 days long — all of the extra length sits before ovulation, not after it.
  2. LH surge onset. Ovulation follows surge onset by about 36 h = 1.5 days, so the surge begins around day 19–20. An ovulation-predictor kit detects urinary LH and therefore turns positive about a day and a half before the egg is released — which is exactly what makes it useful.
  3. Fertile window. Sperm survive up to about 5 days in the female tract; the oocyte survives 12–24 h. The window is therefore about six days, days 16 to 21, and it ends at ovulation. Intercourse the day after ovulation is essentially unproductive.
  4. Mid-luteal progesterone. Seven days after ovulation, i.e. 21 + 7 = day 28. The clinical shorthand "a day-21 progesterone" is correct only for a 28-day cycle; applying it here would sample the day of ovulation itself and return a spuriously low value. This is a real error and a very good distractor.
  5. Temperature shift. Progesterone from the new corpus luteum raises basal body temperature by 0.3–0.5 °C, appearing about day 22–23 — after the fact. Basal body temperature confirms that ovulation happened; it never predicts it.

§ 05

Fertilisation and the blocks to polyspermy

The ideaThe egg has two contradictory requirements. It must be easy enough to enter that one sperm out of a few hundred survivors can get in, and it must be impossible to enter one microsecond later, because a second sperm would be fatal. No single mechanism can be both fast and permanent, so the egg uses two in series: an electrical change that happens in a second and wears off in a minute, and a chemical rebuilding of the coat that takes a minute and lasts forever. The same calcium signal that starts the slow block also wakes the egg up — it restarts a cell cycle that has been frozen since ovulation.

Definition & key facts

Acrosome reaction
Exocytosis of the sperm's acrosomal vesicle — a modified lysosome derived from the Golgi — releasing hydrolytic enzymes (acrosin, hyaluronidase) that let the sperm digest a path through the egg coat. It is triggered by contact with the coat and requires prior capacitation.
fast block: Vm −70 mV → +20 mV in 1–3 s (sea urchin) · slow block: Ca²⁺ wave → cortical reaction, ≈1 min one is electrical and temporary; the other is structural and permanent

Exocytosis of thousands of cortical granules lying just under the egg's plasma membrane, triggered by the Ca²⁺ wave. Their enzymes modify the egg coat from outside so that no further sperm can bind. In sea urchins this lifts the vitelline layer into a hardened fertilisation envelope; in mammals it is the zona reaction.
Egg activation
The wholesale metabolic awakening triggered by the same Ca²⁺ signal: protein synthesis and respiration rise sharply using stored maternal mRNA, and — in mammals — the metaphase-II arrest is released so meiosis II finishes.

Entry of more than one sperm. Lethal not only because of the extra genome but because of the extra centrosome: a tripolar or multipolar first spindle distributes chromosomes chaotically.

The sequence, in order

  1. Capacitation. Hours in the female tract. Without it there is no hyperactivated motility and no competence to acrosome-react. This is why ejaculated sperm placed on an egg in a dish will not fertilise it unless the medium capacitates them first.
  2. Penetration of the cumulus. Hyaluronidase on the sperm surface (and released from the acrosome) loosens the hyaluronan matrix holding the cumulus cells together.
  3. Binding to the egg coat. Species-specific. In sea urchins the acrosomal protein bindin on the acrosomal process binds a receptor on the vitelline layer — the classical demonstration that gamete recognition is molecular and that it enforces reproductive isolation in a free-spawning animal. In mammals the coat is the zona pellucida.
  4. Acrosome reaction. Hydrolytic enzymes digest a channel through the coat; the sperm's hyperactivated tail provides the thrust.
  5. Membrane fusion. IZUMO1 on the equatorial segment of the sperm binds JUNO on the oolemma; the membranes fuse and the sperm nucleus, centriole and PLCζ enter.
  6. Fast block (sea urchin): Na⁺ influx depolarises the egg from about −70 mV to about +20 mV within 1–3 s, and a depolarised membrane will not permit further sperm fusion. It decays over about a minute — just long enough to cover the gap until the slow block is complete.
  7. Ca²⁺ wave. Sperm PLCζ hydrolyses PIP₂ to IP₃; IP₃ opens ER calcium channels; released Ca²⁺ opens more channels, so the release is regenerative and sweeps across the egg as a wave starting at the sperm entry point.
  8. Cortical reaction / slow block. Ca²⁺ triggers cortical-granule exocytosis. In mammals the released protease ovastacin cleaves ZP2, and the zona hardens and loses its sperm-binding capacity.
  9. Egg activation and completion of meiosis II. Ca²⁺ acts through CaMKII to destroy the arrest-maintaining machinery; the second polar body is extruded, and the two pronuclei approach, replicate their DNA and deliver their chromosomes to a shared first mitotic spindle.

Which block is which, and which animal it belongs to

Fast Flash, Slow Seal

Fast is a flash: electrical, a depolarisation from about −70 mV to +20 mV within 1–3 s, and it fades in about a minute — and it is a sea-urchin finding, never demonstrated in mammals. Slow is a seal: structural, driven by the Ca²⁺ wave through cortical-granule exocytosis, complete by about 60 s, and it sticks — permanent. Two Fs against two Ss, and the mammal question answers itself. My own construction, not a hook you will meet in other material. It does not carry the machinery, and the machinery is where the second trap sits: the fast block is triggered by Na⁺ influx, the slow block by Ca²⁺ released from the endoplasmic reticulum, and those two ions are routinely swapped in options.

The mammal fast-block trap, and three neighbours

The fast electrical block is a sea urchin result. Mammalian eggs show no comparable fertilisation potential, and the standard textbook position is that mammals rely on the zona reaction plus the sheer scarcity of sperm at the site of fertilisation — of a few hundred million ejaculated, only a hundred or two ever reach the oocyte. An option asserting a 1–3 s depolarisation "in mammals" is wrong. Three neighbouring errors: (i) cortical granules do not fuse with each other — they fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outward, which is ordinary exocytosis; (ii) the Ca²⁺ that drives the wave comes overwhelmingly from the endoplasmic reticulum, not from outside the cell; (iii) the sperm does not "activate the egg by depolarising it" — activation is the Ca²⁺ signal, and injecting Ca²⁺ or even a single sperm protein (PLCζ) into an unfertilised egg activates it and can start parthenogenetic development with no sperm at all.

Exam technique · fertilisation options you can kill without solving anything

  1. Scan for the word "mammal" sitting next to a depolarisation — the fast electrical block is a sea-urchin result. Any option granting mammals a 1–3 s fertilisation potential is dead on sight, and something close to it appears in almost every set written on this topic.
  2. Check where the Ca²⁺ came from — the wave is IP₃-released from the endoplasmic reticulum, not influx from outside. "Ca²⁺ entering across the plasma membrane triggers the cortical reaction" is a fluent sentence and a wrong one.
  3. Check what the cortical granules fuse with — the plasma membrane, releasing their contents outward; this is ordinary exocytosis. An option in which granules fuse with one another, or build the envelope from the inside out, is describing cell biology that does not exist.
  4. Use the vocabulary itself as a filter — "ovum" is correct only after meiosis II is finished, so an option in which the ovary releases an ovum is gone before you consider its content; and in mammals the two pronuclei do not fuse into a zygote nucleus, they deliver chromosomes to a shared first spindle. Two free eliminations, and with five options and negative marking two is enough to make answering strictly better than a blank.
  5. For the consequences of a failed block, count chromosomes — three haploid sets is 3 × 23 = 69, and the extra centrosome matters as much as the extra genome because it gives a multipolar first spindle. 69 chromosomes with an excess paternal genome is the partial mole; 46 chromosomes but entirely paternal is the complete mole. If the stem says there is no fetal tissue at all, it is the complete one.

Clinical corollary: what a failed block produces

A dispermic conceptus is triploid — 69 chromosomes, typically 69,XXY — with two paternal genomes and one maternal. Because imprinted paternal genes drive trophoblast growth and maternal ones drive embryonic growth, an excess of paternal genome gives a partial hydatidiform mole: some fetal tissue, abnormal villi, raised hCG. The extreme case is the complete mole, 46 chromosomes but entirely paternal — an oocyte that has lost its own genome, fertilised and the paternal set duplicated. No embryo at all, only trophoblast, and hCG far above the normal range. This pair is the single cleanest demonstration in human biology that the two parental genomes are not interchangeable, which is what makes genomic imprinting necessary and mammalian parthenogenesis impossible.

Go deeper · ZP3 versus ZP2, and where the textbook has quietly moved on

The version in most textbooks, and the version to give if an exam option demands it, is: ZP3 is the primary sperm receptor and also the trigger of the acrosome reaction; ZP2 is a secondary receptor that holds the acrosome-reacted sperm; the cortical reaction modifies ZP3 so no further sperm can bind. It was built on elegant mouse experiments in the 1980s in which purified ZP3 competed for sperm binding.

Three lines of evidence have since complicated it, and it is worth knowing them because they are exactly the sort of "beyond-textbook" material that shows up in a minority of items. First, live imaging of mouse sperm carrying fluorescent acrosome markers showed that most sperm reaching the zona have already acrosome-reacted, in the cumulus or even before — so ZP3-triggered reaction at the zona surface is not the normal route. Second, genetic replacement experiments point to the N-terminus of ZP2 as the ligand that supports sperm binding: mice whose ZP2 cannot be cleaved by ovastacin have zonae that keep binding sperm after fertilisation, and mice expressing a non-cleavable ZP2 fail the block. Third, the fusion step itself was unknown until IZUMO1 (2005, named for a Japanese marriage shrine) and its egg receptor JUNO (2014) were identified; Juno-null females are infertile and JUNO is shed from the oolemma within about 40 minutes of fertilisation, which may be a membrane-level contribution to the block.

The transferable lesson is the one this note keeps returning to: when a mechanism is stated as a chain of named molecules, ask which step was demonstrated in vivo and which was inferred from a binding assay. That habit is what lets you reject a fluent, textbook-sounding option.

§ 06

Cleavage: how yolk decides the pattern

The ideaThe zygote is a single enormous cell with far too much cytoplasm per nucleus to behave normally. Cleavage fixes that by dividing without growing: the same volume is cut into more and more pieces until the cells are ordinary size. Because nothing grows, the divisions can be extremely fast — they skip the growth phases of the cell cycle entirely. And because a cleavage furrow cannot cut through a dense mass of yolk, the amount and position of the yolk decides the geometry: little yolk and the egg divides right through; a lot of yolk and the divisions are confined to a small disc sitting on top of it.

Definition & key facts

Cleavage
The series of rapid mitotic divisions immediately after fertilisation, with no growth between divisions. The embryo's total volume stays roughly constant; cell number rises and cell size falls. G₁ and G₂ are skipped, so the cycle is essentially S–M–S–M.

Holoblastic vs meroblastic
Holoblastic cleavage cuts completely through the egg; meroblastic cleavage is incomplete, because the furrow cannot penetrate the yolk. Which one you get is determined entirely by yolk.
Blastula / blastocyst

Mid-blastula transition

Yolk, and the four patterns it produces

Yolk is stored nutrient. Its amount is set by how long the embryo must survive before it can feed itself, and its distribution is set by whether the egg was polarised before it was laid. An egg with little yolk evenly spread (isolecithal) divides completely and fairly evenly. An egg with a moderate vegetal concentration (mesolecithal, the amphibians) still divides completely, but the vegetal furrow is slowed by the yolk, so the third cleavage lands above the equator and produces small animal-pole micromeres and large vegetal macromeres. An egg with a huge yolk mass (telolecithal, birds and most fish) cannot be cut at all, so cleavage is confined to a blastodisc of cytoplasm on top of it. And an egg with the yolk in the middle and cytoplasm at the rim (centrolecithal, insects) divides its nuclei without dividing its cytoplasm at all, producing a syncytium that is only cellularised later, from the outside in.

There is no mnemonic for that four-row table and you do not need one, because the Greek already encodes it. Lekithos is egg yolk — the same root as lecithin — so each term reads as "yolk that is …": iso-, equal; meso-, middling; telo-, at one end; centro-, in the middle. The cleavage term then falls straight out of the one physical fact above: a furrow cannot cut yolk, so little yolk gives holoblastic (whole) cleavage and a great deal gives meroblastic (partial). And the shape of a partial cleavage is simply wherever the yolk is not — a disc on top of an end-loaded egg, the surface of a centre-loaded one. Six word-roots regenerate the table on demand, which is strictly better than holding four arbitrary pairings and hoping they have not drifted.

Radial vs spiral, indeterminate vs determinate — a clade-typical rule, not a law

Deuterostomes typically show radial cleavage (blastomeres sit directly above one another) that is indeterminate or regulative — separate the cells of a 4-cell sea urchin and each can give a complete, smaller larva, which is also the reason identical twins are possible in mammals. Protostomes typically show spiral cleavage (successive tiers are offset by 45°, so cells nest in the grooves between the tier below) that is determinate or mosaic — each blastomere's fate is already fixed by inherited cytoplasmic determinants, so an isolated blastomere makes only its own part. Two cautions. First, this is a correlation across clades, not a definition: the clades are defined phylogenetically, and there are protostomes without spiral cleavage (nematodes, arthropods) and regulative behaviour in some of them. Second, "determinate" does not mean the cell has already differentiated — it means it is determined, that is, committed. The visible differentiation comes much later.

The mammalian route, which is different in three ways

Mammalian cleavage is slow (about one division per 12–24 h, against roughly 30–60 min in a sea urchin and 8–10 min for the early syncytial nuclear divisions of a fly), asynchronous (so you observe 3-cell and 5-cell embryos, which never happens in a sea urchin), and rotational — the first division is meridional, and at the second division one blastomere divides meridionally while the other divides equatorially. Zygotic transcription starts unusually early, at the 4–8 cell stage in humans, which is consistent with there being almost no maternal stockpile to live on.

At the 8-cell stage the embryo compacts: E-cadherin-mediated adhesion flattens the cells against one another and establishes an inside–outside distinction, which is the first positional information in the mammalian embryo. Outside cells become trophoblast; inside cells become the inner cell mass. By day 5 the fluid-filled blastocyst has formed, by day 5–6 it hatches from the zona, and implantation begins around day 6–7. Note the sequence for the exam: fertilisation in the ampulla, cleavage during transit down the tube, arrival in the uterus as a morula at about day 3–4, blastocyst at day 5, implantation at day 6–7. A conceptus that implants before it reaches the uterus is an ectopic pregnancy.

Exam technique · cleavage stems, and where the days go

  1. Find the yolk word and stop reading — one term in the stem decides the whole pattern question. Iso- or meso-lecithal → holoblastic (equal, then unequal); telolecithal → meroblastic discoidal; centrolecithal → meroblastic superficial. If the stem names an animal instead of a yolk type, convert it first: sea urchin and mammal iso, frog meso, bird and most fish telo, insect centro.
  2. Place any stated day on the mammalian timeline — fertilisation in the ampulla, morula reaching the uterus at about day 3–4, blastocyst at day 5, hatching from the zona day 5–6, implantation day 6–7. A conceptus that implants before it reaches the uterus is by definition ectopic, which is what a stem about tubal implantation is really asking.
  3. On a clade-character option, read to the very end — these options bundle four features (cleavage geometry, determinacy, coelom formation, blastopore fate) and make exactly one of them wrong. Deuterostome = radial + indeterminate + enterocoelous + blastopore→anus; protostome = spiral + determinate + schizocoelous + blastopore→mouth. The coelom term is the one most often swapped, so check that word first and you will often finish the item there.
  4. Do not let "determinate" slide into "differentiated" — determinate means committed, and commitment is invisible. An option saying an isolated protostome blastomere "has already differentiated" is wrong for a reason that costs nothing to spot.
  5. Sanity-check any size answer against a real cell — cleavage divides volume, so diameters fall as the cube root of the cell number. Anything below about 1 µm is a method error rather than an arithmetic slip, and should be discarded rather than rechecked.
Worked example 3 · Cleavage arithmetic on a frog egg

A Xenopus egg is 1.2 mm in diameter. It undergoes 12 cleavage divisions with no growth. How many cells are there, how big is each, and by how much has the surface-to-volume ratio changed?

  1. Cell number. Each division doubles the count, so N = 2¹² = 4096 cells. (This is exactly the mid-blastula transition in Xenopus — not a coincidence, since the transition is triggered by the nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio reaching a threshold.)
  2. Cell volume. Total volume is unchanged, so each cell has 1/4096 of the original volume.
  3. Cell diameter. Volume scales as diameter cubed, so diameter falls by the cube root of 4096. Since 40961/3 = 16, d = 1200 µm / 16 = 75 µm.
  4. Surface-to-volume ratio. S/V scales as 1/d, so it has risen by the same factor, 16×. That is the entire purpose of cleavage: to convert one cell with a hopeless surface-to-volume ratio into thousands with a normal one, without adding any material.

4096 cells · 75 µm each · S/V up 16-fold

The classic wrong turn is to divide the diameter by the cell number (1200/4096 ≈ 0.29 µm — smaller than a bacterium, which should ring an alarm). Volumes divide; lengths divide by the cube root. A second wrong turn is 2¹¹ = 2048, from counting the zygote as the first division; d would then be 94 µm.

§ 07

Gastrulation and the three germ layers

The ideaUp to now the embryo has only made more cells. Gastrulation is the first time cells move, and the movement is what creates the body plan: a hollow ball rearranges itself into a tube within a tube, with a third layer packed in between. Once that has happened the embryo has an inside and an outside, a front and a back, and three sheets of tissue each of which will go on to make a fixed and predictable set of organs. Nothing else in development changes the animal so much in so little time, which is why the biologist Lewis Wolpert's line is quoted so often: it is not birth, marriage or death, but gastrulation, that is truly the most important time in your life.

Definition & key facts

Gastrulation
The set of coordinated cell movements — invagination, involution, ingression, delamination, epiboly and convergent extension — that converts the blastula into a three-layered gastrula with a primitive gut (archenteron) opening at the blastopore. Cell division contributes almost nothing; this is migration, not proliferation.
ectoderm = outside & nervous · mesoderm = between & support · endoderm = inside & lining the three-word version that recovers most of the derivative table
Full definition, symbols and units
Blastopore
The opening of the archenteron. In protostomes ("first mouth") it becomes the mouth and the anus forms later; in deuterostomes ("second mouth") it becomes the anus and the mouth breaks through later.
Primitive streak
The amniote equivalent of the blastopore: a groove along the midline of the epiblast through which cells ingress. Its anterior end is the primitive node (Hensen's node in the chick), which is the amniote organiser.
Neural crest
A population that delaminates from the edges of the neural plate as the neural tube closes and migrates throughout the body. Ectodermal in origin but mesenchymal in behaviour — often called the "fourth germ layer", and a vertebrate innovation.

Three versions of the same event

Sea urchin — the textbook diagram. Primary mesenchyme cells ingress from the vegetal plate into the blastocoel; the vegetal plate then invaginates, and the archenteron elongates by convergent extension, guided by filopodia from secondary mesenchyme that pull it across to the animal pole. Where it touches, the mouth breaks through. The blastopore, at the vegetal end, becomes the anus.

Frog — the same event constrained by yolk. Bottle cells at the future dorsal side create the dorsal lip of the blastopore; surface cells roll over the lip and inwards (involution), while the animal cap spreads down over the embryo (epiboly). The archenteron enlarges as the blastocoel is obliterated. The last yolky cells to be covered protrude as the yolk plug. The dorsal lip is the organiser — see §10.

Chick and human — the same event constrained by a flat disc. There is no ball to invaginate, so cells of the upper layer (epiblast) migrate medially, ingress through the primitive streak, and spread out beneath. The first cells through displace the hypoblast and become endoderm; later cells spread between the two layers and become mesoderm; whatever epiblast is left on top is ectoderm. Two consequences are regularly examined: all three germ layers come from the epiblast, and the human embryo is a flat bilaminar then trilaminar disc, not a ball, at this stage.

The blastopore rule is real but leaky, and the etymology is doing work

Proto-stoma = first mouth, deutero-stoma = second mouth: in a protostome the blastopore becomes the mouth. That is the answer to give. But be aware of two things. First, molecular phylogenetics — not embryology — is what actually defines Protostomia and Deuterostomia today, and the blastopore fate is a clade-typical character with real exceptions in both groups. Second, a distractor often pairs the rule correctly and then bundles a wrong companion fact: "deuterostomes show radial, indeterminate cleavage, schizocoelous coelom formation and a blastopore that becomes the anus" — three right and one wrong, since deuterostomes are classically enterocoelous. Reading option lists to the end is worth marks here.

The derivative table — the single most reliably examined thing on this page

Learn this by logic rather than by list. Ectoderm is what stays on the outside plus everything that signals; mesoderm is everything structural in the middle; endoderm is the lining of the tube that runs through the animal and the glands that bud off it. Where a structure looks like an exception, it is nearly always because an epithelium and its supporting wall come from different layers, or because neural crest has migrated somewhere unexpected.

Exam technique · assigning any structure to a germ layer

  1. Split the structure before you answer — epithelium, wall, connective tissue. "The small intestine", "the bladder", "the adrenal", "the eye" are all multi-layer objects, and the correct answer depends entirely on which part the stem names. If the stem says lining or epithelium of a gut-derived tube, it wants endoderm; if it says wall, muscle or stroma, it wants mesoderm.
  2. Run the neural-crest checklist next — adrenal medulla, all ganglia and Schwann cells, melanocytes, craniofacial bone and cartilage, dentine and odontoblasts, the aorticopulmonary septum, thyroid C cells, the enteric neurons. Anything on it is ectoderm, and that single check resolves most "counterintuitive" items on this topic.
  3. Pouch or cleft? Endoderm or ectoderm respectively. Thymus and parathyroids are pouches, therefore endoderm, however lymphoid or endocrine they look.
  4. On a NOT / EXCEPT stem, prove the other three — you are not asked to justify the odd one out, only to confirm the rest belong. That is usually faster and much less error-prone, and it is the format this topic is most often set in.
  5. Distrust an option that reads like a textbook sentence — "the gut is derived from endoderm" is a real sentence from a real book and is still the wrong answer to a stem about the muscularis. Match the option to the structure named in the stem, not to a remembered phrase.
Go deeper · neural crest, the "fourth germ layer", and why its failures cluster

Neural crest cells arise at the border between neural plate and epidermis, undergo an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition as the neural folds fuse, and migrate along stereotyped routes. They are ectodermal by origin but produce tissues that in any other context would be called mesodermal — bone, cartilage, smooth muscle — which is why they are called a fourth germ layer and why they are a favourite trap. Their derivatives sort by axial level:

  • Cranial crest — most of the bones and cartilage of the face and skull vault, dentine and odontoblasts, corneal stroma and endothelium, cranial ganglia.
  • Cardiac crest (a subset of cranial) — the aorticopulmonary septum that divides the outflow tract into aorta and pulmonary trunk.
  • Vagal and sacral crest — the entire enteric nervous system, colonising the gut from rostral to caudal.
  • Trunk crest — dorsal root ganglia, sympathetic chain, adrenal medulla, Schwann cells, and (by a dorsolateral route) melanocytes.

Because one migratory population produces all of these, a single defect produces a bizarre-looking combination of features — a neurocristopathy — and that combination is the diagnostic clue:

  • Hirschsprung disease: vagal crest fails to reach the distal colon → aganglionic segment, no peristalsis, functional obstruction, and the aganglionic part is the narrow one.
  • DiGeorge / 22q11.2 deletion: cardiac crest and pharyngeal pouch failure together → conotruncal heart defects, absent thymus and parathyroids, facial anomalies.
  • Waardenburg syndrome: melanocyte and inner-ear crest → white forelock, heterochromia, congenital deafness — and, in some subtypes, Hirschsprung as well, which is only sensible once you know both tissues are crest.
  • Treacher Collins: cranial crest deficiency → mandibular and zygomatic hypoplasia.
  • Neuroblastoma and phaeochromocytoma: tumours of trunk crest derivatives; melanoma is a tumour of a crest derivative too.

One curiosity worth carrying because it makes the layers concrete: the tympanic membrane is the only place in the body where all three germ layers meet in a single sheet — ectoderm from the first pharyngeal cleft outside, endoderm from the first pouch inside, and a thin mesodermal layer between.

Worked example 4 · An exam-style five-option item on germ layers

Written in the format of the paper: single best answer, five options, negative marking

A teratoma is a germ-cell tumour containing tissues from more than one germ layer. A sacrococcygeal teratoma is found to contain hair follicles, cartilage, respiratory epithelium, and clusters of catecholamine-secreting chromaffin cells. Which of the following correctly assigns the embryonic origin of all four?

  1. (A) ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm, mesoderm
  2. (B) ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm, ectoderm (neural crest)
  3. (C) ectoderm, ectoderm, endoderm, mesoderm
  4. (D) mesoderm, mesoderm, endoderm, ectoderm (neural crest)
  5. (E) ectoderm, mesoderm, mesoderm, endoderm

Reasoning. Hair follicles are epidermal appendages → ectoderm. Cartilage is connective tissue → mesoderm (with the specific exception of craniofacial cartilage, which is neural crest — not relevant in the sacrum). Respiratory epithelium is a bud of the foregut → endoderm. Chromaffin cells are the cell type of the adrenal medulla, i.e. modified sympathetic neurons → neural crest, therefore ectoderm. That is (B).

Each distractor is a real error: (A) puts the medulla with the cortex in mesoderm; (C) makes cartilage ectodermal by over-generalising the neural-crest exception; (D) assigns hair to mesoderm by confusing the follicle with the dermal papilla that surrounds it; (E) makes lung mesodermal by thinking of the organ rather than the epithelium.

Answer (B) · ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm, ectoderm

Note the negative-marking logic. Even knowing only that respiratory epithelium is endoderm eliminates (E); knowing only that the adrenal medulla is crest eliminates (A) and (C). With five options, eliminating two takes a blind 1-in-5 guess to 1-in-3, and the expected value of answering becomes clearly positive.

§ 08

Neurulation, the notochord and organogenesis

The ideaOnce the three layers are in place, the embryo has to turn sheets into organs. It does this in the simplest way a sheet can: by rolling. A strip of ectoderm along the back rolls up into a tube and sinks below the surface, and that tube becomes the brain and spinal cord. Nothing instructs the strip from far away — a rod of mesoderm lying directly underneath tells the cells right above it what to be. That is the whole principle of organogenesis: a signal from a neighbour, a sheet that folds, a tube that pinches off.

Definition & key facts

Neurulation
Formation of the neural tube. The notochord induces the overlying ectoderm to thicken into a neural plate; the plate's edges rise as neural folds flanking a neural groove; the folds meet and fuse into a neural tube that sinks beneath the surface ectoderm, releasing the neural crest as it does so.

How a flat sheet rolls into a tube

The mechanics matter because they explain the failures. Cells in the neural plate constrict their apical ends using an actomyosin ring — a wedge-shaped cell forces the sheet to curve. Constriction is concentrated at hinge points: a median hinge point directly over the notochord, and paired dorsolateral hinge points. At the same time the sheet narrows and lengthens by convergent extension, cells intercalating between one another in the midline. Surface ectoderm on either side then pushes the folds together and the two epithelia fuse — neural to neural, surface to surface — so the tube ends up covered by continuous skin. Neural crest cells are released at exactly this junction.

Two lists in this section look like memorisation and are not, so take the warning explicitly: there is no mnemonic for either and you do not need one. The mesodermal columns are named in the order they occupy — axial is on the axis, paraxial is beside it, intermediate lies between paraxial and lateral, and lateral plate is at the side — so the four names are the mediolateral order, and a hook would only be a second thing to keep. The somite's three parts are the same trick in Greek: sclerotome, hard, giving vertebrae and ribs; myotome, muscle; dermatome, skin. If you find yourself reaching for an acronym here, the words have already told you the answer.

Neural tube defects, and why the advice is preconception folate

Failure of the anterior neuropore to close by day 25 gives anencephaly — the forebrain does not develop and the vault of the skull is absent; it is incompatible with life. Failure of the posterior neuropore by day 27 gives spina bifida, ranging from occulta (a vertebral arch defect only) through meningocele to myelomeningocele. Both are detectable by a raised maternal serum α-fetoprotein and on ultrasound. Now the timing: the neural tube is closed by day 28 post-fertilisation, which is about day 42 from the last menstrual period — roughly two weeks after a missed period, and often before a pregnancy is recognised at all. Starting folate when pregnancy is confirmed is therefore too late, and this is precisely why the recommendation is 400 µg per day before conception and through the first trimester, rising to 4 mg per day (ten times the dose) for a woman who has already had an affected pregnancy. The same logic explains why anti-epileptic drugs that interfere with folate — valproate, carbamazepine — carry neural tube defect risk.

Which neuropore, which day, which defect

Anterior → Anencephaly; 25 before 27

Both words start with A, so the anterior neuropore is the one whose failure gives anencephaly; the posterior neuropore is then the only one left, and its failure gives spina bifida. The days run in the same direction as the alphabet — anterior closes on day 25, posterior on day 27 — and the tube is shut by day 28 post-fertilisation, which is about day 42 from the last menstrual period: roughly a fortnight after a missed period, and often before the pregnancy is known at all. That last line is the whole argument for preconception folate. The A-for-anencephaly link is the version in circulation; pairing it with 25-before-27 is mine. Neither half carries the doses, which are their own small pair of fours — 400 µg a day before conception, 4 mg a day after an affected pregnancy.

Organogenesis in outline, and the two things worth memorising about it

Beyond the neural tube, organ formation reduces to a small number of repeated manoeuvres: an epithelial sheet folds (neural tube, gut tube), a bud evaginates and branches (lung, kidney, salivary and mammary glands, liver), a solid cord cavitates (much of the vasculature, the caudal neural tube), a block of mesenchyme condenses and differentiates (bone, cartilage), and unwanted tissue is removed by apoptosis (the interdigital webs, one of the two duct systems). The embryo also folds in two planes at once during week 4 — laterally, which closes the ventral body wall, and cephalocaudally, which pulls the heart and the septum transversum from in front of the head down to the chest, and which converts a flat disc into a recognisable C-shaped embryo with a gut tube inside it.

The two examinable generalities: (i) the embryonic period, weeks 3–8, is when organ primordia form and therefore when structural malformations are generated — the teratogen window; (ii) each organ has its own narrow window inside that period, so the same exposure produces a different defect depending on the week. Thalidomide taken on days 20–36 after conception produces limb reduction (phocomelia); the identical dose a fortnight later produces nothing, because the limb bud has already formed.

Extra-embryonic membranes, the placenta, birth and twinning

The ideaLeaving the water meant carrying the water with you. The amniote egg's four membranes are a private pond, a private lung, a private toilet and a private lunchbox — and mammals kept all four while changing what three of them are for. The placenta is the strangest organ in the body because it is built by the fetus, invades the mother, and secretes hormones that override her own cycle; and because the two bloodstreams it serves come within a few micrometres of each other and never mix.

"Maternal and fetal blood mix in the placenta" — the single most common false statement on this topic

They do not. Fetal capillaries run inside chorionic villi; maternal blood, delivered by spiral arteries, bathes the outside of those villi in the intervillous space. Everything crosses a barrier — O₂, CO₂ and lipophilic drugs by simple diffusion; glucose by facilitated diffusion (GLUT1); amino acids, iron and some vitamins by active transport; maternal IgG alone among the antibody classes by receptor-mediated transcytosis (FcRn). Three consequences worth having ready. (i) Because IgG but not IgM crosses, a positive fetal IgM implies fetal infection while a positive IgG may simply be the mother's. (ii) Because the blood does not mix, Rh sensitisation normally requires a feto-maternal haemorrhage — most often at delivery — which is why anti-D is given at 28 weeks and again postpartum, and why the first Rh-incompatible pregnancy is usually unaffected. (iii) Because everything crosses the same barrier, "the placental barrier protects the fetus from drugs" is also false: it is a selective exchange surface, not a shield.

Why the fetus wins the tug-of-war for oxygen

Maternal blood arrives at the villus already partly desaturated. For O₂ to move into the fetus, fetal haemoglobin must bind it at a lower partial pressure than maternal haemoglobin releases it. Fetal haemoglobin is α₂γ₂ rather than α₂β₂, and the γ chain lacks the cationic residues that bind 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate well. With less BPG bound, the equilibrium favours the high-affinity R state, so the HbF dissociation curve sits to the left of the maternal curve — the P50 is lower. Note the mechanism carefully, because it is a classic distractor: the difference is not that γ chains intrinsically "grip oxygen harder" in isolation, it is that they respond less to the allosteric inhibitor present in the red cell. The exchange is helped further by the double Bohr effect — CO₂ moving from fetus to mother acidifies maternal blood (making it release O₂) and alkalinises fetal blood (making it take O₂ up), in the same place at the same moment.

Placental hormones, and what each is for

hCG (syncytiotrophoblast, from ≈day 8) — an LH-receptor agonist that rescues the corpus luteum. Its α subunit is identical to that of LH, FSH and TSH, which is why very high hCG (twins, molar pregnancy) can produce biochemical hyperthyroidism, and why it is the target of every pregnancy test. Progesterone — from the corpus luteum, then the placenta; maintains the decidua and keeps the myometrium quiet. Oestrogens — made by a fetal–placental partnership, since the placenta lacks the enzyme (CYP17A1) to make androgen precursors and the fetal adrenal supplies them; a falling oestriol therefore reports on fetal wellbeing. Human placental lactogen — a growth-hormone-like protein that induces maternal insulin resistance so that glucose is spared for the fetus, which is the physiological basis of gestational diabetes. Relaxin — softens the pubic symphysis and cervix.

Exam technique · placenta, hCG and cord items

  1. Turn the elapsed time into doublings before touching the numbers — divide the hours by 48, then raise 2 to that power. 48 h → ×2, 96 h → ×4, 144 h (six days) → ×8. The two distractors that are always present are the per-day multiplier (240 → 1440) and a 24-hour doubling time (240 → 15 360), so if your answer matches either shape, recheck the exponent rather than the multiplication.
  2. If the stem gives only one hCG value, the answer is not arithmetic — a single value is nearly uninformative and the item must be about something else. The examinable content is the slope (a rise of less than about 50% in 48 h is the abnormal one) and the discriminatory zone of roughly 1500–3500 mIU·mL⁻¹, above which an intrauterine sac should be visible on transvaginal ultrasound.
  3. Kill any option that lets the two bloodstreams meet — maternal and fetal blood never mix, however the option dresses it up. The same rule settles the Rh items: sensitisation needs a feto-maternal haemorrhage, which is exactly why the first incompatible pregnancy is usually unaffected.
  4. Define every vessel by direction, never by oxygen content — an artery carries blood away from the heart. Two umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood to the placenta; the one umbilical vein returns oxygenated blood to the fetus. Any option reasoning from "arteries are oxygenated" fails here, and fails on the pulmonary vessels for exactly the same reason — in both cases the artery is heading towards the gas-exchange surface, so of course it is the deoxygenated one.
  5. For antibody-transfer items, reason from size — IgG alone crosses, by FcRn transcytosis; IgM is a pentamer and stays behind. So fetal IgM implies fetal infection, whereas fetal IgG may simply be the mother's. Nothing here needs memorising beyond "the big one does not cross".

Birth: the standard example of positive feedback

Through pregnancy, progesterone dominance keeps the myometrium quiescent and its cells electrically uncoupled. Towards term the oestrogen-to-progesterone ratio rises, which upregulates oxytocin receptors, gap junctions (connexin-43) and prostaglandin synthesis, so the uterus becomes both sensitive and electrically coordinated. Then the loop closes: the fetal head presses on the cervix; stretch receptors signal to the hypothalamus; oxytocin is released from the posterior pituitary; oxytocin and prostaglandins strengthen contractions; stronger contractions push the head harder onto the cervix. Each step increases the stimulus for the next, so the cycle escalates until delivery removes the stimulus altogether. Two exam points: oxytocin is synthesised in the hypothalamic paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei and merely released from the posterior pituitary, and this is one of the very few genuine positive-feedback loops in physiology — the others being the LH surge (§04), blood clotting and the Na⁺ upstroke of the action potential.

After delivery, expulsion of the placenta removes the progesterone that had been blocking prolactin's action on the breast, and milk production begins. Keep the two lactation hormones apart: prolactin (anterior pituitary) drives milk synthesis; oxytocin (posterior pituitary) drives milk ejection — the let-down reflex, itself another positive-feedback loop driven by suckling.

Twinning, and why the membranes tell you the timing

Dizygotic twins come from two oocytes fertilised by two sperm. They are no more alike than any two siblings (r = 0.5), can be of different sexes, and are always dichorionic and diamniotic because each has its own conceptus. Their frequency varies enormously with maternal age, parity, family history on the mother's side, and population — roughly 6–20 per 1000 births in most of Europe and much higher in parts of West Africa, but only about 2–7 per 1000 in East Asian populations. That variation is essentially variation in FSH, which is why fertility treatment raises the dizygotic rate and not the monozygotic one.

Monozygotic twins come from one conceptus that splits. Their frequency is about 3–4 per 1000 births almost everywhere, largely independent of age and ancestry — which is itself evidence that the event is a developmental accident rather than a heritable tendency. "Identical twins are genetically identical" — nearly true, and the exceptions are informative

Monozygotic twins start with the same genome and then diverge. Somatic mutations accumulate independently from the moment of the split — the largest whole-genome study of MZ pairs finds on average only about five early post-zygotic differences, though a minority of pairs differ by many more. In females, X-inactivation is independent in each twin, so an XX pair can be discordant for an X-linked condition: both members of an MZ pair may carry a Duchenne muscular dystrophy allele and only one be a manifesting carrier, because the two happened to inactivate different X chromosomes in muscle. Lymphocytes in each twin perform their own V(D)J recombination, so their antibody and T-cell receptor repertoires differ. Epigenetic marks drift with age and environment. And fingerprints differ, because ridge patterns depend on the mechanical microenvironment in the womb, not on sequence. Note the direction of the argument: MZ twins are the natural experiment that separates genotype from environment precisely because they are almost identical — the residual differences are what make twin studies quantitative rather than merely suggestive. A twin concordance below 100% for a disease is evidence of a non-genetic contribution, which is how the heritability of schizophrenia (≈50% concordance) and of type 1 diabetes (≈30–50%) was established.

Worked example 5 · Reading an hCG series

A serum hCG is 240 mIU·mL⁻¹. What should it be six days later in a normally progressing intrauterine pregnancy, and what would 1 400 mIU·mL⁻¹ at 96 h tell you?

  1. Set up the model. Early hCG rises exponentially with a doubling time of about 48 h, so [hCG]t = 240 × 2^(t/48 h).
  2. Six days. 6 d = 144 h = 3 doubling times, so the concentration should be 240 × 2³ = 240 × 8 = 1920 mIU·mL⁻¹.
  3. Check the tempting wrong routes. Multiplying by 6 (once per day) gives 1440; multiplying by 2 per 24 h gives 240 × 2⁶ = 15 360, far too fast. The doubling time is the whole content of the question.
  4. The 96-hour figure. 96 h is two doublings, so the expected value is 240 × 4 = 960. An observed 1400 is above expectation — the rise is faster than the minimum, which is reassuring, and if anything raises the question of a multiple pregnancy. It is a value below about 960, and particularly a plateau or a rise of less than about 50% in 48 h, that suggests an ectopic pregnancy or a failing intrauterine one.
  5. Interpretation matters more than the arithmetic. A single hCG value is nearly uninformative; the slope is the measurement. Above the "discriminatory zone" of roughly 1500–3500 mIU·mL⁻¹, an intrauterine gestational sac should be visible on transvaginal ultrasound, and its absence at that level is what makes an ectopic likely.

Determinants, induction and morphogen gradients

The ideaEvery cell in the embryo has the same DNA, so nothing in the genome can tell a cell what to become. The information has to come from outside the nucleus, and there are only two possible sources: something the cell inherited from a particular corner of the egg's cytoplasm, or something a neighbour told it. That is the whole of developmental biology in one sentence — asymmetric inheritance and cell–cell signalling. A gradient is simply the second one made quantitative: if a signal is strong here and weak there, then "how much signal am I receiving" is a usable measurement of "where am I".

Definition & key facts

A substance that spreads from a localised source and specifies two or more distinct fates at different concentration thresholds. The threshold requirement is what distinguishes a morphogen from an ordinary signal: a molecule that simply switches one response on or off is not a morphogen.

Mechanism one: something you inherited

The frog egg is polarised before fertilisation — pigmented animal hemisphere, yolky vegetal hemisphere. Sperm entry then triggers a cortical rotation of about 30°, sliding the egg's outer cortex relative to its inner cytoplasm and carrying dorsal determinants (Dishevelled protein, wnt11 mRNA) from the vegetal pole towards the side opposite the point of sperm entry. A crescent of intermediate pigmentation, the grey crescent, appears there, and it marks the future dorsal side.

Spemann tested this in the 1920s with a loop of his infant son's hair. Tie a fertilised egg so that the grey crescent is divided between the two halves and you get two normal tadpoles. Tie it so that all the grey crescent goes to one side and you get one normal tadpole and one shapeless mass of undifferentiated belly tissue — the Bauchstück. The nucleus is the same in both halves; only the cytoplasm differs. That is the cleanest demonstration ever made that positional information can live in the cytoplasm, and it is the experiment most likely to be described in an exam stem without being named.

Mechanism two: something a neighbour told you

In experiments run in 1921–22 and published in 1924, Hilde Mangold transplanted the dorsal lip of the blastopore from one newt gastrula to the ventral side of another. The host developed a second, complete body axis — a conjoined twin embryo with its own neural tube, somites and gut. Mangold used donor and host species with different pigmentation, so she could see which cells were which, and the result is the entire point: the secondary embryo was built almost entirely from host cells. The graft contributed little more than the notochord. It had not become a second embryo; it had persuaded host tissue that would otherwise have made belly to make a back instead. Spemann named it the organiser and received the 1935 Nobel Prize; Mangold had died in a domestic accident in September 1924, aged 25, before the paper's significance was recognised.

What the organiser actually does is the reverse of what it sounds like

The intuitive reading — "the organiser sends a signal that instructs ectoderm to become neural tissue" — is backwards, and knowing why is worth a mark. Isolated ectoderm, cultured as dissociated single cells with no signals at all, becomes neural. Neural is the default fate. What keeps ectoderm epidermal in the intact embryo is BMP4, an active signal present throughout. The organiser secretes antagonists — noggin, chordin and follistatin — which bind BMP4 in the extracellular space and stop it reaching its receptor. Neural induction is therefore inhibition of an inhibitor: the organiser does not instruct, it releases a brake. This "default model" is a favourite because the plausible-sounding version and the correct version differ by exactly one negation.

The default model, pointed in the direction that gets asked

BMP builds skin; block BMP and you get brain

Ectoderm left entirely alone becomes neural — neural is the default. BMP4 is the active signal that overrides the default and makes epidermis. The organiser secretes noggin, chordin and follistatin, which bind BMP4 in the extracellular space so it never reaches its receptor. Neural induction is therefore the removal of an inhibitor, not the delivery of an instruction, and an option saying the organiser "instructs ectoderm to become neural" differs from the truth by exactly one negation. Mine. It fixes the direction, which is where the marks are, and it deliberately does not separate the three antagonists — where a stem names one of them it is using the name as a label for "BMP antagonist", and the mechanism is the same for all three.

Mechanism three: making the signal quantitative

Lewis Wolpert's French flag model is the standard picture. A field of identical cells sits next to a source of a diffusible substance. The concentration falls with distance. Cells read the local concentration and compare it against two internal thresholds: above the higher one, become blue; between them, white; below the lower one, red. One graded signal has produced three sharply bounded domains. Nothing about the cells differs; only their position, converted into a concentration, converted into a fate.

The mathematics is worth having because it makes a prediction. At steady state, a substance made at a source, diffusing with coefficient D and degraded at first-order rate k, settles into an exponential profile C(x) = C₀e−x/λ with decay length λ = √(D/k). Two consequences follow directly. First, because the profile is exponential, the threshold positions are spaced logarithmically, so doubling the amount of morphogen at the source moves every boundary outward by only λ ln 2 ≈ 0.69λ — the pattern is far more robust to variation in source strength than a linear gradient would be. Second, the range of a gradient is set by the ratio of diffusion to destruction, not by either alone: a fast-diffusing but rapidly destroyed molecule acts locally, and this is precisely how cells tune the reach of a signal.

Three distinctions examiners build items around

Morphogen vs inducer. Every morphogen is an inducer; not every inducer is a morphogen. If the substance produces only one response, above threshold or not at all, it is an inducer. Regulative vs mosaic. A regulative embryo replaces lost parts because fates are assigned by position and position can be re-read; a mosaic embryo cannot, because fates were assigned by inherited determinants. Mammals are strongly regulative early on — which is why splitting an 8-cell embryo gives two complete individuals and why preimplantation genetic testing can remove a cell without harm. Totipotent vs pluripotent. Embryonic stem cells derived from the inner cell mass are pluripotent: they make all three germ layers but not a placenta, so they cannot on their own make an organism. An option calling ES cells totipotent is wrong, and it is a very common one.

Exam technique · the four calculations this topic actually sets

  1. Units before algebra — µm²·s⁻¹ ÷ s⁻¹ leaves µm², a squared length, so the decay length must be a square root. That check alone kills the 10 000 µm option in a morphogen item without any biology, and it generalises: whenever a constant is a rate and a coefficient, form the quotient and look at what dimension comes out.
  2. Lengths divide by the cube root — cleavage divides volume by the cell number, so diameters divide by N1/3. Memorise the one pair you need: 2¹² = 4096 and 40961/3 = 16 exactly. A distractor that divides the diameter by the cell count always lands on something smaller than a bacterium.
  3. Convert time into doublings first — for hCG, divide the elapsed hours by 48, then raise 2 to that power. The two standard traps are multiplying by the number of days (240 → 1440) and halving the doubling time (240 → 15 360); both appear as options because both are things candidates actually do.
  4. Sanity-check against a known length scale — a cell is ≈10 µm, an embryonic field 10²–10³ µm, a human oocyte ≈120 µm, a frog egg ≈1200 µm. An answer outside that range is a method error, not a rounding error, and should be discarded rather than adjusted.
  5. Suspect the answer that came too easily — calculational distractors on this paper are built from named wrong methods, not from noise. If your number fell out of a single obvious operation, spend ten of your sixty-six seconds asking which wrong method would have produced exactly that.
Go deeper · the Drosophila segmentation hierarchy, and why the fly was the right animal

The fly's early embryo is a syncytium: after fertilisation, 13 rounds of nuclear division occur with no cytokinesis, and although 2¹³ is 8192, the pole cells drop out early and a few hundred nuclei stay behind in the yolk, so about 6000 nuclei are actually enclosed when membranes are finally built around them. That single fact is why Drosophila gave up the logic of pattern formation before any other animal — a transcription factor released at one end can simply diffuse to every nucleus, so gradients act directly on the genome without receptors, transduction or any intervening machinery. In 1980 Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard and Eric Wieschaus ran a saturation mutagenesis screen for every gene whose loss disturbed the larval segment pattern, and found that the mutants fell into a small number of classes that turned out to be a temporal hierarchy. They shared the 1995 Nobel Prize with Edward Lewis.

  1. Maternal-effect genes — transcribed in the mother, deposited in the egg. bicoid mRNA is tethered anteriorly, nanos posteriorly. The protein gradients that form define the axis. The diagnostic feature is that the phenotype depends on the mother's genotype, not the embryo's: a homozygous mutant female produces defective embryos whatever their own genotype.
  2. Gap genes — hunchback, Krüppel, knirps. Read the maternal gradients at different thresholds and divide the embryo into broad regions. Mutants lack a contiguous block of segments, hence "gap".
  3. Pair-rule genes — even-skipped, fushi tarazu, hairy. Expressed in seven stripes each, generated by modular enhancers that each read a different combination of gap-protein concentrations. Mutants lose alternate segments. The even-skipped stripe-2 enhancer, which integrates Bicoid, Hunchback, Giant and Krüppel to make one stripe, is the best-understood piece of gene regulation in biology.
  4. Segment-polarity genes — engrailed, wingless, hedgehog. Fourteen stripes; establish anterior and posterior compartments within each segment and maintain the boundaries by a reciprocal signalling loop that runs for the rest of development.
  5. Homeotic selector (Hox) genes — assign each segment its identity. See §11.

The shape of the hierarchy is the transferable lesson: a continuous maternal gradient is converted into broad domains, then into a periodic pattern, then into sharp cellular boundaries, and only then is identity assigned. Each step increases spatial precision, and none of it requires any cell to measure a long distance directly.

Worked example 6 · Where does the French flag put its stripes?

A morphogen is produced at the left edge of a tissue, diffuses with D = 10 µm²·s⁻¹ and is degraded with rate constant k = 1.0 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. Cells adopt fate A above 0.5C₀, fate B between 0.5C₀ and 0.1C₀, and fate C below 0.1C₀. Where are the two boundaries, and how wide is the middle stripe?

  1. Decay length. λ = √(D/k) = √(10 / 1.0 × 10⁻³) = √(1.0 × 10⁴) = 100 µm. Check the units: µm²·s⁻¹ divided by s⁻¹ gives µm², whose square root is µm. Good.
  2. Boundary formula. Set C₀e−x/λ = fC₀, so e−x/λ = f and x = λ ln(1/f).
  3. First boundary, f = 0.5. x = 100 × ln 2 = 100 × 0.693 = 69 µm.
  4. Second boundary, f = 0.1. x = 100 × ln 10 = 100 × 2.303 = 230 µm.
  5. Width of the middle stripe. 230 − 69 = 161 µm.
  6. Sanity check on the biology. The whole pattern spans a few hundred micrometres — about twenty cell diameters — which is the observed range of real morphogen gradients. Gradients do not pattern whole organisms; they pattern fields, and large structures are built by chaining fields together.

λ = 100 µm · boundaries at 69 µm and 230 µm · middle stripe 161 µm wide

Two instructive traps. Forgetting the square root gives λ = D/k = 10 000 µm, a centimetre — implausible for an embryo and a good reason to check units before trusting a number. And quoting λ itself (100 µm) as "the boundary" confuses the 1/e point (≈37% of C₀) with a threshold you were actually given.

§ 11

Hox genes, apoptosis, model organisms and teratogens

The ideaTwo loose ends finish the story. Once a cell knows roughly where it is, something has to record that answer permanently and tell the whole region what kind of structure to build — a leg here, an antenna there — and that is what Hox genes do; they are address labels, not blueprints. And building by adding is only half of development: a great deal of shaping is done by removing cells that were deliberately made and are then deliberately killed, quietly and without inflammation. Both ideas were discovered in small animals chosen because they are convenient, which is why the last part of this section is about the animals themselves.

What a homeotic mutation actually shows you

Two Drosophila mutants defined the field. In Antennapedia, a gain-of-function allele expresses a gene in the head segment where it does not belong, and the fly grows perfectly formed legs where its antennae should be. In bithorax, loss of Ultrabithorax function transforms the third thoracic segment towards the identity of the second, so the halteres become a second pair of wings and the fly has four. Notice what is not happening in either case. No structure is deleted. No structure is malformed. A leg is built correctly; it is simply built in the wrong place. That is the signature of a gene that specifies identity rather than construction — the segment already knew how to build appendages, and the Hox gene only told it which kind.

Which end of the cluster is which end of the animal

Hox 1 = head, Hox 13 = tail

The paralogue number is the position, so one picture carries both colinearities. Group 1 sits at the 3′ end of the cluster, is expressed anteriorly, and switches on first; group 13 sits at the 5′ end, is expressed posteriorly, and switches on last. "3′ = anterior = early" and "5′ = posterior = late" are then recovered rather than memorised as two separate rules. The human bookkeeping hangs on the same frame: 39 genes, 4 clusters, 13 paralogue groups. Standard teaching, compressed — not an acronym anyone circulates. Note what it does not claim: a Hox gene never tells a cell which type to become, only which level it is at, which is why a Hox mutant grows a wrong-shaped vertebra instead of losing one.

Homeobox is not homeotic, and Hox genes do not choose cell types

Two errors, both common enough to be reliable distractors. First, the homeobox is a 180-base-pair DNA sequence encoding a DNA-binding homeodomain, and it occurs in hundreds of genes that have nothing to do with homeosis — Pax6, Pit-1, engrailed, the bicoid gene itself. A homeotic gene is one whose mutation converts one body part into another. Most homeotic genes contain a homeobox; most homeobox genes are not homeotic. Second, Hox genes do not instruct a cell to become a neuron or a muscle fibre. They tell a whole region where it is, and the region's cells then build the appropriate version of whatever they were already going to build. A mouse missing a Hox gene does not lose its vertebrae; it grows a vertebra of the wrong shape at that level — a lumbar vertebra where a thoracic one belongs, complete with the presence or absence of a rib. In humans, HOXD13 mutations cause synpolydactyly and HOXA13 mutations the hand–foot–genital syndrome: extra or fused digits and misshapen structures, never missing tissue types.

Apoptosis: sculpting by subtraction

A surprising amount of anatomy is produced by killing cells that were made on purpose. The classic examples are worth having in a list because they recur: the interdigital webs between the digital rays of a seven-week embryo are removed by BMP-driven apoptosis, and failure gives syndactyly; one entire duct system — Müllerian in the male, Wolffian in the female — is deleted; the tadpole's tail is resorbed under thyroid hormone; the lumen of many tubes is opened by killing the cells in the middle of a solid cord; and roughly half of all neurons initially generated die, because they are produced in excess and then compete for a limited supply of target-derived survival factor such as nerve growth factor. That last one is a beautiful piece of engineering: rather than specifying in advance how many motor neurons a muscle needs, the embryo overproduces and lets the target set the number.

The genetics came from C. elegans, where every one of the 1090 somatic cells generated in the hermaphrodite has a known ancestry and exactly 131 of them die at reproducible times, leaving 959. Because the lineage is invariant, a mutant in which a specific cell failed to die could be spotted under a microscope. ced-3 turned out to encode a caspase, ced-4 the homologue of Apaf-1, ced-9 the homologue of Bcl-2 (an inhibitor), and egl-1 a BH3-only activator — the same four-part module that operates in the human intrinsic pathway. Sydney Brenner, Robert Horvitz and John Sulston shared the 2002 Nobel Prize for it.

Every discovery in this note was made in an animal picked for a specific technical convenience, and questions often work backwards from the convenience to the organism. Gurdon's experiment, and what it does and does not prove

A nucleus taken from a differentiated intestinal epithelial cell of a Xenopus tadpole, transplanted into an enucleated egg, can support development to a fertile adult. The conclusion is genomic equivalence: differentiation does not delete or permanently alter the genome, so the information for every cell type is still present in every nucleus. What it does not show is that differentiation is easily reversible — the success rate was very low, and it took another 40 years and Yamanaka's four transcription factors to reprogramme a somatic cell without an egg at all. The two shared the 2012 Nobel Prize. If a stem describes cloning by nuclear transfer, the examinable inference is about the genome's completeness, not about the ease of the procedure.

Teratogens in outline

Three principles carry most of the marks. Timing: before implantation (roughly the first two weeks) the effect is all-or-none, because the embryo is regulative enough either to replace lost cells completely or to die; weeks 3–8 is the organogenesis window when structural malformations are produced; after week 8 exposure causes functional and growth problems rather than gross malformation, with the central nervous system remaining vulnerable throughout. Dose: nearly every teratogen has a threshold. Specificity: each agent hits the organs that happen to be forming when it is present, which is why the same drug produces one defect on day 25 and none on day 50.

Exam technique · dating a teratogen stem

  1. Extract the date before you even look at the agent — and check which clock the stem is running. Days post-fertilisation and weeks from the last menstrual period differ by about 14 days, so "six weeks pregnant" almost always means LMP, that is about four weeks post-conception, which is the middle of organogenesis.
  2. Then drop the date into one of three windows — first two weeks: all-or-none, so an option offering a specific malformation from a day-10 exposure is wrong on principle; weeks 3–8: structural malformation, and the defect is whichever organ was under construction; after week 8: growth and function rather than gross structure, with the CNS vulnerable throughout.
  3. Expect the deliberate counter-examples — ACE inhibitors and ARBs act in the second and third trimesters on a kidney that already works, and tetracyclines and aminoglycosides are late effects too. When a stem hands you a late exposure, "weeks 3–8" is the trap answer rather than the safe one.
  4. Match the defect to a mechanism you already hold — retinoic acid disrupts neural crest, hence craniofacial and cardiac-outflow defects; folate antagonists give neural tube defects acting in weeks 3–4; thalidomide has a window of days 20–36 and gives limb reduction. If the stem gives a day and asks for the defect, it is asking which structure was being built that day.
  5. Where the time goes — the ladder calls this table lookup material, and it is right. Learn the six agents with an unmistakable signature — alcohol, thalidomide, isotretinoin, valproate, warfarin, rubella — and answer those in fifteen seconds. Do not spend sixty reconstructing a row you never learned: the elimination you can do from timing alone is usually worth more than the row would have been.

One sentence to leave with

Development answers one question over and over — where am I? — using only two kinds of answer: something you inherited from a particular part of the egg, and something a neighbour told you. Gradients make the second answer quantitative, Hox genes write it down permanently, induction chains it into cascades, and apoptosis erases the parts of the draft that were never meant to be kept.
