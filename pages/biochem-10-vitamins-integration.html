Vitamins, Cofactors and Metabolic Integration

Two questions organise everything here. First: what chemistry can a protein not do with the twenty side chains it has? Every vitamin on this page is an answer to that question — a reagent the cell must buy in because it cannot build one out of amino acids, and each one therefore maps onto exactly one kind of reaction. Second: who is eating what, right now? Metabolism is not a set of pathways, it is a negotiation between five organs with different enzymes, arbitrated by two hormones and one nucleotide ratio. Get those two frames and the deficiency syndromes, the fed-to-starved timetable, the picture in diabetes and the drugs that intervene all stop being lists and become deductions. This note is the integrator: it points back at note 04 and at notes 06–08 constantly rather than re-teaching them.

§ 01

What a cofactor is for

The ideaA protein is built from twenty building blocks, and those twenty between them can push protons around and act as decent nucleophiles — and not much else. They cannot hold a spare pair of electrons, they cannot carry a one-carbon fragment, they cannot grab carbon dioxide, and they cannot pass electrons to oxygen. So for every one of those jobs the cell buys in a small molecule that can, and bolts it into the active site. That bought-in molecule is the coenzyme; the piece of it we cannot manufacture ourselves is the vitamin. Which is why the vitamins are not a random list to memorise: each one is the answer to a specific chemical problem, and if you know the problem you can name the vitamin.

Definition & key facts

Cofactor
Any non-protein component an enzyme needs to work. Two families: inorganic ions — mostly metals (Zn2+, Fe2+, Mg2+, Cu2+), though selenium is a non-metal and is built in as selenocysteine rather than bound as an ion — and coenzymes, which are organic. Most coenzymes are chemically modified vitamins.
apoenzyme (inactive protein) + cofactor → holoenzyme (active) the vitamin is the part of the cofactor you have to eat
Full definition, symbols and units
Prosthetic group vs cosubstrate
A prosthetic group stays bound — tightly or covalently — through the whole catalytic cycle and is regenerated in place: FAD, lipoamide, biotin, PLP, haem. A cosubstrate binds, is chemically altered, and leaves carrying the change to another enzyme: NAD+, CoA-SH, THF. The distinction predicts stoichiometry — a cosubstrate is consumed in molar amounts, a prosthetic group is catalytic.
Vitamin
An organic micronutrient required in milligram or microgram amounts that the organism cannot synthesise fast enough. "Cannot synthesise" is species-specific: humans lost L-gulonolactone oxidase and so need vitamin C; most mammals do not. Lipoic acid and CoQ10 are coenzymes but not vitamins — we make both.

The chemistry the side chains cannot do

Line up what the twenty side chains actually offer. Histidine, aspartate, glutamate, lysine, cysteine and tyrosine give acid–base catalysis across a useful pKa range. Serine, cysteine and lysine give nucleophiles. Arginine and lysine give positive charge for stabilising oxyanions. That is a complete list, and it covers hydrolysis, isomerisation, group transfer between heteroatoms — a great deal of chemistry. Now list what it does not cover:

  • Redox at a defined potential. Only cysteine is reliably redox-active, over a narrow range, and it cannot hand electrons on to anything else. (Tyrosine and tryptophan do form catalytic radicals in a few specialist enzymes — ribonucleotide reductase is the classic — but only transiently and only inside the protein.) Solution: nicotinamide (niacin) for obligate two-electron hydride transfer, flavin (riboflavin) when one electron at a time is needed.
  • Stabilising a carbanion. Breaking a C–C bond usually leaves a carbon with a lone pair and nowhere to put it. Solution: a ring with a positively charged nitrogen that can soak the electrons up — the thiazolium of TPP, the pyridinium of PLP.
  • Carrying an activated acyl group. An oxygen ester is too stable to be a useful donor. Solution: a thiol, from pantothenate, giving a thioester.
  • Grabbing CO2. CO2 is unreactive and bicarbonate is worse. Solution: biotin, which spends ATP to make a carboxy-nitrogen that is reactive.
  • Carrying one carbon at a chosen oxidation level. Solution: tetrahydrofolate, with three interconvertible carrier states.
  • Moving a whole carbon skeleton, or a methyl group onto a thiol. Solution: cobalamin, the only place in human biochemistry where a metal–carbon bond exists.

That is the whole logic of the water-soluble vitamins, and it is worth reading the list backwards too. Given a reaction, ask what bond is changing. A C–H removed as hydride with a carbonyl appearing? Nicotinamide. A C–C bond broken next to a carbonyl? A thiazolium or pyridinium electron sink. A carboxyl added? Biotin and ATP. A single carbon delivered? Folate. Nearly every cofactor question on the paper is answerable from the reaction type alone, without recalling the enzyme's name.

The distinction the paper actually tests

Not "which vitamin is B5" — that is trivia. What gets asked is coenzyme → reaction type and reaction type → coenzyme, usually embedded in a pathway or a clinical vignette: an enzyme is named, you must supply its cofactor, or a deficiency is described and you must say which step failed. A five-option stem will typically offer one right cofactor, one that catalyses the adjacent step in the same pathway, one that does the same class of chemistry in a different pathway, and two that are simply the wrong reaction type. Eliminating on reaction type kills the last two instantly, turning a one-in-five guess into a one-in-three — which, on a paper that penalises wrong answers, is already enough to make answering worth the risk.

Exam technique · naming a cofactor you have never heard of

  1. Do not read the enzyme name first — read the bond that changes. Examiners choose unfamiliar enzymes deliberately, and the name is the one part of the stem carrying information you cannot use. If the stem names no enzyme at all, that is a signal you are meant to answer from chemistry.
  2. Run the reaction-type list, in this order — carbonyl made or unmade → NAD+ · alkane to alkene, or anything involving O2 or a disulfide → FAD · a C–C bond cut next to a carbonyl, or a two-carbon unit moved between sugars → TPP · anything at all happening at Cα of an amino acid → PLP · an acyl group handed on, or a Claisen condensation → CoA · CO2 added and ATP spent → biotin · a single carbon delivered at any level short of CO2 → THF · a methyl put onto a thiol, or a carbon skeleton rearranged → B12 · a hydroxyl added by an Fe2+ enzyme that also consumes α-ketoglutarate → ascorbate · a glutamate γ-carboxylated with no ATP → vitamin K.
  3. Kill on reaction class before you think about pathways — of five options, two are usually the wrong kind of chemistry altogether and go in about five seconds. Those are the cheapest marks in the vitamin half, and you collect them without knowing the enzyme.
  4. Separate the survivors on location, not on chemistry — the two that remain are typically the right cofactor and the cofactor of the adjacent step in the same pathway. Re-read which substrate the stem actually named; that is what decides it.
  5. Know your guessing threshold, because Part II penalises −0.5. Expected value per question is −0.20 at five options, −0.125 at four, 0 at three and +0.25 at two. So in the biochemistry half two eliminations only reach break-even and the third is what makes answering profitable. The familiar "eliminate one and always answer" rule belongs to Part I, where the penalty is −0.25 and a blind guess is already neutral.

Two exceptions that carry most of the marks

B12 is water-soluble but behaves like a fat-soluble vitamin — hepatic stores of 2–5 mg against an obligate loss of 1–2 µg per day, so a newly vegan adult takes years, not weeks, to become deficient. And vitamin B6 is water-soluble but genuinely toxic: chronic megadoses (grams per day, and occasionally as little as 200–500 mg/day over years) cause a dose-dependent sensory ataxic neuropathy from dorsal root ganglion damage. If a question offers "water-soluble vitamins cannot cause toxicity" as an option, it is wrong.

Worked example 1 — why one deficiency takes three weeks and another takes four years

Estimate the time to depletion for (a) thiamine in a man who stops eating but continues drinking spirits, given a body pool of about 30 mg and an obligate daily loss of about 1.4 mg; (b) vitamin B12 in an adult who turns strictly vegan, given hepatic stores of 3 mg and an obligate daily loss of 2 µg; (c) folate, given a body pool of about 10 mg and a daily turnover of about 0.1 mg. Assume no intake at all.

  1. The model is just t = pool / daily loss, which is crude but captures the whole clinical difference.
  2. (a) Thiamine. 30 mg ÷ 1.4 mg·day⁻¹ = 21 days — three weeks. And this is generous: alcohol independently reduces absorption, hepatic storage and the pyrophosphorylation step, so the real figure is shorter. This is why Wernicke encephalopathy is an emergency of days, not months.
  3. (b) B12. Convert first: 3 mg = 3000 µg. 3000 µg ÷ 2 µg·day⁻¹ = 1500 days ≈ 4.1 years. Note the enterohepatic circulation of B12 is what makes the loss so small — which is also why losing intrinsic factor (pernicious anaemia) or the terminal ileum is worse than losing dietary intake: it interrupts reabsorption as well, and the clock runs in months rather than years.
  4. (c) Folate. 10 mg ÷ 0.1 mg·day⁻¹ = 100 days ≈ 3.3 months. Which matches the classic self-experiment: a folate-free diet gives hypersegmented neutrophils at about 7 weeks and a frankly megaloblastic marrow at about 19 weeks.
  5. Read the three answers together. All three vitamins are "water-soluble", and the time to disease differs by a factor of seventy. Pool size divided by turnover is the quantity that matters, not the solubility label.

§ 02

Niacin and riboflavin: two redox currencies, and how to tell which one a reaction needs

The ideaAlmost every step in metabolism that releases energy is an oxidation, and an oxidation needs somewhere for the electrons to go. The cell keeps two carriers for the job and they are not interchangeable. One takes exactly two electrons at a time and then floats away to spend them elsewhere; the other is bolted into its enzyme and will take one electron or two, which lets it act as a gearbox between the two-at-a-time world of metabolism and the one-at-a-time world of the iron and copper centres in the respiratory chain. Which carrier a given reaction uses is not arbitrary — it is decided by how hard the electrons are to remove, and you can predict it from the kind of bond being broken.

Definition & key facts

NAD+ / NADH (from niacin, B3)
Accepts a hydride ion (H− = one proton + two electrons) at C4 of the nicotinamide ring; the second hydrogen leaves as a free H+. Obligately two-electron. Binds loosely and dissociates — a cosubstrate, not a prosthetic group.
substrate-H₂ + NAD⁺ → substrate + NADH + H⁺ note the free H⁺ — writing "NADH₂" is wrong and is marked as wrong
Full definition, symbols and units
FAD / FMN (from riboflavin, B2)
FMN is riboflavin 5′-phosphate; FAD is FMN joined to AMP. The reactive part is the tricyclic isoalloxazine ring, which can accept one electron (giving a stable, coloured semiquinone radical) or two. Almost always a tightly bound prosthetic group — covalently attached in succinate dehydrogenase.
NADP+ / NADPH
NAD+ carrying an extra phosphate on the 2′-OH of the adenosine ribose. Chemically it does exactly the same hydride transfer; the phosphate is a recognition handle that lets enzymes sort the two pools.

Deriving the rule: why succinate dehydrogenase cannot use NAD+

This is the best worked argument in the whole cofactor topic, because it turns a memorised pairing into a calculation. Succinate dehydrogenase removes two hydrogens from succinate to make fumarate — a C–C dehydrogenation, creating an alkene. The fumarate/succinate couple sits at E°′ = +0.031 V. Ask whether NAD+ could accept those electrons:

ΔE°′ = (−0.320) − (+0.031) = −0.351 V → ΔG°′ = −2(96.485)(−0.351) = +67.7 kJ·mol⁻¹uphill by more than two ATP hydrolyses — not happening
enzyme-bound FAD, E°′ ≈ +0.03 V: ΔE°′ ≈ 0.00 V → ΔG°′ ≈ 0 kJ·mol⁻¹thermodynamically neutral; mass action carries it

So the pairing is forced. NAD+ is simply too poor an oxidant to strip electrons from an alkane; the flavin, and particularly a flavin whose potential the protein has tuned upwards by its binding environment, is not. That last point is worth holding on to: free flavin has one potential, bound flavin has whatever potential its protein gives it — the measured range across flavoenzymes runs from about −0.45 V to +0.06 V. Nicotinamide has no such flexibility, because it dissociates and must therefore work at its solution potential everywhere in the cell.

Generalise it and you get a rule that answers most exam pairings without any recall at all:

Which carrier?

Carbonyl calls NAD · alkene asks FAD

Two clauses, each held together by its own letter, and the two carriers rhyme so they cannot swap places. Carbonyl calls NAD+ — if the step makes or unmakes a C=O, an alcohol going to an aldehyde or ketone, or an aldehyde going to an acid, the oxygen already on that carbon makes the electrons easy to take and nicotinamide is a good enough oxidant. Alkene asks FAD — if the step makes a C=C out of an alkane, with no oxygen on either carbon to help, nicotinamide is too weak and the flavin has to be asked instead. So: C–O oxidation → NAD+; C–C oxidation → FAD. The line is our own phrasing; the rule underneath it is standard and, better still, derivable — the calculation above is where it comes from, so if the phrase deserts you, ask whether NAD+ at −0.320 V is a strong enough oxidant, and it never is for a C–C bond. What the line does not cover is the flavin's second speciality: reactions involving O2 or a disulfide need single-electron chemistry, so xanthine oxidase, monoamine oxidase, glutathione reductase and dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase are all FAD even though no alkene appears. Add that clause and the rule covers every dehydrogenase in the note.

Check it against every case you know. NAD+: glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, lactate dehydrogenase, malate dehydrogenase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, β-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase, alcohol dehydrogenase, glutamate dehydrogenase — in every one of them a carbonyl is made or unmade (in glutamate dehydrogenase the new carbonyl replaces an amino group rather than a hydroxyl, but the electron bookkeeping is identical). FAD: succinate dehydrogenase, acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (the first step of β-oxidation, and the same alkane-to-alkene chemistry), mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase, xanthine oxidase, monoamine oxidase, glutathione reductase. The last three point at the flavin's second specialty: reactions involving O2 or a disulfide, both of which need single-electron chemistry that nicotinamide cannot do.

Why the flavin is the gearbox

Metabolic dehydrogenations hand over two electrons at once. The respiratory chain's Fe–S clusters, cytochromes and Cu centres accept one at a time. Something has to split the pair, and the flavin's stable semiquinone is what does it. That is exactly the job of FMN in Complex I — take a hydride from NADH, then pass the electrons on singly to the Fe–S chain — and of FAD in Complex II, and of the electron-transferring flavoprotein (ETF) that collects electrons from the acyl-CoA dehydrogenases. Note also what Complex II means for the ATP ledger of note 06: its electrons enter at ubiquinone, below Complex I, so they drive two proton pumps instead of three — the whole reason FADH2 is worth ~1.5 ATP and NADH ~2.5.

Go deeper · why the cell keeps two nicotinamide pools, and what G6PD deficiency does about it

NADH and NADPH are the same molecule doing the same chemistry, separated only by one phosphate. So why keep two? Because a cell needs to run oxidations and reductions at the same time in the same cytosol, and a single pool cannot be poised for both. The trick is to hold them at opposite ratios:

  • [NAD+]/[NADH] ≈ 700 in the cytosol — overwhelmingly oxidised, so any dehydrogenase that needs to take electrons finds an acceptor waiting. This is the pool that feeds respiration.
  • [NADPH]/[NADP+] ≈ 75 — overwhelmingly reduced, so any reductase that needs to give electrons finds a donor waiting. This is the pool that builds things.

Where NADPH comes from: the oxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, two NADPH per glucose-6-phosphate), malic enzyme, and cytosolic isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH1). Where it goes: fatty-acid and cholesterol synthesis (reductive biosynthesis — 14 NADPH per palmitate); regenerating reduced glutathione via glutathione reductase; thioredoxin reductase, which feeds ribonucleotide reductase; cytochrome P450 mono-oxygenases; nitric oxide synthase; and — deliberately destructively — the NADPH oxidase of the phagocyte respiratory burst.

G6PD deficiency is the clinical demonstration. The red cell has no mitochondria and no nucleus, so the pentose phosphate pathway is its only source of NADPH and it cannot make more enzyme. Lose G6PD and glutathione cannot be kept reduced; an oxidant challenge — primaquine, dapsone, sulfonamides, nitrofurantoin, fava beans, or simply the oxidative stress of infection — then denatures haemoglobin into Heinz bodies, which the spleen bites out to leave bite cells. The haemolysis is episodic and self-limiting in the common Mediterranean and African variants because the youngest red cells retain most enzyme activity. X-linked; the commonest enzymopathy in the world; and the geographic overlap with malaria is the usual heterozygote- advantage story.

Niacin: the vitamin you can partly make, and the disease of maize

Niacin (nicotinic acid or nicotinamide) is unusual: the body can synthesise it from tryptophan, at roughly 60 mg of tryptophan per 1 mg of niacin. Hence the unit — the niacin equivalent — and hence three separate ways to become deficient, all of which appear in vignettes:

  • A maize-based diet. Maize niacin is bound as niacytin and is not absorbed, and maize protein is low in tryptophan. Traditional Mesoamerican nixtamalización — soaking maize in lime water — hydrolyses the bond and releases the niacin, which is why pellagra appeared in Europe and the American South when maize was adopted without the alkali step.
  • Hartnup disease. An autosomal recessive defect of the neutral amino-acid transporter B0AT1 (SLC6A19) in intestine and proximal tubule: tryptophan is lost in the urine and poorly absorbed, so the endogenous route fails. Pellagra-like rash and cerebellar ataxia; treated with nicotinamide.
  • Carcinoid syndrome. A serotonin-secreting tumour diverts tryptophan down the 5-hydroxyindole route, starving the niacin pathway. Add the flushing and diarrhoea of the carcinoid itself and the picture becomes confusing — which is exactly why examiners like it.
  • Drugs. Isoniazid depletes pyridoxal phosphate, and the tryptophan → niacin route needs PLP at the kynureninase step. So an anti-tuberculous drug causes a B3 deficiency by way of a B6 deficiency — a two-step inference the paper is fond of.

Pellagra is the classic three Ds, then a fourth: dermatitis — symmetrical, on sun-exposed skin, with the collar-like Casal's necklace; diarrhoea from an atrophic, inflamed gut; dementia, in practice a confusional state with irritability and memory loss; and death untreated. The photosensitivity is the tell: NAD+-dependent repair and antioxidant systems fail first where the oxidative load is highest.

Worked example 2 — niacin equivalents, and what a carcinoid does to them

A 62-year-old man has a metastatic midgut carcinoid tumour. His diet supplies 4 mg of preformed niacin and 900 mg of tryptophan per day. Normally about 1/60 of dietary tryptophan is converted to niacin; the tumour diverts an estimated 70 % of his tryptophan into serotonin synthesis. Compute his niacin equivalents with and without the tumour and compare with the adult RDA of 16 mg NE per day.

  1. Without the tumour. NE = 4 + 900/60 = 4 + 15 = 19 mg NE·day⁻¹ — comfortably above 16, which is why he was previously well on this diet.
  2. With the tumour. Tryptophan still available to the niacin route = 900 × (1 − 0.70) = 270 mg.
  3. NE = 4 + 270/60 = 4 + 4.5 = 8.5 mg NE·day⁻¹.
  4. That is 8.5/16 = 53 % of requirement — a sustained deficit, and he presents months later with a photosensitive rash and diarrhoea that are mistakenly attributed to the carcinoid itself.
  5. Read the arithmetic for what it teaches: the endogenous route was carrying 15/19 = 79 % of his niacin. Any condition that competes for tryptophan, or blocks the PLP-dependent step in the pathway, is a niacin disease even though niacin intake never changed.
normal 19 mg NE·day⁻¹ · with the tumour 8.5 mg NE·day⁻¹ (53 % of the 16 mg RDA)

Exam technique · niacin-equivalent arithmetic in forty seconds

  1. Recognise it instantly — the stem gives milligrams of preformed niacin and milligrams of tryptophan. Nothing conceptual is being tested; every mark is in one division, so do not re-read the physiology.
  2. Run the same chain every time — take the tryptophan, remove whatever fraction the stem says is diverted or lost, ÷ 60, then add the preformed niacin. Compare the total against 16 mg NE·day−1 to say whether the patient is deficient.
  3. Kill on order of magnitude before you calculate — any option in the hundreds of mg has dropped the ÷ 60 (900 mg of tryptophan is 15 mg NE, not 900). No diet supplies hundreds of milligrams of a B vitamin. That is one free elimination on every version of this question.
  4. Kill the "diversion forgotten" option, which is always present — with 4 mg preformed and 900 mg tryptophan, 19 mg NE is the answer without the tumour and 8.5 mg NE is the answer with it. Both are true sentences; only one answers the question asked. Check the stem's tense and the word "currently" before you commit.
  5. If the stem mentions tryptophan, think B6 as well — the kynureninase step needs PLP, so isoniazid and the other carbonyl-trapping drugs produce pellagra with niacin intake untouched. A stem that describes a photosensitive rash in a tuberculosis patient is a B6 question wearing a B3 coat.

Riboflavin, and why its deficiency hides

Riboflavin deficiency (ariboflavinosis) gives angular stomatitis and cheilosis at the corners of the mouth, a smooth magenta glossitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis around the nose, corneal neovascularisation, and a normochromic normocytic anaemia. It is almost never isolated, because the diets and the malabsorptions that remove riboflavin remove the other B vitamins too — so it appears in vignettes as one component of a mixed deficiency in an alcoholic or a patient with untreated coeliac disease. Two connections are worth carrying: riboflavin is needed for the FAD in MTHFR (§ 06), so riboflavin status modifies homocysteine, particularly in carriers of the common MTHFR C677T variant; and FAD is required by glutathione reductase, whose activation by added FAD in vitro is the standard functional assay for riboflavin status.

Niacin the drug is not niacin the vitamin

At 1.5–3 g/day — a thousand times the RDA — nicotinic acid lowers LDL and triglyceride and raises HDL, and was for decades a lipid drug. Its side effects are a good exam item: prostaglandin D2-mediated cutaneous flushing (blunted by aspirin, and absent with nicotinamide, which lacks the lipid effect entirely), hyperglycaemia, hyperuricaemia and gout, and hepatotoxicity with the sustained-release preparations. Note the modern verdict: large outcome trials (AIM-HIGH, HPS2-THRIVE) found no cardiovascular benefit when added to a statin, so raising HDL turned out not to be the same thing as reducing risk — a useful caution about surrogate endpoints.

§ 03

Thiamine, TPP and the α-keto acid dehydrogenases

The ideaThere is one chemical trick this section is about. To cut a carbon off the end of a molecule as carbon dioxide, you have to leave the neighbouring carbon holding a pair of electrons it very much does not want. Nothing in a protein can look after those electrons, so the cell brings in a ring with a positive nitrogen in it, which is delighted to take them. That ring comes from thiamine. Once you see that, the four enzymes that use it stop looking like an arbitrary set — they are simply the four places in metabolism where a carbon has to be cut off next to a carbonyl. And the deficiency syndromes are simply what happens when those four places jam.

Definition & mechanism

Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP)
Thiamine (B1) plus a pyrophosphate transferred from ATP. The catalytic atom is C2 of the thiazolium ring, sitting between a sulfur and a positively charged nitrogen. Losing its proton gives a carbanion — the ylide — which the adjacent N+ stabilises.

The catalytic cycle, in four moves
(1) The ylide carbanion attacks the keto carbon of the α-keto acid. (2) The C–C bond to the carboxylate breaks, releasing CO2 and leaving a carbanion that is delocalised onto the ring nitrogen — this intermediate is an acyl anion equivalent, a carbon that is nucleophilic where it "should" be electrophilic. (3) That carbanion attacks the next acceptor — the disulfide of lipoamide in the dehydrogenases, or an aldose in transketolase. (4) The ylide is released unchanged.
Why C2 and not some other carbon
The C2 proton is unusually acidic (pKa around 18 in water, and far lower in the enzyme, which also uses a conserved glutamate to deprotonate the aminopyrimidine ring, which in turn deprotonates C2). No amino-acid side chain comes close to stabilising a carbanion this way — hence the vitamin.

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex: five cofactors, and why it needs all of them

PDH is the classic examination object because it is the one place where five cofactors are visible at once, and because each is there for a reason you can state. Follow the two carbons of pyruvate through:

  1. E1 (pyruvate dehydrogenase), TPP. Decarboxylates pyruvate and holds the remaining two carbons as the hydroxyethyl-TPP carbanion. Why TPP: nothing else stabilises that carbanion.
  2. E2 (dihydrolipoyl transacetylase), lipoamide. The carbanion attacks the disulfide of lipoic acid, which is amide-linked to a lysine side chain — the resulting long, flexible lipoyllysine arm (about 1.4 nm) swings the growing acetyl group between the three active sites. Why lipoamide: the disulfide is simultaneously the oxidant and the acceptor, and the arm solves the geometry problem.
  3. E2 again, CoA-SH. The acetyl group is transferred from the lipoyl arm to coenzyme A, giving acetyl-CoA. Why CoA: a thioester is the only acyl carrier energetic enough to donate to citrate synthase later (§ 04).
  4. E3 (dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase), FAD. Reoxidises the dihydrolipoamide back to the disulfide. Why FAD: disulfide chemistry needs a flavin (§ 02).
  5. E3 again, NAD+. Reoxidises FADH2. Why NAD+: it is the pool connected to the respiratory chain, so the electrons end up where they are useful.

It pays three times over: α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase take the identical five, sharing the same E3. What it does not encode is which two are consumed and which three are catalytic — that is the line above, and it is the stoichiometry question. As for the four TPP enzymes themselves, there is no mnemonic and you do not need one: they are every α-keto acid dehydrogenase in the body (pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, branched-chain) plus transketolase, which is the only one you have to hold separately — and you will, because it is the enzyme the diagnostic red-cell assay measures. A hook is sometimes given for the first three; it drops the branched-chain one, which is the one worth having.

The same E3 subunit is shared by all three dehydrogenase complexes and by the glycine cleavage system. That is why an inherited E3 (dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase) deficiency produces a combined picture — lactic acidosis from PDH, α-ketoglutaric aciduria, branched-chain ketoaciduria and hyperglycinaemia all at once — while an E1α mutation hits only pyruvate.

Arsenic, and the one mechanism you should be able to state

Trivalent arsenic (arsenite, As3+) forms a stable ring with vicinal dithiols — two SH groups on adjacent carbons. The reduced lipoamide arm is exactly that. So arsenite inactivates PDH, α-KGDH and BCKDH simultaneously, producing a biochemical picture indistinguishable from severe thiamine deficiency: lactic acidosis, encephalopathy, neuropathy. Clinical clues are garlicky breath, Mees' lines on the nails and a rice-water diarrhoea; dimercaprol works because it is itself a dithiol and competes the arsenic away. Do not confuse this with pentavalent arsenate (As5+), which is a phosphate analogue and uncouples glycolysis at glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase by forming an unstable acyl arsenate that hydrolyses spontaneously — so ATP is not made at the phosphoglycerate kinase step. Different oxidation state, different target, both examinable.

Beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff: reading the syndrome from the biochemistry

Thiamine deficiency damages the tissues with the highest and least flexible oxidative demand, which is why the clinical picture is neurological and cardiac rather than, say, hepatic. Four presentations:

  • Dry beriberi — a symmetrical distal sensorimotor peripheral neuropathy with wrist and foot drop. Long axons have the largest ATP bill per cell.
  • Wet beriberi — high-output cardiac failure with peripheral oedema. The counter-intuitive bit is the high output: impaired ATP generation in vascular smooth muscle causes systemic vasodilatation and a fall in peripheral resistance, so cardiac output rises even as the myocardium itself is failing. A vignette describing warm extremities, a wide pulse pressure and oedema in a malnourished patient is pointing at this.
  • Wernicke encephalopathy — the triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia (classically lateral rectus palsy and nystagmus) and ataxia, from lesions in the mammillary bodies and periaqueductal grey. Reversible with prompt thiamine.
  • Korsakoff syndrome — the irreversible sequel: anterograde and retrograde amnesia with confabulation, from damage to the mammillary bodies and dorsomedial thalamus.

Alcohol is the commonest cause in high-income settings and it attacks thiamine four ways at once: poor intake, impaired intestinal absorption, reduced hepatic storage, and impaired conversion of thiamine to TPP. Other settings that appear in vignettes: hyperemesis gravidarum, bariatric surgery, prolonged parenteral nutrition without vitamins, refeeding after starvation, and chronic loop-diuretic use in heart failure.

Glucose before thiamine precipitates Wernicke encephalopathy

This is the single most quoted clinical corollary in the whole vitamin topic, and it is derivable rather than memorisable. A glucose load drives glycolysis, which floods the mitochondrion with pyruvate and demands PDH and α-KGDH activity — consuming what little TPP is left. In a depleted patient a bag of dextrose can therefore convert a marginal state into acute Wernicke encephalopathy within hours. Give thiamine first, or at least at the same time. The same logic explains why refeeding syndrome includes a thiamine requirement alongside the phosphate, potassium and magnesium shifts.

§ 04

Pantothenate, CoA and biotin: the acyl carrier and the CO2 carrier

The ideaTwo of metabolism's commonest moves need a molecule that no protein can supply. The first is handing a two-carbon acetyl group from one enzyme to another in a form that is still reactive when it arrives — an ordinary ester would be far too placid, so the cell uses a sulfur instead of an oxygen and gets a carrier that is chemically loaded. The second is adding a carbon dioxide molecule onto something. Carbon dioxide is a famously unreactive gas, so it has to be activated first, and that costs an ATP; the molecule that holds the activated form on a long swinging arm is biotin. Every carboxylation in human metabolism runs through it, and you can name all four.

Why a thioester is "high energy"
In an ordinary ester the oxygen's lone pair overlaps well with the carbonyl π system, so the starting material is resonance-stabilised. Sulfur's 3p orbitals overlap poorly with carbon's 2p, so a thioester gets almost none of that stabilisation — while the carboxylate product is fully stabilised. Large drop, large −ΔG°′. Coenzyme A: one molecule, three jobs

Pantothenate is the vitamin nobody becomes deficient in — it is in essentially every food, and the name means "from everywhere". Experimental deficiency gives a burning-feet dysaesthesia. But the coenzyme it becomes is everywhere in metabolism, and its three roles are worth separating:

  • Acyl transfer. Acetyl-CoA donating to oxaloacetate (citrate synthase), to choline (choline acetyltransferase), to lysine side chains (histone acetyltransferases), to arylamines (N-acetyltransferase, the slow/fast acetylator polymorphism that governs isoniazid and hydralazine toxicity).
  • Carbanion chemistry. The acidic α-carbon of a thioester is the nucleophile in every Claisen condensation in metabolism: citrate synthase, thiolase (β-oxidation and ketogenesis, note 07), HMG-CoA synthase, and the condensing step of fatty-acid synthase.
  • Activation, at a price. Attaching CoA to a fatty acid (acyl-CoA synthetase) costs two high-energy phosphate bonds, because ATP is cleaved to AMP + PPi and the pyrophosphate is then hydrolysed. That is the standard trick for making an activation step irreversible, and it is why β-oxidation's ledger starts at −2 ATP.

One structural point that gets asked: the same pantothenate-derived arm, 4′-phosphopantetheine, is the prosthetic group of the acyl carrier protein domain of fatty-acid synthase. So pantothenate appears twice in fatty-acid synthesis — once as CoA on the substrate, once as the ACP arm that walks the growing chain between catalytic domains.

And the line says nothing about the clinical picture, which you derive instead: all four carboxylases fail at once, so you get lactic acidosis plus an organic aciduria on top of the skin and hair signs.

Biotin deficiency is rare but has three memorable causes, all of which appear in questions. Raw egg white contains avidin, which binds biotin with extraordinary affinity (Kd ≈ 10−15 M) and is denatured by cooking — hence "the body-builder who eats a dozen raw eggs a day". Prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics or total parenteral nutrition without supplementation. And biotinidase deficiency, an autosomal recessive failure to recycle biotin from degraded carboxylases, which is on newborn screening programmes because it is treatable with oral biotin and devastating if missed. The clinical picture in all cases: periorificial dermatitis, alopecia (including loss of eyebrows and lashes), conjunctivitis, glossitis, and — because all four carboxylases fail — lactic acidosis with an organic aciduria, seizures and hypotonia.

A very modern clinical hook: biotin ruins immunoassays

High-dose biotin supplements (5–10 mg, sold for hair and nails) saturate the streptavidin–biotin capture chemistry used in many automated immunoassays. In a competitive assay format the result is falsely high; in a sandwich format falsely low. The classic consequence is a patient who appears to have Graves' disease — high free T4, suppressed TSH — with no clinical thyrotoxicosis, and a falsely low troponin in someone with chest pain. Stopping the supplement for 2–3 days normalises everything. This is exactly the kind of item the clinical-vignette half of the paper has been reaching for.

§ 05

Pyridoxine and PLP: one electron sink, six reaction types

The ideaAmino acids are all built the same way: a central carbon carrying a hydrogen, an acid group and a side chain. If you want to do anything to an amino acid, you have to break one of those three bonds, and breaking any of them leaves the central carbon holding electrons it cannot keep. So the cell has one universal solution — a ring with a positively charged nitrogen that clamps onto the amino acid and drains the electrons away down a chain of double bonds. The remarkable part is that which of the three bonds breaks is decided by the enzyme, simply by holding the amino acid at the right angle. One cofactor, one mechanism, and a family of completely different-looking reactions.

Definition & mechanism

Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)
The active form of vitamin B6. At rest it is held in the active site as an internal aldimine — a Schiff base to a lysine side chain. The substrate amino acid displaces the lysine to form an external aldimine, and catalysis proceeds from there.
enzyme–Lys=PLP + H₂N–CHR–COO⁻ ⇌ substrate=PLP + enzyme–Lys–NH₂ transaldimination: the substrate takes the cofactor off the enzyme's own lysine
Full definition, symbols and units
The electron sink
The protonated pyridinium nitrogen of the ring is a sink for the electron pair left behind when a bond at Cα breaks. The resulting delocalised intermediate is the quinonoid. Every PLP reaction passes through it.
Dunathan's hypothesis
The bond that breaks is the one the enzyme holds perpendicular to the plane of the conjugated ring system, because only that orientation lets the developing carbanion overlap with the π system. Rotate the substrate by 120° in the active site and you get a completely different reaction from the same cofactor.
Vitamers
Pyridoxine (the supplement), pyridoxal and pyridoxamine, all phosphorylated by pyridoxal kinase. PLP and pyridoxamine phosphate interconvert during transamination.

Reading the deficiency off the mechanism

Because PLP sits in so many pathways, its deficiency is best learned as four consequences rather than a list of symptoms:

  • Seizures. Glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) makes GABA. Lose PLP and the inhibitory transmitter fails while the excitatory one accumulates. Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy in neonates, and seizures in isoniazid overdose, are both treated with pyridoxine — often in gram doses.
  • Sideroblastic anaemia. δ-Aminolaevulinate synthase, the rate-limiting step of haem synthesis, is PLP-dependent. Iron arrives in the mitochondrion and has no protoporphyrin to go into, so it deposits in a ring around the nucleus — ringed sideroblasts. The anaemia is microcytic with a high serum iron and ferritin, which is what separates it from iron deficiency.
  • Peripheral neuropathy, cheilosis, glossitis — the general picture of a B-vitamin deficiency, shared with riboflavin and niacin.
  • Raised homocysteine. Cystathionine β-synthase, the entrance to transsulfuration, is PLP-dependent (§ 06). So B6 joins folate and B12 as a determinant of homocysteine — but note the pattern differs, which the next section uses.

The commonest cause in a vignette is a drug. Isoniazid forms a hydrazone with pyridoxal and inhibits pyridoxal kinase, which is why prophylactic pyridoxine is co-prescribed. Penicillamine, cycloserine and hydralazine do the same thing by the same carbonyl-trapping chemistry. Chronic alcohol accelerates PLP degradation.

Why this section carries no mnemonic — and does not need one

There is deliberately no mnemonic here for the B6 deficiency picture, because every item on that list is the name of a PLP enzyme you have already met, and a hook would be a second thing to remember on top of a derivation that is quicker than the hook. Seizures = GAD, so GABA fails while glutamate does not. Sideroblastic anaemia = ALA synthase, so haem cannot be built and the iron has nowhere to go — microcytic with a high iron, which is what separates it from iron deficiency. Raised homocysteine = CBS, so transsulfuration is shut. Cheilosis, glossitis and neuropathy are the generic B-vitamin picture and carry no information at all. Learn those three enzymes and the syndrome is a deduction. The same move runs forwards, and it is the more valuable direction: given an unfamiliar enzyme doing anything whatever at Cα of an amino acid, answer PLP without recognising the name. The one fact here that is genuinely not derivable is the exception below — glycogen phosphorylase — and it has to be held on its own precisely because no rule generates it.

Two PLP facts that are asked precisely because they are exceptions

Glycogen phosphorylase contains PLP — one molecule per subunit, and because muscle holds so much of the enzyme this is the body's largest single reservoir of vitamin B6. But it does not use PLP as a Schiff base at all: the cofactor's 5′-phosphate group acts as a general acid–base catalyst assisting the incoming inorganic phosphate. It is the one major PLP enzyme that has nothing to do with amino acids. Second: pyridoxine is the water-soluble vitamin with a real toxicity — chronic high-dose supplementation causes a dose-dependent sensory ataxic neuropathy with preserved motor power, from dorsal root ganglion damage. Both facts are attractive exam material because both contradict the tidy generalisation.

§ 06

Folate, B12 and one-carbon metabolism

The ideaBuilding DNA and methylating things both come down to moving single carbon atoms around, and a single carbon is far too reactive to let loose in a cell. Folate is the handle that carries it — and it can carry it at three different levels of "cooked", from almost-formaldehyde to almost-methanol. There is one exit from the most-reduced level, and only one, and that exit needs vitamin B12. So if B12 runs out, the entire folate supply piles up behind that door and the cell starves of folate even though folate is everywhere. That single traffic jam explains why the two deficiencies look identical in the blood, why giving the wrong vitamin is dangerous, and which laboratory test tells them apart.

What the one-carbon pool is spent on

Three things, and they explain the entire clinical picture:

  1. Thymidylate. Thymidylate synthase methylates dUMP to dTMP using N5,N10-methylene-THF. This is the only de novo source of thymine for DNA (salvage by thymidine kinase is why exogenous thymidine rescues an antifolate-blocked cell), and it is the step that fails first when folate runs short — because it is the only folate reaction that also spends THF's own reducing power, leaving DHF that must be re-reduced.
  2. Purines. N10-formyl-THF donates C2 and C8 of the purine ring, at two separate transformylase steps.
  3. Methylation. The methyl group handed to homocysteine becomes methionine, then S-adenosylmethionine, the universal methyl donor for DNA, histones, phospholipids, creatine, adrenaline and dozens of small molecules.

Now put the failure together. Blocked thymidylate and purine synthesis means a cell that can transcribe and grow but cannot finish replicating DNA. In tissues that divide fastest — bone marrow and gut epithelium — the nucleus lags behind the cytoplasm. That is exactly what megaloblastic means: large immature erythroid precursors with an open, lacy nuclear chromatin in a cell whose cytoplasm has matured normally, giving nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony. In the peripheral blood you see macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils (more than 5 % with five lobes, or any with six), with a raised MCV and — because many precursors die in the marrow — ineffective erythropoiesis: a high LDH, a raised indirect bilirubin and a low reticulocyte count despite anaemia.

Methyl makes Methionine · Methylene methylates dUMP · Formyl forms the purine ring

The same line as note 08, so learn it once and it serves both chapters, and two thirds of it check themselves — the donor names its own product. Methyl makes methionine: N5-methyl-THF, the most reduced form, hands its carbon to homocysteine at methionine synthase, which is where B12 is required. Methylene methylates dUMP: N5,N10-methylene-THF is the substrate of thymidylate synthase, and the carbon it delivers ends up as the methyl of dTMP — thymidylate. Formyl forms the ring: N10-formyl-THF, the most oxidised form, supplies purine C2 and C8. Read the three clauses left to right and you are also reading the oxidation ladder from most reduced to most oxidised. Our own phrasing rather than a circulating hook, and it earns its place for one reason: the pair people invert is methyl versus methylene, and that is exactly the pair the methyl trap turns on. If it deserts you, derive it from oxidation level — a methyl transfer needs a carbon already at the methyl level, a ring carbon flanked by two nitrogens needs one at the formate level, and thymine's methyl needs the level in between plus a reduction, which is why that reaction alone leaves DHF behind. It encodes neither the drugs nor the one carbon THF cannot carry, which is CO2 — biotin's job, and a standard trap.

Telling the two apart — the item that is worth the most marks in this note

Both deficiencies produce an identical megaloblastic anaemia. Both raise homocysteine, because homocysteine is disposed of through methionine synthase, which needs a methyl group from folate carried by B12 — remove either and homocysteine backs up. Only B12 deficiency raises methylmalonic acid, because that branch is a purely B12 reaction with no folate involvement at all. And only B12 deficiency causes neurological disease.

Our own line, and if you carry one sentence out of the vitamin half, carry this one: the two things that belong to B12 alone both begin with M, and homocysteine belongs to neither side because it belongs to both. Myelin is the clinical half of the same claim: subacute combined degeneration is B12's alone. Read it as a 2 × 2 and it answers every version of the question: ↑Hcy with normal MMA = folate; ↑Hcy with ↑MMA = B12; both normal = neither, so the macrocytosis is coming from alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, a drug or myelodysplasia. One thing it deliberately excludes: the neural tube defect is the folate item, and it is the one neurological word in this section that does not mean B12 — it is a fetal developmental lesion, not a deficiency neuropathy in the patient in front of you.

Never treat a megaloblastic anaemia with folate before excluding B12

Folate supplementation bypasses the trap — enough THF reaches thymidylate synthase for the marrow to recover, so the blood count normalises and the diagnosis looks made. But the second B12 reaction is untouched, and the accumulating propionyl-CoA and methylmalonyl-CoA (plus impaired SAM-dependent methylation of myelin) go on damaging the cord. The patient walks out with a normal haemoglobin and progressive, eventually irreversible, subacute combined degeneration. This is the classic clinical consequence of the methyl trap, and it is asked in almost exactly this form.

Exam technique · any megaloblastic-anaemia stem

  1. Recognition rule, five seconds — macrocytosis plus hypersegmented neutrophils means the marrow is megaloblastic, so the question is folate versus B12 and nothing else. Macrocytosis without hypersegmentation is a different question entirely: alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, reticulocytosis after bleeding.
  2. Go straight to the MMA line and stop — if the stem gives methylmalonic acid, the answer is already decided: raised = B12, normal with a raised homocysteine = folate. Read no further into the panel; the rest of it is there to spend your 66 seconds.
  3. Two free eliminations, on almost every version — kill any option that gives folate deficiency subacute combined degeneration, and kill any option that leaves homocysteine normal in B12 deficiency. Neither requires you to have decided the answer.
  4. If the stem contains a treatment, check the tense — "given folate, count corrected, neurology then progressed" is asking what was true before treatment. The post-treatment picture will be sitting there as an option, and it will look right because it is a true sentence about the wrong moment. This is the single most reliable trap in the section.
  5. Get the cause from anatomy, not from a list — walk the gut in order and the causes appear in order: stomach (pernicious anaemia, gastrectomy, achlorhydria, PPI), pancreas (chronic pancreatitis), terminal ileum (Crohn, resection, Diphyllobothrium), then the drugs that act there (metformin). There is no mnemonic for the four hand-offs and you do not need one — each hand-off is a disease.

Why B12 absorption is so easy to break

B12 is the only vitamin that needs a dedicated protein escort through the gut, and the route has four hand-offs — each one a place a disease can attack. Dietary B12 bound to protein is released by gastric acid and pepsin, then picked up by haptocorrin from saliva. In the duodenum pancreatic proteases digest the haptocorrin and hand B12 to intrinsic factor, secreted by gastric parietal cells. The IF–B12 complex is absorbed by the cubilin receptor in the terminal ileum, and travels in blood on transcobalamin II. Now map the diseases onto the hand-offs: achlorhydria and PPIs block release from food; autoimmune destruction of parietal cells or anti-IF antibodies gives pernicious anaemia (with atrophic gastritis, achlorhydria and a raised gastrin); chronic pancreatitis blocks the haptocorrin hand-off; ileal resection or Crohn disease removes the receptor.

Go deeper · the antifolate drugs, and why leucovorin rescues methotrexate but potentiates 5-FU

This pathway is one of the most heavily drugged in medicine, and the drugs are examinable precisely because each one names its step.

  • Sulfonamides — structural analogues of PABA, competitive inhibitors of bacterial dihydropteroate synthase. Selective because humans do not synthesise folate at all; we eat it.
  • Trimethoprim, pyrimethamine, methotrexate — all inhibit dihydrofolate reductase, differing only in which species' enzyme they fit. Trimethoprim binds bacterial DHFR some 105-fold more tightly than human; pyrimethamine targets protozoal DHFR (toxoplasmosis, malaria); methotrexate binds the human enzyme with a Ki in the picomolar range, which is why it is a cytotoxic.
  • 5-Fluorouracil — converted intracellularly to FdUMP, which forms a covalent ternary complex with thymidylate synthase and N5,N10-methylene-THF. A suicide inhibitor: the enzyme is trapped, not merely blocked. Capecitabine is its oral prodrug.

Now the rescue logic, which is the point. Folinic acid (leucovorin, N5-formyl-THF) is folate that has already been reduced — it does not need DHFR.

  • After high-dose methotrexate, the block is at DHFR, so supplying pre-reduced folate bypasses the block and rescues normal marrow and gut. Timed rescue is standard practice.
  • With 5-FU, the block is at thymidylate synthase and the ternary complex needs methylene-THF to form. Supplying more reduced folate therefore stabilises the inhibited complex and increases both efficacy and toxicity. Leucovorin is deliberately co-administered with 5-FU in colorectal cancer for exactly this reason.

Same drug, opposite purposes, and the difference is entirely predictable from where the block sits relative to the rescue agent's entry point. Note also the everyday consequence: trimethoprim raises homocysteine and can precipitate megaloblastic change in a folate-marginal patient, and phenytoin and valproate interfere with folate status — which is why women on anticonvulsants take higher periconceptional folate doses. Standard prophylaxis is 400 µg/day from before conception through the first trimester, rising to 4–5 mg/day after a previously affected pregnancy, because neural tube closure is complete by day 28 — often before the pregnancy is known.

§ 07

Ascorbate and the fat-soluble four — and why only these can poison you

The ideaThe last five vitamins do not fit the pattern of the B group. Vitamin C is not really a coenzyme at all — it is a spare electron supply that keeps a metal ion in the right state inside certain enzymes, and the tissue that suffers most is the one that depends on those enzymes to build its scaffolding. The other four dissolve in fat, which changes everything about them: they need bile to be absorbed, they can be stockpiled for months, and two of them behave less like nutrients than like hormones, switching genes on and off. The same fat solubility that lets you store them is what lets them accumulate to poisonous levels — the body has no quick way to flush out something that will not dissolve in urine.

Scurvy, read off the chemistry

4-Hydroxyproline is what preorganises the proline ring so that three collagen chains can wind into a stable triple helix; without it the helix denatures at body temperature. Hydroxylysine does two further jobs — it carries the sugar attachments and it is the residue that lysyl oxidase later converts into covalent cross-links. So a vitamin C-deficient patient makes collagen, but it is unstable and uncross-linked, and it fails wherever mechanical load is highest and turnover fastest: the walls of small vessels, the periodontal ligament, healing wounds and the growth plate. Hence the picture — perifollicular haemorrhages with corkscrew hairs, swollen bleeding gums with loose teeth, easy bruising, poor wound healing and re-opening of old scars, and in children subperiosteal haemorrhage with painful pseudoparalysis of the legs. Anaemia is common, partly from bleeding and partly because ascorbate reduces dietary Fe3+ to the absorbable Fe2+.

Vitamin C hydroxylates; copper cross-links

The step that forms the covalent cross-links between collagen and elastin molecules is lysyl oxidase, which is a copper enzyme, not a vitamin C enzyme. That is why copper deficiency and Menkes disease produce vascular fragility and arterial aneurysms that superficially resemble scurvy, and why an option offering "vitamin C is required for collagen cross-linking" is subtly but definitely wrong — it is required for the hydroxylation that creates the residues cross-linking later uses. The same distinction sorts Ehlers–Danlos (defects in collagen genes or in lysyl hydroxylase itself) from scurvy: identical mechanics, entirely different lesion.

Two smaller vitamin C roles get asked. It is a cofactor for dopamine β-hydroxylase (a copper enzyme making noradrenaline) and for the two dioxygenases of carnitine synthesis — which is the usual explanation for the fatigue of early scurvy. And at gram doses it is not harmless: unabsorbed ascorbate causes osmotic diarrhoea, and its metabolite oxalate promotes calcium oxalate renal stones in susceptible people.

The fat-soluble four

Learn these as four different kinds of molecule rather than four vitamins, because their mechanisms have nothing in common. A and D are hormones acting on nuclear receptors. E is a radical scavenger. K is a classical cofactor for one reaction.

1972 — and C & S

Shortest first — factor VII (≈6 h) drives the PT/INR up early, protein C (≈8 h) opens the procoagulant window, prothrombin (≈60 h) is why real anticoagulation takes days. And note there is no hook for A, D, E, K and none is needed — the four letters already are one.

Warfarin: three deductions the paper likes

(1) Why the INR moves first. Warfarin does not touch existing clotting factors; it stops new ones being carboxylated. So the effect appears at the rate the old ones decay, and factor VII has the shortest half-life (≈6 h) — factor VII is measured by the extrinsic pathway, so the PT/INR rises long before full anticoagulation, which needs prothrombin (t½ ≈ 60 h) to fall, taking several days. (2) Why patients need heparin cover. Protein C is also vitamin K-dependent and has a half-life of only ≈8 h, so the first 24–48 h of warfarin are transiently procoagulant — the mechanism of warfarin-induced skin necrosis, particularly in protein C deficiency. (3) Why vitamin K reverses it and fresh frozen plasma reverses it faster. Vitamin K restores carboxylation but new factors take hours to synthesise; plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate supplies already-carboxylated factors immediately. Add the pharmacogenetics — VKORC1 and CYP2C9 variants explain much of the dose variation — and you have covered most of what can be asked.

Retinoic acid cannot restore vision

Retinol and retinal interconvert freely, but the oxidation of retinal to retinoic acid is irreversible. So retinoic acid supports every transcriptional function of vitamin A — epithelial differentiation, growth — while doing nothing at all for the retina, because it cannot be reduced back to 11-cis-retinal. A patient on isotretinoin still needs dietary vitamin A for vision. The related clinical hook worth knowing: all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) is curative in acute promyelocytic leukaemia, where the t(15;17) PML–RARα fusion blocks differentiation and pharmacological ATRA forces it to resume — with differentiation syndrome as the characteristic complication.

§ 08

Organ fuel logic: who eats what, and why they cannot swap

The ideaThere is no such thing as "metabolism" in the singular. Five organs run different subsets of the same pathways, because each one is missing a few enzymes, and those absences are not accidents — they are what gives each organ a job. Muscle cannot give its stored sugar back to the bloodstream, so its store is selfish. The liver can, so its store is public. Fat cells cannot recycle their own glycerol, so the glycerol has to travel. The brain cannot burn fat, so something else has to be delivered when sugar runs out. Learn the missing enzymes and the traffic between organs becomes obvious rather than something to memorise.

Definition & key facts

The rule that generates the rest
An organ can release free glucose into blood only if it has glucose-6-phosphatase. That is the liver, the kidney cortex, and the intestinal epithelium — and nothing else. Muscle, brain and adipose lack it, so once glucose is phosphorylated inside them it is committed.

Four "cannot" statements that are asked over and over

Muscle cannot raise blood glucose — no glucose-6-phosphatase, so McArdle disease (muscle phosphorylase deficiency) causes exercise intolerance with a flat lactate response but never hypoglycaemia, while von Gierke disease (glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency) causes severe fasting hypoglycaemia with lactic acidosis. Adipocytes cannot phosphorylate glycerol — so re-esterification depends on glucose uptake, which is why low insulin releases fatty acids twice over. The liver cannot burn ketone bodies — it lacks SCOT, which is precisely what forces it to export them. Animals cannot convert acetyl-CoA to glucose — the two carbons entering citrate are matched by two leaving as CO2, and we have no glyoxylate cycle. That last one is the reason the starvation arithmetic in § 09 comes out the way it does.

Who is allowed to put glucose back into the blood

Let Kids Indulge

Our own line, and it is the shortest thing in this note that generates a whole section. Liver, Kidney cortex, Intestinal epithelium — and indulge is the point of it: those three alone are allowed to hand free sugar back to the blood, because those three alone carry glucose-6-phosphatase and can strip the phosphate off glucose 6-phosphate. Everybody else — muscle, brain, adipose, red cell — commits the sugar the instant it is phosphorylated, because a phosphorylated sugar cannot cross a membrane and there is no enzyme to un-phosphorylate it. Nearly all of § 08 is a consequence of this rather than a second thing to memorise: muscle glycogen is private, so McArdle disease gives exercise intolerance and never hypoglycaemia, while von Gierke disease removes the export step itself, so glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis both stall and the trapped G6P spills into lactate, urate and triglyceride. The line does not cover the other two "cannot"s, and they are separate facts: adipose lacks glycerol kinase (so glycerol must travel to the liver), and the liver lacks SCOT (so it cannot burn the ketone bodies it exports). Three absences, three different stories.

The interorgan cycles

Three shuttles carry the traffic, and each solves a specific problem.

  • The Cori cycle. Muscle (or red cell) glycolysis makes lactate; the liver turns it back into glucose. The point is not energy — the cycle loses energy — it is that the liver has mitochondria and spare capacity while the working muscle does not, so the body exports the metabolic debt to an organ that can pay it.
  • The glucose–alanine cycle. The same carbon skeleton, but muscle transaminates pyruvate to alanine (ALT, PLP-dependent — § 05) first. Alanine therefore carries nitrogen and carbon together to the liver, where the amino group enters the urea cycle and the pyruvate enters gluconeogenesis. One vehicle, two deliveries.
  • Ketone body export. The liver converts fatty-acid-derived acetyl-CoA into a water-soluble, transportable fuel that crosses the blood–brain barrier. This is the only way fat energy ever reaches the brain.
Worked example 3 — the price of the Cori cycle

A sprinter's muscle converts one glucose to two lactate. The liver then converts those two lactate back to one glucose. Count the ATP on both sides and state the net cost to the body per turn — and say what the body buys with it.

  1. Muscle side. Anaerobic glycolysis: 2 ATP invested (hexokinase, PFK-1), 4 ATP produced (2 × phosphoglycerate kinase, 2 × pyruvate kinase). Net = +2 ATP. NADH from GAPDH is reoxidised by lactate dehydrogenase, so there is no mitochondrial contribution.
  2. Liver side. Gluconeogenesis from 2 pyruvate, counting high-energy phosphates:
    • 2 × pyruvate carboxylase = 2 ATP
    • 2 × PEP carboxykinase = 2 GTP
    • 2 × phosphoglycerate kinase (running backwards) = 2 ATP
    Total = 6 high-energy phosphates per glucose. (Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose-6-phosphatase are hydrolyses — they cost no ATP, they simply discard the phosphate's energy, which is why the pathway is not just glycolysis reversed.)
  3. Net. 6 spent − 2 gained = 4 ATP equivalents lost per turn.
  4. What that buys. The cycle is not futile. It transfers the energetic burden from a muscle that is oxygen-limited and working, to a liver that is well perfused and idle; it clears lactate that would otherwise acidify the muscle; and it keeps blood glucose available for the brain, which has no alternative. The liver funds the 6 ATP by oxidising fatty acids — so in effect the Cori cycle converts fat energy into glucose energy without converting fat into glucose.
  5. Compare the glucose–alanine cycle: identical carbon arithmetic, plus the cost of disposing of the amino group as urea (4 high-energy phosphates per urea, note 08). It is more expensive still, and the body pays it because the nitrogen has to travel somehow.

Glucokinase is a sensor, hexokinase is a servant

The temptation is to describe glucokinase as "the liver's hexokinase". The functional difference is the whole point. Hexokinase I is saturated at 5 mmol·L⁻¹ and switched off by its own product, so a brain cell takes a steady ration whatever the blood is doing. Glucokinase, with an S0.5 near 10 mmol·L⁻¹, mild sigmoidicity and no product inhibition, is running at well under a third of its maximum at normal glucose and speeds up steeply as portal glucose rises after a meal — so the liver's uptake is proportional to the excess. In the β-cell the same enzyme is the actual glucose sensor: its flux sets the ATP/ADP ratio that closes the KATP channel (§ 11). Inactivating GCK mutations therefore cause MODY2, a mild, stable hyperglycaemia that behaves like a resetting of the thermostat rather than a disease of insulin supply — which is why it usually needs no treatment. Activating mutations cause congenital hyperinsulinism.

§ 09

Fed, post-absorptive, fasting, starved: the timetable, with numbers

The ideaStop eating and the body works through its options in a fixed order, and the order is forced by arithmetic. The sugar store is tiny — gone in about fifteen hours. The fat store is enormous but almost none of it can be turned into sugar. The protein store is large but it is not a store at all, it is the machinery, and spending it is spending yourself. So the body's central problem in starvation is not finding energy — it has months of energy — but finding the one specific fuel the brain insists on. Everything that happens over the following weeks is a strategy for reducing that demand, and the strategy is ketone bodies.

Phase by phase

  1. Fed (0–4 h). Insulin high, glucagon low. Glucose enters muscle and adipose through recruited GLUT4; the liver stores glycogen and, if carbohydrate is abundant, runs de novo lipogenesis; dietary fat circulates as chylomicrons and is stripped by lipoprotein lipase. Protein synthesis exceeds breakdown. RQ approaches 1.0.
  2. Post-absorptive (4–16 h). The overnight state — this is what a "fasting glucose" measures. Insulin falls, glucagon rises, and hepatic glycogenolysis supplies most of the ≈200 g·day⁻¹ of glucose output. Adipose lipolysis begins, and muscle quietly switches to fatty acids.
  3. Early fasting (16 h – 2 days). Liver glycogen is gone by around 24 hours. Gluconeogenesis takes over completely, running on lactate (Cori), glycerol (from lipolysis) and alanine and glutamine (from muscle protein). Protein catabolism peaks at roughly 75 g/day — measured as ≈12 g of urinary nitrogen. Ketogenesis starts.
  4. Adapted starvation (beyond ~3 days to weeks). Ketone bodies rise from about 1–3 to 6–8 mmol·L⁻¹. The brain progressively switches to them, cutting its glucose requirement from 120 to about 40 g/day. Because less glucose is needed, less protein is broken down — nitrogen loss falls to about 4 g/day (≈25 g protein). Worked example 4 — the starvation ledger, and why ketone bodies are not optional

Take a 70 kg adult with 100 g of liver glycogen, 15 kg of adipose triacylglycerol and 6 kg of body protein, an obligate glucose demand of 160 g/day (brain 120, other anaerobic tissues 40) and a basal requirement of about 1800 kcal/day. (a) How long does liver glycogen last? (b) In early fasting about 160 g of triacylglycerol is mobilised daily — how much glucose can its glycerol backbone supply? Treat the fat as tripalmitin, M = 807 g·mol⁻¹; glucose M = 180 g·mol⁻¹. (c) If the shortfall were met entirely from protein, at roughly 0.57 g glucose per gram of protein, how long could that continue? (d) What does adaptation change?

  1. (a) 100 g ÷ 160 g·day⁻¹ = 0.63 day = 15 hours. That is the whole carbohydrate reserve of the body — one night's sleep and a late breakfast.
  2. (b) Moles of TAG mobilised: 160 ÷ 807 = 0.198 mol, giving 0.198 mol of glycerol. Two glycerol make one glucose, so 0.198 ÷ 2 = 0.099 mol glucose = 0.099 × 180 = 17.8 g.
  3. That is 17.8 ÷ 120 = 15 % of the brain's requirement alone. And the fatty acids themselves contribute nothing — acetyl-CoA cannot become glucose in animals (§ 08). To cover 120 g of glucose from glycerol you would have to hydrolyse (120/180) × 2 × 807 ≈ 1076 g of fat per day, releasing about 10 000 kcal — more than five times basal requirement. The body would cook itself. Fat cannot feed the brain as glucose.
  4. (c) Shortfall ≈ 160 − 18 = 142 g glucose·day⁻¹, needing 142 ÷ 0.57 ≈ 249 g of protein·day⁻¹ — but measured early-fasting catabolism is only about 75 g/day (≈43 g glucose), so even at peak proteolysis the demand cannot be met at 160 g/day. Something must give, and what gives is the demand. Note the cost anyway: at 75 g/day, losing a lethal third of 6 kg of protein takes 2000 ÷ 75 ≈ 27 days.
  5. (d) Ketoadaptation cuts the brain's glucose need from 120 to about 40 g/day, so total obligate glucose falls to ≈80 g/day. Now 18 g of glycerol plus ≈14 g of glucose from 25 g of protein plus recycled lactate can cover it. Protein loss falls to ≈25 g/day, and survival time becomes 2000 ÷ 25 = 80 days — a threefold extension, from about four weeks to nearly three months.
  6. That factor of three is the entire evolutionary purpose of ketone bodies. They do not supply energy the body lacks; they substitute for glucose in the one tissue that cannot burn fat, and in doing so they stop you from digesting your own muscles.

Exam technique · fuel-ledger arithmetic, and the two traps inside it

  1. Recognise the type — a stem that hands you grams of triacylglycerol and a molar mass is a glycerol question. The fatty acids are in the stem to tempt you, and in animals they contribute exactly nothing to glucose.
  2. One fixed chain, always the same direction — g TAG ÷ M = mol TAG = mol glycerol; ÷ 2; × 180 = g glucose. The ÷ 2 is the step people drop, because it takes two three-carbon units to build one hexose.
  3. Elimination without calculating: hunt for a 2 : 1 pair — if two options stand in exactly that ratio (36 g and 18 g, say), the larger one is the dropped ÷ 2 and the smaller is the answer. That single observation settles the question in ten seconds and you can move on.
  4. Elimination on principle — kill any option in which fatty acids or acetyl-CoA become glucose: two carbons enter citrate, two leave as CO2, and we have no glyoxylate cycle. Kill any option that makes ketone bodies gluconeogenic, for the same reason. Kill any option that has adipose tissue re-using its own glycerol — no glycerol kinase.
  5. Sanity-check against the day — obligate glucose is ≈160 g·day−1 unadapted and ≈80 g·day−1 adapted, the brain being 120 g falling to 40 g. Any answer in which glycerol covers the brain is out by an order of magnitude; the real figure is about 15 %.
  6. Where the time goes — these calculations deserve the full 66 seconds, because the arithmetic is short and every distractor is a near-miss of the same sum. The qualitative timetable (which fuel, which phase) should be answered in ten seconds off the phase names and the time banked here.

Two traps about ketone bodies

Acetone is not a fuel. It arises by spontaneous, non-enzymatic decarboxylation of acetoacetate, cannot be metabolised back, and is simply exhaled — hence the fruity breath. The two useful ketone bodies are acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate. And note the pedantic point the paper enjoys: β-hydroxybutyrate is not chemically a ketone at all, it is a secondary alcohol. Ketone bodies are not made from glucose and cannot make glucose. They are the acetyl-CoA overflow, and acetyl-CoA is a one-way street into the TCA cycle. A patient in deep ketosis still requires gluconeogenesis, which still requires protein.

§ 10

Insulin and glucagon: one switchboard, two directions

The ideaEverything in the previous section is executed by a single mechanism repeated over and over: a phosphate group is stuck onto an enzyme, or taken off it. One hormone puts phosphates on, the other takes them off, and the cell then behaves entirely differently — not because the enzymes changed, but because a small chemical tag did. The elegant part is that the same tag activates the enzyme that breaks glycogen down and inactivates the enzyme that builds it up. One signal, opposite effects, guaranteed never to run both directions at once. If you understand only one thing about hormonal control of metabolism, make it that.

The one-line summary

Phosphorylation switches the cell from storing to mobilising. So it activates the two lipases and glycogen phosphorylase, and inactivates almost everything synthetic. Glycogen phosphorylase is the enzyme people get wrong — it is activated by phosphorylation while every other "phosphorylase" instinct says otherwise. Learn it as the pair: phosphate on → phosphorylase ON, synthase OFF.

Three refinements the paper tests

  • Muscle has no glucagon receptor. Muscle glycogen is mobilised by adrenaline at β2-receptors — which uses the same cAMP/PKA arm — and by Ca2+ directly, because the δ subunit of phosphorylase kinase is calmodulin. So a contracting muscle mobilises its own glycogen without any hormone at all. Muscle phosphorylase b is also activated allosterically by AMP; the liver isoform is not, because the liver's job is to respond to the body's needs rather than its own.
  • Insulin does two separate things to cAMP-driven signalling. It activates PP1, which strips existing phosphates, and it activates PDE3B, which destroys cAMP so no new ones are added. Belt and braces — which is why insulin dominates when both hormones are present.
  • Allosteric control is faster and often decisive. Glucose itself binds liver phosphorylase a and exposes its phosphate to PP1 — so a rising portal glucose switches off glycogenolysis before insulin has arrived. Likewise pyruvate carboxylase absolutely requires acetyl-CoA, PFK-1 is inhibited by ATP and citrate, and PDH kinase reads acetyl-CoA and NADH directly. Hormones set the policy; allosteric effectors do the moment-to-moment work.

Exam technique · predicting what a phosphate does to any enzyme

  1. Ask one question: does this enzyme store, or spend? Phosphorylation always pushes towards spending. Every row of the table above obeys it without exception — including the one everybody calls an exception, since activating glycogen phosphorylase is spending. There is no mnemonic for that table and you do not need one; there is one rule and it is 8 for 8.
  2. Turn the rule into eliminations before you choose — an option saying a phosphate activates glycogen synthase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, HMG-CoA reductase or pyruvate dehydrogenase is dead on arrival; so is one saying it inactivates hormone-sensitive lipase or glycogen phosphorylase. On a five-option stem that is routinely two or three gone before any thinking.
  3. Check the tissue before you commit — muscle has no glucagon receptor, so any option in which glucagon acts on skeletal muscle is wrong whatever else it claims. Muscle answers to adrenaline at β2, and to Ca2+ directly through the calmodulin subunit of phosphorylase kinase.
  4. Check the isozyme if the stem names the heart — cardiac PFK-2 (PFKFB2) is phosphorylated by Akt at a different site and that activates the kinase, so in heart muscle insulin raises F2,6BP and accelerates glycolysis. Which direction a phosphate pushes is a property of the isozyme, not a law of nature, and a stem that specifies a tissue is usually specifying it for this reason.
  5. If the stem's timescale is seconds, answer allosterically — glucose binding liver phosphorylase a, AMP on muscle phosphorylase b, acetyl-CoA obligatory on pyruvate carboxylase, ATP and citrate on PFK-1, acetyl-CoA and NADH on PDH kinase. Hormonal options are the trap when the stem says "immediately", "during a sprint" or "within seconds"; hormones set policy, allostery does the minute.

Malonyl-CoA: the most examinable switch in lipid metabolism

Fatty-acid synthesis begins with acetyl-CoA carboxylase making malonyl-CoA, and malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-1, the carnitine shuttle that admits fatty acyl groups to the mitochondrion for β-oxidation. So the very first committed intermediate of synthesis is the signal that shuts off degradation. In the fed state insulin dephosphorylates and activates ACC (and citrate activates it allosterically), malonyl-CoA is high, CPT-1 is closed, fat is made. In fasting, glucagon and AMPK phosphorylate ACC, malonyl-CoA collapses, CPT-1 opens, fat is burned and ketogenesis follows. Given the weight the paper puts on lipid metabolism, being able to state this cycle in both directions is worth more than any single fact in this note.

§ 11

Diabetes, and the drugs the paper reaches for

The ideaDiabetes is not really a disease of sugar. It is what happens when the switchboard of the last section is stuck in the fasting position while food is abundant — the cells behave as though they are starving, in the middle of plenty. Once you read it that way, everything follows: the liver keeps making glucose it does not need, fat keeps being mobilised, ketones are produced as if in famine, and the excess sugar starts sticking chemically to proteins because there is simply too much of it about. Reading diabetic ketoacidosis off § 09 and § 10

Nothing in DKA is new chemistry. It is the starvation programme of § 09 running with the brakes cut:

  • No insulin → GLUT4 stays inside the cell → muscle and adipose cannot take glucose up, so plasma glucose rises even as the tissues starve.
  • No insulin, plus high glucagon → PKA phosphorylates the switchboard → glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis run at full rate, adding glucose the body cannot use.
  • Unrestrained HSL → free fatty acids flood the liver; ACC is phosphorylated so malonyl-CoA collapses, CPT-1 opens, β-oxidation is maximal and ketogenesis is unbraked.
  • Glucose above the renal threshold (≈10 mmol·L⁻¹) → osmotic diuresis → profound water and electrolyte loss, which concentrates everything further. The patient is typically 5–7 litres down.
  • Acidosis → hyperventilation (Kussmaul respiration), and a shift of K+ out of cells. Worked example 5 — a ketoacidosis panel, worked from first principles

A 19-year-old presents with vomiting and deep sighing respiration. Na+ 128, K+ 5.6, Cl− 92, HCO3− 6 mmol·L−1; glucose 45 mmol·L−1. Compute (a) the anion gap, (b) the corrected sodium, (c) the expected pCO2, (d) the effective osmolality — and (e) explain why her urine dipstick for ketones may look worse after treatment starts.

  1. (a) Anion gap. 128 − (92 + 6) = 30 mmol·L⁻¹ against a normal of 8–12 — a large high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. The unmeasured anions are acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate. Note that the gap has risen by 18 while bicarbonate has fallen by 18, a ratio of 1.0, which says this is a pure anion-gap acidosis with no additional hyperchloraemic component.
  2. (b) Corrected sodium. 128 + 1.6 × (45 − 5.6)/5.6 = 128 + 1.6 × 7.04 = 128 + 11.3 = 139 mmol·L⁻¹. So the apparent hyponatraemia is entirely dilutional — glucose trapped in the extracellular space has drawn water out of cells. Her true sodium is normal, and it will rise as glucose falls, which is why sodium must be watched during treatment rather than corrected at presentation.
  3. (c) Winter's formula. 1.5 × 6 + 8 = 17 ± 2 mmHg. If her measured pCO2 is around 17, respiratory compensation is appropriate and there is no second acid–base disorder. A pCO2 of 30 would signal a superimposed respiratory acidosis — an exhausted patient about to arrest.
  4. (d) Effective osmolality. 2 × 128 + 45 = 301 mOsm·kg⁻¹. Below the 320 threshold that defines the hyperosmolar state, so this is DKA rather than HHS — consistent with her age and the size of the anion gap.
  5. (e) The dipstick paradox. The nitroprusside reagent detects acetoacetate (and weakly acetone) but not β-hydroxybutyrate. In severe DKA the mitochondrial NADH/NAD+ ratio is high, so the equilibrium sits far towards β-hydroxybutyrate — the dipstick therefore underestimates the ketosis on arrival. As treatment restores the redox state, β-hydroxybutyrate is reoxidised to acetoacetate and the dipstick gets stronger while the patient is getting better. Measure serum β-hydroxybutyrate, follow the anion gap and the bicarbonate, and ignore the dipstick trend. The same trap applies with alcoholic ketoacidosis, where the redox shift is even more extreme.

Chronic hyperglycaemia: four ways sugar damages tissue

All four are consequences of glucose being present in concentrations the chemistry was never designed for, and all four preferentially hit the tissues that take glucose up insulin-independently — which is precisely why the complications are retinal, renal, neural and vascular.

  1. Non-enzymatic glycation. Glucose's open-chain aldehyde forms a Schiff base with free amino groups, which rearranges to a stable Amadori product and eventually to advanced glycation end-products that cross-link collagen and engage the RAGE receptor. HbA1c is the measurable, benign version of this reaction.
  2. The polyol pathway. Aldose reductase reduces glucose to sorbitol using NADPH; sorbitol dehydrogenase then oxidises it to fructose using NAD+. Tissues rich in aldose reductase but poor in sorbitol dehydrogenase — lens, retina, kidney, Schwann cells — accumulate sorbitol, which is osmotically active and membrane-impermeant, drawing in water. Cataract and neuropathy follow. And because NADPH is consumed, glutathione regeneration suffers, adding oxidative injury. The same chemistry explains the infantile cataracts of galactokinase deficiency, where galactose becomes galactitol.
  3. Protein kinase C activation via de novo diacylglycerol synthesis, altering vascular permeability and blood flow.
  4. The hexosamine pathway, shunting fructose-6-phosphate to UDP-GlcNAc and modifying transcription factors.

When HbA1c lies

HbA1c measures glycation integrated over a red cell's life, so anything that changes that lifespan corrupts it. Falsely low: haemolysis, recent bleeding, recent transfusion, pregnancy, erythropoietin therapy — all shorten average red cell age. Falsely high: iron deficiency anaemia, B12 or folate deficiency, splenectomy — all lengthen it. Haemoglobin variants can interfere in either direction depending on the assay. In any of these settings, use fructosamine or continuous glucose monitoring instead. This is a favourite stem because it requires you to know why the test works, not just what it is.

The statin question, and how it is usually got wrong

"How does a statin lower plasma LDL?" The reflex answer — "by blocking cholesterol synthesis" — is only the first half, and on a five-option stem it will be an available and wrong choice. The liver makes only part of the body's cholesterol, and simply making less would not clear the LDL already circulating. What lowers plasma LDL is the compensatory upregulation of the hepatic LDL receptor when intracellular sterol falls and SREBP-2 is released. That is why statins are far less effective in homozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia, where there are no functional receptors to upregulate — and why PCSK9 inhibitors, which stop the receptor being degraded in the lysosome so it recycles instead, work through the same final pathway from a different angle.

Go deeper · the rest of the lipid-lowering armoury, by mechanism
  • Ezetimibe — blocks NPC1L1 on the enterocyte brush border, cutting intestinal cholesterol absorption (dietary and biliary). Hepatic sterol falls, SREBP-2 rises, LDL receptors rise: same endpoint as a statin by a different route, which is why the combination is more than additive.
  • Bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine) — bind bile acids in the gut so they are excreted rather than recycled. The liver must make more bile acids from cholesterol, depleting hepatic sterol and again upregulating LDL receptors. Raise triglycerides, and interfere with the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (§ 07).
  • PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab, alirocumab; inclisiran is a siRNA against PCSK9 mRNA) — PCSK9 binds the LDL receptor and routes it to lysosomal degradation instead of recycling. Block PCSK9 and each receptor makes many more trips. Loss-of-function PCSK9 variants give lifelong low LDL and low cardiovascular risk, which is how the target was found.
  • Bempedoic acid — inhibits ATP-citrate lyase, the enzyme that supplies the cytosolic acetyl-CoA the whole mevalonate pathway is built from, so it acts upstream of HMG-CoA reductase. It is a prodrug activated by an enzyme expressed in liver but not muscle, which is the whole point: statin-level LDL reduction without the myopathy.
  • Fibrates — PPARα agonists; induce lipoprotein lipase and apolipoprotein A-I and suppress apoC-III, so they are triglyceride drugs rather than LDL drugs. Combining a fibrate with a statin raises myopathy risk (gemfibrozil worst).
  • Omega-3 ethyl esters — reduce hepatic VLDL secretion; triglyceride-lowering.

Notice the pattern: four of these six work by manipulating one variable — how much cholesterol is inside the hepatocyte — because that variable, through SREBP-2, sets LDL receptor density, and LDL receptor density sets plasma LDL. Reasoning from that single control point rather than memorising six mechanisms is what makes the class answerable under time pressure.
