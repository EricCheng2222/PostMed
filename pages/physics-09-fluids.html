Physics · Note 09

Fluid Statics and Dynamics

A fluid at rest cannot support a shear stress. That single fact forces everything in the static half of this chapter: pressure acts equally in all directions, it depends on depth and nothing else, and buoyancy is just the depth-dependence of pressure applied to a submerged shape. Once the fluid moves, the story splits cleanly in two — Bernoulli redistributes a fixed amount of energy between pressure and speed, and Poiseuille describes the energy that viscosity destroys. Almost every clinical fluid fact you will be asked about is one of those two, and the commonest exam mistake is using the wrong one.

§ 01

Density and specific gravity

The ideaSome things feel heavy for their size and others feel light. Density is that everyday comparison made exact: how much material is packed into a given amount of room. It matters here because gravity pulls on how much material there is, while pipes, containers and bodies are described by how much room they take up — density is the exchange rate between the two, and almost every result on this page needs it. Specific gravity is the same comparison made against water, so that "twice as heavy as water" or "a tenth as heavy" becomes a single plain number.

Definition & formula

Density (ρ)
The mass contained in unit volume of a substance — an intensive property, independent of how much of the substance you have.
ρ = m / V uniform substance; for a mixture ρ is the volume-weighted mean
Full definition, symbols and units
Specific gravity (relative density)
The ratio of a substance's density to the density of water at 4 °C. A pure number with no unit.
SG = ρ / ρwater  (ρwater = 1.000 × 10³ kg·m⁻³)dimensionless; water at 4 °C is the reference
1 g·cm⁻³ = 1 × 10³ kg·m⁻³ = 1 kg·L⁻¹the conversion students get wrong most often
ρdensity (kg·m⁻³)
mmass (kg)
Vvolume (m³)
SGspecific gravity (dimensionless)

Density is the bridge between the two things you can actually measure about a fluid — how much space it occupies and how heavy it is. Every formula in this chapter that involves gravity contains ρ, because gravity acts on mass while geometry is written in volume, and ρ is the exchange rate.

Specific gravity exists because the conversion is so convenient: since water is 1.000 × 10³ kg·m⁻³ by definition of the reference, SG and density in g·cm⁻³ are numerically identical. Blood at SG 1.06 means ρ = 1.06 g·cm⁻³ = 1060 kg·m⁻³. Clinically, urine specific gravity (normal range 1.003–1.035) is a bedside index of renal concentrating ability, read directly off a floating urinometer — a hydrometer, which is itself an application of Archimedes' principle (§5).

§ 02

Pressure, its units, and Pascal's principle

The ideaPressure is not how hard something pushes, but how hard it pushes for each bit of surface it pushes on. The same shove concentrated on a pinpoint bites; spread over a whole palm it is barely noticed. A liquid that is sealed in has a further property worth pausing on: squeeze it anywhere and every part of it, and every part of its container, feels that extra squeeze immediately and in full. Press gently on a small piston and the same pressure, acting over a much larger piston somewhere else, becomes a force large enough to lift a car — which is the entire idea behind a garage jack. The rest of the section is bookkeeping about the many different units people use to quote a pressure.

Definition & formula

Pressure (p)
The magnitude of the force exerted perpendicular to a surface, per unit area of that surface. Pressure is a scalar; the force it produces is a vector, always normal to the surface.
p = F⟂ / A   [1 Pa = 1 N·m⁻²] force must be the component perpendicular to A
Full definition, symbols and units
Pascal's principle
A pressure change applied anywhere to an enclosed incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every point in the fluid and to the walls of the container.
F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂  ⟹  F₂ = F₁ (A₂/A₁)hydraulic lift: enclosed, incompressible, same height
A₁ d₁ = A₂ d₂  ⟹  F₁ d₁ = F₂ d₂volume conservation ⟹ work is not multiplied
ppressure (Pa = N·m⁻²)
F⟂normal force (N)
Aarea (m²)
dpiston displacement (m)

Because a fluid at rest cannot sustain a shear, the only force it can exert on a surface is a normal force — and the magnitude of that force per unit area is the same in every direction at a given point. That isotropy is what makes pressure a scalar and what makes Pascal's principle work: squeeze an enclosed fluid anywhere and the whole fluid registers the increase equally.

Exam point · the conversion chain to memorise

1 atm = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa = 1.013 bar = 760 mmHg = 1033 cmH₂O = 14.70 psi.
Working backwards from these: 1 mmHg ≈ 133 Pa and 1 mmHg = 1.36 cmH₂O. A systolic pressure of 120 mmHg is 120 × 133 = 1.60 × 10⁴ Pa = 16.0 kPa. Any question that mixes mmHg with Pa is testing exactly this step.

Where does 760 mmHg come from? It is not arbitrary: it is the height of a mercury column whose weight per unit area equals atmospheric pressure, h = p/(ρHgg) = 101 325 / (13 600 × 9.8) = 0.760 m (§4). Mercury is used because 13.6 × denser than water makes the column 13.6 × shorter — a water barometer would need to be 10.3 m tall.

Atmospheric pressure is large but usually invisible because it acts on both sides of everything. Over a body surface of ≈ 1.8 m² the atmosphere presses with F = pA = 1.013 × 10⁵ × 1.8 ≈ 1.8 × 10⁵ N — the weight of about 19 tonnes. We are not crushed because the pressure inside our tissues and cavities is essentially the same, so the net force is zero. This is why gauge pressure (§4), not absolute pressure, is the clinically useful quantity.

Worked example 1 · Hydraulic lift

A garage lift has an input piston of radius 2.0 cm and an output piston of radius 16 cm, connected by enclosed oil. A 1200 kg car sits on the output piston. Find (a) the force needed on the input piston, (b) the gauge pressure in the oil, (c) how far the input piston must move to raise the car by 10 cm. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Area ratio. Areas go as radius squared, so A₂/A₁ = (16/2.0)² = 64. The individual areas are A₁ = π(0.020)² = 1.257 × 10⁻³ m² and A₂ = π(0.160)² = 8.042 × 10⁻² m².
  2. Load. F₂ = mg = 1200 × 9.8 = 1.176 × 10⁴ N.
  3. Input force. Pascal gives the same pressure on both pistons, so F₁ = F₂ (A₁/A₂) = 1.176 × 10⁴ / 64 = 183.75 N ≈ 1.8 × 10² N — about the weight of a 19 kg mass.
  4. Pressure. p = F₂/A₂ = 1.176 × 10⁴ / 8.042 × 10⁻² = 1.46 × 10⁵ Pa (≈ 1.4 atm gauge). Check with the small piston: 183.75 / 1.257 × 10⁻³ = 1.46 × 10⁵ Pa. ✓
  5. Distance. The oil is incompressible, so the swept volumes match: A₁d₁ = A₂d₂ ⟹ d₁ = d₂(A₂/A₁) = 0.10 × 64 = 6.4 m.
  6. Energy check. Input work 183.75 × 6.4 = 1176 J; output work 1.176 × 10⁴ × 0.10 = 1176 J. Identical — as they must be.

F₁ = 1.8 × 10² N · p = 1.46 × 10⁵ Pa · d₁ = 6.4 m · W = 1176 J both sides

Trap · the hydraulic lift multiplies force, never energy

The force gain of 64 is paid for by a distance loss of 64. Any exam option claiming a hydraulic press "creates energy" or has efficiency above 1 is wrong by conservation of energy — and in a real machine friction makes the output work slightly less. The lever, the pulley and the hydraulic press are the same bargain in three costumes.

§ 03

Hydrostatic pressure and the hydrostatic paradox

The ideaGo deeper under water and the squeeze on you grows, simply because there is more water stacked overhead. The surprising part is how little else counts: only how far down you are. Not the shape of the container, not how wide it is, not how many litres it holds — a thin pipe of water and a whole lake press just as hard at the same depth. That single fact is why a dam is built thick at the bottom and thin at the top, why your ears hurt at the deep end of a pool, and why a drip bag is hung high above the patient rather than beside them.

Definition & formula

Hydrostatic pressure
The pressure at a point in a fluid at rest, equal to the pressure at the free surface plus the weight per unit area of the fluid column vertically above that point.
p = p₀ + ρ g h fluid at rest, uniform ρ, h measured vertically downward from the free surface
Full definition, symbols and units
Hydrostatic paradox
The observation that the pressure — and therefore the force — on the base of a vessel is fixed by the depth and the base area alone, and is independent of the shape of the vessel and of the total weight of fluid it holds.
Δp = ρ g Δhpressure difference between any two depths in one connected fluid
p(A) = p(B) if A and B are at the same heightonly if A and B are joined by a continuous path through the same fluid
pabsolute pressure at depth h (Pa)
p₀pressure at the free surface, usually patm (Pa)
ρfluid density (kg·m⁻³)
ggravitational field strength, 9.8 (m·s⁻²)
hvertical depth below the surface (m)

The derivation takes one line and is worth doing once, because it shows why shape is irrelevant. Isolate an imaginary vertical cylinder of fluid of cross-section A stretching from the surface down to depth h. It is in equilibrium, so the upward force on its base balances the downward force on its top plus its own weight:

pA = p₀A + (ρ A h) g  ⟹  p = p₀ + ρghA cancels — depth is the only variable

Notice what never entered: the width of the vessel, the shape of its walls, the fluid sitting sideways beyond the cylinder. Only the vertical distance matters, because only the vertical direction has a gravitational force to balance. And any two points at the same level in one connected body of fluid must be at the same pressure — if they were not, the pressure difference would drive a horizontal flow, contradicting "at rest".

Worked example 2 · A cone and a cylinder that disagree by a factor of three

Two open vessels stand on a bench. Both have a flat circular base of area A = 0.010 m² and both are filled with water (ρ = 1000 kg·m⁻³) to a depth h = 0.30 m. Vessel A is a straight cylinder; vessel B is a cone standing on its base, tapering to a point at the water surface. Compare the gauge pressure at the base, the downward force on the base, and the weight of water held.

  1. Gauge pressure — identical. pgauge = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 0.30 = 2940 Pa in both.
  2. Force on the base — identical. F = pA = 2940 × 0.010 = 29.4 N in both.
  3. Water held — different. Cylinder: V = Ah = 3.0 × 10⁻³ m³ ⟹ m = 3.0 kg ⟹ W = 29.4 N. Cone: V = ⅓Ah = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³ ⟹ m = 1.0 kg ⟹ W = 9.8 N.
  4. Resolve the apparent contradiction. In the cone the base feels 29.4 N but the water only weighs 9.8 N. The missing 19.6 N comes from the sloping walls: the fluid pushes outward and upward on them, so by Newton's third law they push inward and downward on the fluid. Add the wall reaction to the weight and the base force balances exactly.

p = 2940 Pa and F = 29.4 N in both · water weight 29.4 N vs 9.8 N

Trap · three ways ρgh goes wrong

  • h is vertical. In a tube inclined at 30°, a 2.0 m length of tube is only 1.0 m of depth. Pressure does not care about path length, only about height.
  • Dropping p₀ silently converts absolute to gauge. "p = ρgh" is the gauge pressure. If a question asks for absolute pressure at 10 m depth in a lake, you must add 1 atm.
  • Two immiscible fluids do not share one ρ. With oil on water you must add the columns separately: p = p₀ + ρoilghoil + ρwghw.

The 10-metre rule

Water adds one atmosphere every 10.3 m of depth (101 325 / (1000 × 9.8) = 10.3 m). So at 10 m under water the absolute pressure is ≈ 2 atm, at 20 m ≈ 3 atm. This is why a breath-hold diver's lung volume roughly halves at 10 m, and why ascending while holding your breath ruptures alveoli.

§ 04

Gauge vs absolute: manometer, barometer, blood pressure

The ideaEvery pressure reading has to be counted from somewhere, and hospital instruments count from the air in the room rather than from nothing at all. So a reading of zero means "the same as the air around us", and a reading below zero is not impossible or mysterious — it just means slightly less than the surrounding air, the way a straw does when you suck on it. The second half of the section is the reminder that a standing body is itself a tall column of liquid: blood down at the ankles is under far more pressure than blood up in the head, purely because of the height between them, which is why the arm has to be level with the heart when a cuff reading is taken.

Definition & formula

Absolute pressure
Pressure measured from a true vacuum as zero. It can never be negative.
pabs = patm + pgauge always true; every clinical pressure is quoted as gauge
Full definition, symbols and units
Gauge pressure
Pressure measured from the local atmospheric pressure as zero — the excess over atmosphere. It is negative whenever the pressure is sub-atmospheric (a "suction").
Manometer / barometer
A manometer reads a pressure difference as the height of a liquid column it can support; a barometer is a manometer whose reference side is an evacuated space, so it reads absolute pressure.

A tyre "at 2 bar" contains gas at 3 bar absolute. A pleural pressure of "−5 cmH₂O" is not a negative absolute pressure — it is 5 cmH₂O below atmosphere, i.e. about 100 830 Pa absolute. Every pressure a clinician says out loud is a gauge pressure, and the reference is whatever atmosphere the patient is breathing.

Why blood pressure is quoted in millimetres of mercury

The unit is a fossil of the instrument. Riva-Rocci's sphygmomanometer inflated an arm cuff with air and connected it to a mercury manometer; the cuff pressure was read directly as the height of the mercury column it supported, so "120" literally meant 120 mm of mercury. Mercury was chosen because ρ = 13 600 kg·m⁻³ keeps the instrument on a desk rather than three storeys tall. Modern devices are aneroid or oscillometric and contain no mercury at all, but every diagnostic threshold in cardiology — 140/90, 180/120, a MAP of 65 — is defined in mmHg, so the unit survives. In SI: 120/80 mmHg = 16.0 / 10.7 kPa.

What the cuff actually measures is a balance point. Inflate above systolic and the brachial artery is held shut: no flow, no sound. Deflate, and the instant cuff pressure falls just below peak systolic pressure a jet of blood squirts through a slit-like opening — fast, and therefore turbulent (§9) — and the vessel wall vibrates audibly: the first Korotkoff sound, read as SBP. As the cuff falls further the artery stays open longer in each cycle until, below diastolic pressure, it never closes, flow becomes laminar again and the sounds disappear: DBP. Korotkoff sounds are turbulence, which is why an auscultatory gap or a very stiff artery corrupts the reading.

Exam point · cuff geometry and arm position are physics, not nursing detail

  • A cuff that is too narrow or too short for the arm needs a higher pressure to transmit the same pressure to the artery, so it over-reads. Too wide → under-reads.
  • The arm must be at heart level. Every centimetre of vertical offset adds or subtracts ρgh: 1060 × 9.8 × 0.010 = 104 Pa = 0.78 mmHg per cm, so an arm 10 cm below the heart over-reads by ≈ 7.8 mmHg — enough to reclassify a patient as hypertensive.

The hydrostatic column inside you

The circulation is a fluid-filled system in a gravitational field, so p = p₀ + ρgh applies inside it just as it does in a beaker. When you are supine, every large vessel is at roughly one level and the hydrostatic term vanishes. Stand up and it reappears with a vengeance.

Worked example 3 · Arterial pressure at the head and at the feet

A standing adult has MAP = 93 mmHg measured at heart level. The brain (mid-cerebral level) is 40 cm above the heart and the feet are 130 cm below it. Blood density is 1060 kg·m⁻³. Find MAP at the head and at the feet.

  1. Pressure per metre of blood. ρg = 1060 × 9.8 = 1.039 × 10⁴ Pa·m⁻¹, i.e. 1.039 × 10⁴ / 133.3 = 77.9 mmHg per metre of vertical column.
  2. Head (40 cm above). Δp = −1060 × 9.8 × 0.40 = −4155 Pa = −31.2 mmHg. MAPhead = 93 − 31 = 62 mmHg.
  3. Feet (130 cm below). Δp = +1060 × 9.8 × 1.30 = +13 504 Pa = +101 mmHg. MAPfeet = 93 + 101 = 194 mmHg.
  4. Interpretation. Cerebral perfusion is the fragile end: a further 20 mmHg fall (a rapid stand, a vasovagal episode, hypovolaemia) takes the head below the autoregulatory range and causes syncope — which conveniently restores the horizontal posture that abolishes the hydrostatic term. At the ankle, about 1.1 m below the heart, standing perfectly still lets the venous pressure climb to 5 + 86 ≈ 90 mmHg, and that is the pressure driving capillary filtration into the tissues: dependent oedema and varicose veins are ρgh problems.

MAP ≈ 62 mmHg at the head, ≈ 194 mmHg at the feet (heart-level MAP 93 mmHg)

Trap · gravity does not make the heart work harder in a closed loop

Every mmHg the blood gains falling down the arteries to the foot is given back climbing the veins to the heart. In a rigid closed circuit gravity therefore does no net work over a circuit. The real consequences of standing are different ones: veins are compliant, so the raised pressure distends them and pools ≈ 500 mL in the legs, cutting venous return; and the raised capillary hydrostatic pressure drives filtration (Starling) into the interstitium. That is why the fix is the calf muscle pump and venous valves, not a stronger heart. A giraffe, needing ≈ 60 mmHg at a brain 2 m above its heart, needs 60 + 1060 × 9.8 × 2.0 / 133.3 ≈ 216 mmHg at heart level — and it has one.

§ 05

Archimedes' principle and flotation

The ideaPut something into water and the water pushes back up on it, which is why a heavy stone feels lighter once it is under the surface. The size of that upward push is not mysterious: it equals the weight of the water the object shoved out of the way to make room for itself. So anything that can shove aside more than its own weight before it is fully under will float, and anything that cannot will sink. Nothing new is needed to explain the push — water lower down presses harder than water higher up, so the upward press on an object's underside beats the downward press on its top. This is why a steel ship floats, why you feel weightless in a pool, and why the brain, sitting in its own fluid, is not crushed by its own weight.

Definition & formula

Buoyant force (FB)
The net upward force a fluid exerts on a body immersed in it, equal in magnitude to the weight of the fluid the body displaces, and acting through the centre of the displaced volume.
FB = ρfluid Vdisplaced g fluid at rest; V is the submerged volume, not the total volume
Full definition, symbols and units
Apparent weight
The reading of a scale supporting a submerged body: its true weight minus the buoyant force.
Vsub / Vtotal = ρobject / ρfluidfreely floating body, in equilibrium, ρobj < ρfluid
Wapparent = W − FB = (ρobj − ρfluid) V gfully submerged body
FBbuoyant force (N)
ρfluiddensity of the fluid (kg·m⁻³)
Vsubsubmerged volume of the body (m³)
Wtrue weight, mg (N)

Archimedes' principle is not an extra law — it is p = p₀ + ρgh applied to a shape. Take a submerged block of height H and horizontal area A with its top at depth h. The downward force on the top is (p₀ + ρgh)A; the upward force on the bottom is (p₀ + ρg(h+H))A, which is larger. The sideways forces cancel. The difference is

FB = ρ g H A = ρ g V= the weight of the displaced fluid, for any shape

Three consequences follow immediately and are examined constantly. First, FB does not depend on the density of the object — a lead ball and a wooden ball of equal volume feel the same buoyant force in the same fluid, they merely respond to it differently. Second, in a fluid of uniform density FB does not depend on depth, because both faces gain the same p₀ + ρg·(extra depth). Third, an object floats when its average density is less than the fluid's, and then it sinks exactly far enough for the displaced weight to equal its own weight.

That last statement gives the flotation rule directly. At equilibrium ρobj Vtotal g = ρfluid Vsub g, so Vsub/Vtotal = ρobj/ρfluid. Ice (917 kg·m⁻³) in seawater (1025 kg·m⁻³) floats with 917/1025 = 89.5% of its volume below the surface — the iceberg cliché, now a one-line calculation.

Worked example 4 · Body density by hydrostatic weighing

A 70.0 kg subject is fully submerged in fresh water (ρ = 1000 kg·m⁻³) after a maximal expiration, and the underwater scale reads an apparent weight of 30.0 N. Neglecting residual lung gas, find the subject's volume and mean body density.

  1. True weight. W = mg = 70.0 × 9.8 = 686 N.
  2. Buoyant force. FB = W − Wapp = 686 − 30.0 = 656 N.
  3. Displaced volume = body volume (fully submerged): V = FB/(ρwg) = 656/(1000 × 9.8) = 6.694 × 10⁻² m³ = 66.9 L.
  4. Body density. ρ = m/V = 70.0 / 6.694 × 10⁻² = 1046 kg·m⁻³.
  5. Reading it. Fat is ≈ 900 kg·m⁻³ and fat-free mass ≈ 1100 kg·m⁻³, so a whole-body density of 1046 sits between them: the body-fat percentage follows from a two-compartment formula such as Siri's. This is the reference method that DEXA and bio-impedance are validated against.

V = 66.9 L · ρbody ≈ 1.05 × 10³ kg·m⁻³

Exam point · buoyancy in the body

The brain (ρ ≈ 1040 kg·m⁻³, mass ≈ 1400 g) is buoyed up by CSF (ρ ≈ 1007 kg·m⁻³) — it is denser than CSF, so it does not literally float, but almost all of its weight is carried by buoyancy. Its apparent weight is reduced in the ratio (ρbrain − ρCSF)/ρbrain = 33/1040 = 0.032, i.e. it weighs the equivalent of only about 45 g on its supporting structures instead of 1400 g. Remove CSF too quickly (over-drainage of a shunt, a large-volume lumbar puncture) and that support is lost — traction on bridging veins and cranial nerves gives the classic post-LP headache. The same principle makes aquatic rehabilitation possible: immersion to the neck removes ≈ 90% of the load from the joints.

Trap · displaced volume vs displaced weight

A submerged body displaces its whole volume, and FB may be less than its weight (it sinks). A floating body displaces only its submerged part, but that part weighs exactly as much as the whole body. Classic question: a boat carrying a rock floats in a pond; throw the rock overboard — does the water level rise, fall, or stay the same? It falls: in the boat the rock displaced its own weight of water; on the bottom it displaces only its own (smaller) volume.

§ 06

Continuity: the equation that conserves volume

The ideaLiquid has to go somewhere. If a tube is not leaking and is not filling up, then whatever goes in each second must come out each second — so wherever the tube is narrow the liquid has to hurry through, and wherever it is wide it can dawdle. Squeezing the end of a garden hose is the everyday demonstration. This is pure bookkeeping rather than a law about forces, but stating it carefully pays off twice: it explains why blood races through a narrowed artery, and why blood crawls through the capillaries, which are individually tiny but so numerous side by side that together they are the widest part of the whole circulation.

§ 07

Bernoulli's equation — and what it does not explain

The ideaA moving fluid carries a fixed budget of energy, and it can hold that energy either as pressure or as speed. Spend on one and there is less of the other. So where a fluid is forced to run fast — through a narrow throat, out of a nozzle — its sideways push on the walls actually drops, and when it slows down again in the wide part beyond, most of that push comes back. This is the idea behind a scan that turns the measured speed of a jet through a heart valve into the pressure the heart must be generating. What it deliberately leaves out is friction, so it can never explain a steady, permanent loss of pressure along a pipe — that is the next section's job.

Every term in Bernoulli's equation has units of J·m⁻³, which is the fastest way to remember what the equation is: energy per unit volume. Push a parcel of fluid from a wide section into a narrow one and continuity forces it to accelerate. The work that accelerates it can only come from the pressure behind exceeding the pressure ahead. So the pressure in the narrow section must be lower — not because narrow tubes "suck", but because kinetic energy has been bought with pressure energy.

Exam point · quote the four assumptions before you use it

  1. Steady flow — the velocity at each point does not change with time. (Pulsatile arterial flow strictly violates this.)
  2. Incompressible — ρ constant. Fine for liquids; fine for gases well below the speed of sound.
  3. Non-viscous — no internal friction, so no energy is dissipated. This is the one that fails in the circulation.
  4. Along a single streamline — and, if you want to compare any two points in the field, the flow must also be irrotational.
Worked example 5 · Speed and pressure through an arterial stenosis

Blood (ρ = 1060 kg·m⁻³) flows at a mean 0.30 m·s⁻¹ through an artery of internal radius 5.0 mm. An atheromatous plaque reduces the radius to 2.5 mm over a short segment.   2. Bernoulli, horizontal. p₁ − p₂ = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) = 0.5 × 1060 × (1.44 − 0.09) = 530 × 1.35 = 715 Pa.
  3. Convert. 715 / 133.3 = 5.4 mmHg — the static pressure inside the throat is about 5 mmHg below that of the neighbouring wide segments.
  4. Reality check. 5 mmHg is small compared with the tens of mmHg a tight stenosis actually costs. Aneurysm and stenosis, done correctly

These two are the classic application, and they are also where sloppy reasoning is punished. Work through them in the order continuity → Bernoulli → Laplace, and state which effect dominates.

Stenosis (local narrowing)

  • Continuity: A falls ⟹ v rises as 1/r².
  • Bernoulli: the rise in ½ρv² is paid for out of static pressure, so pressure dips in the throat — a genuine local minimum.
  • Downstream: the vessel re-widens, v falls again and pressure partially recovers — but only partially, because energy has been lost.
  • Poiseuille: the sustained pressure drop across the lesion is a resistance effect, R ∝ 1/r⁴, and it is the dominant term for anything but the shortest lesion.
  • Reynolds: at fixed Q, Re ∝ 1/r, so a narrowing raises Re; past the critical value the jet becomes turbulent and audible as a bruit.
  • Clinically: a very low throat pressure can even collapse a compliant vessel, limiting flow however hard the upstream pressure is raised.

Aneurysm (local widening)

  • Continuity: A rises ⟹ v falls in the sac.
  • Bernoulli: lower v ⟹ static pressure rises slightly. (Small — a few mmHg — so do not build the whole explanation on it.)
  • Laplace is the real story: for a thin-walled tube the circumferential wall tension is T = Δp · r and the wall stress is σ = Δp·r/t. A larger r raises T, the wall stretches and thins, r grows further, T grows again — a positive feedback that ends in rupture. This is why rupture risk scales steeply with diameter and why repair thresholds are diameter-based.
  • Turbulence here is geometric, not a pipe-Re effect: at fixed Q a wider tube actually has a lower Re. The eddies come from flow separation at the abrupt expansion, and the resulting stasis and recirculation promote mural thrombus.

Trap · do not use Bernoulli to explain the pressure fall from aorta to capillary

Mean pressure drops from ≈ 95 mmHg in the aorta to ≈ 15 mmHg at the venous end of the capillaries. Bernoulli cannot be the cause: total cross-sectional area increases downstream, so blood slows, and an ideal fluid slowing down would show a pressure rise. The observed fall is entirely viscous dissipation — Poiseuille's business — and most of it occurs across the arterioles. Bernoulli explains a local dip at a constriction relative to its neighbours; it never explains a steady drop in the direction of flow.

§ 08

Viscosity, Poiseuille's law and vascular resistance

The ideaReal liquids are sticky. Water drags on itself, and blood much more so, which means the layer of fluid touching the wall of a tube does not move at all and every layer further in has to slide past a slower neighbour. Keeping liquid moving along a tube therefore costs a steady push, and that push is spent for good — it goes to heat, and it is exactly what the last section deliberately left out. What makes this worth learning properly is how savagely the cost depends on the width of the tube: narrowing a tube a little does not reduce the flow a little, it guts it. That one fact is why a thin ring of muscle around a small vessel is enough to decide where the body sends its blood.

Exam point · why the arterioles, and not the capillaries, control blood flow

Because R ∝ 1/r⁴, a tiny change of radius is an enormous change of resistance — so a vessel that can change its radius controls flow with almost no muscular effort. Arterioles have a thick circular smooth-muscle coat and a radius they can halve or double — each of those is a 16-fold change in conductance on its own, so the two extremes are 256-fold apart. That is why the arterioles carry roughly half of the total peripheral resistance and produce the steepest part of the pressure profile, and why almost every vasoactive drug you will ever prescribe acts on them.

Resistances in series and in parallel

Because Δp = QR is formally Ohm's law, vascular networks combine exactly like resistor networks. Vessels in series (aorta → artery → arteriole → capillary → venule) all carry the same Q, and their pressure drops add, so resistances add. Vessels in parallel (the kidneys, the gut, the brain, all hanging off the aorta; the ≈ 10¹⁰ capillaries of one bed) all see the same Δp, and their flows add, so conductances add.

Trap · Poiseuille's fine print

  • It is r⁴, not r². The r² instinct comes from area (continuity) and gives a badly wrong answer. It also means a 5% error in measuring a radius is a 20% error in the computed resistance.
  • r is the internal radius of the lumen. Wall thickening reduces r even if the outer diameter grows.
  • It requires laminar flow. Once Re exceeds the critical value (§9) the law fails and Q rises only as roughly √Δp.
  • Distinguish dynamic viscosity η (Pa·s) from kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ (m²·s⁻¹). Poiseuille and Stokes use η; Reynolds can be written with either.
§ 09

The Reynolds number and turbulence

The ideaFlow comes in two kinds. Either the liquid slides along in orderly layers, quietly and cheaply, or it breaks up into a churning, swirling mess that makes noise and wastes energy. Which one you get is a contest between the liquid's tendency to keep barrelling forward once it is moving and its stickiness, which smooths small disturbances away before they can grow. This section builds one plain number that scores that contest — no units, just a number — so that a garden hose, a wind tunnel and an aorta can all be judged on the same scale. It earns its place in medicine because the messy kind of flow is audible: every murmur and bruit you will ever listen to is a flow that has lost the contest.

Two consequences deserve emphasis. Written as Re = ρvD/η, a bigger tube looks more turbulent — but only at fixed velocity. In the circulation the fixed quantity is usually the flow, and then Re = 4ρQ/(πηD) ∝ 1/D: at constant Q a narrowing doubles Re when it halves the diameter, while a widening lowers it. So the raw Re criterion predicts turbulence in a stenosis but not in an aneurysm — and yet aneurysms are turbulent too, because an abrupt expansion makes the flow separate from the wall and shed vortices. Getting this distinction right is a reliable way to pick up a mark.

Causes of a flow murmur, in Re order

↑ velocity (stenosis, exercise, fever, thyrotoxicosis, AV fistula, pregnancy) · ↓ viscosity (anaemia) · abrupt geometry (prosthetic valve, aneurysm, plaque, cannula tip). Anaemia in pregnancy hits two of the three at once, which is why flow murmurs are so common there.

§ 10

Surface tension, Laplace's law and the alveolus

The ideaThe surface of a liquid behaves like a stretched skin. A molecule down in the body of the liquid is tugged in every direction at once, but a molecule at the surface has nothing above it, so the surface is pulled taut and is forever trying to shrink to the smallest area it can. Curve that skin around a pocket of air and it squeezes inward on whatever it encloses — and the tighter the curve, the harder the squeeze, so a small pocket is squeezed harder than a large one. That is a real problem for a lung, which is a few hundred million wet air pockets of assorted sizes all joined to the same pipework: left to itself, every small one would empty into its larger neighbour and shut. Everything in this section is either that fact, or the body's very neat answer to it.

Laplace's law falls out of a force balance. Cut a spherical droplet of radius r in half. The pressure difference pushes the two hemispheres apart with force Δp · πr²; the surface tension holds them together along the cut circle with force γ · 2πr. Equate: Δp = 2γ/r. Do the same to a cylinder of length L — the cut is two straight lines of length L, and the projected area is 2rL — and you get Δp = γ/r. A soap bubble has an inner and an outer surface, so double the sphere answer: 4γ/r.

Trap · 2γ/r or 4γ/r, and radius or diameter

An alveolus is an air pocket lined by a thin film of fluid inside tissue: there is only one air–liquid interface, so it is 2γ/r. Only a free bubble with air on both sides (a soap bubble) gets 4γ/r. And r is always a radius: substituting a diameter halves the calculated pressure. Note also the direction of the effect — for a fixed γ, smaller radius means larger collapsing pressure, which is the opposite of most students' intuition.

Worked example 8 · The alveolus, with and without surfactant

(a) An alveolus of radius 100 μm lined with plain water-like fluid, γ = 0.070 N·m⁻¹. Find the collapsing pressure. (b) Repeat with surfactant present, γ = 0.010 N·m⁻¹. (c) Two alveoli of radii 50 μm and 150 μm share an airway. Show that a constant γ = 0.050 N·m⁻¹ is unstable, and that surfactant values of γ₁ = 0.010 and γ₂ = 0.030 N·m⁻¹ stabilise them.

  1. (a) Δp = 2γ/r = 2 × 0.070 / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 1400 Pa = 1400/133.3 = 10.5 mmHg = 1400/98.07 = 14.3 cmH₂O. Every breath would have to overcome that inward collapsing pressure — far beyond what the respiratory muscles can sustain for a lifetime.
  2. (b) Δp = 2 × 0.010 / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 200 Pa = 1.5 mmHg = 2.0 cmH₂O. A seven-fold reduction in the work needed to keep the alveolus open.
  3. (c) Constant γ. Δp₁ = 2 × 0.050/5.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 2000 Pa; Δp₂ = 2 × 0.050/1.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 667 Pa. The small alveolus is at three times the pressure of the large one, so gas flows small → large until the small one collapses.
  4. (c) With surfactant. Surfactant molecules are squeezed closer together as the surface shrinks, so γ falls with area: the small alveolus sits at γ₁ = 0.010, the large at γ₂ = 0.030. Δp₁ = 2 × 0.010/5.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 400 Pa and Δp₂ = 2 × 0.030/1.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 400 Pa. Equal — no driving gradient, no emptying, no atelectasis.

Exam point · what pulmonary surfactant actually does

The examinable answer is not simply "it lowers surface tension". Its four functions, in order of how often they are tested:

  1. Lowers γ (from ≈ 0.070 to as little as ≈ 0.005–0.010 N·m⁻¹), reducing the collapsing pressure and hence the work of breathing; lung compliance rises.
  2. Makes γ depend on surface area — this is the unique property. As an alveolus shrinks the surfactant film is compressed and γ falls further, so 2γ/r does not run away. That is what stabilises alveoli of different sizes and prevents small ones emptying into large ones.
  3. Prevents alveolar oedema. A high γ pulls the alveolar wall inward, lowering interstitial pressure and sucking fluid out of the capillaries. Lowering γ removes that suction.
  4. Immune and clearance roles (surfactant proteins A and D are opsonins).

It is secreted by type II pneumocytes from about 24 weeks' gestation, adequate by ≈ 35 weeks; its main surface-active component is dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC). Deficiency gives neonatal respiratory distress syndrome: stiff, atelectatic lungs. The same physics explains why PEEP works — holding a positive end-expiratory pressure keeps r from falling into the steep part of the 2γ/r curve.

Capillarity

Put a narrow tube into a liquid and the liquid climbs it (or is pushed down it). The cause is the competition between cohesion (liquid–liquid) and adhesion (liquid–solid), summarised by the contact angle θ. If θ < 90° the liquid wets the solid, the meniscus is concave, the curved surface pulls the liquid up, and it rises until the weight of the raised column balances the vertical component of the surface force:

γ (2πr) cos θ = ρ g (πr²) h  ⟹  h = 2γ cos θρ g rnarrow tube, θ measured through the liquid

Rise goes as 1/r: the narrower the tube, the higher the climb. Water (γ = 0.073 N·m⁻¹, θ ≈ 0 on clean glass) in a tube of radius 0.20 mm rises h = 2 × 0.073/(1000 × 9.8 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 7.4 cm. This is why a fingerprick blood sample fills a microhaematocrit tube without any suction: for blood (γ ≈ 0.058 N·m⁻¹, ρ = 1060) in a tube of radius 0.25 mm, the tube would self-fill to about 4.5 cm. For mercury on glass θ ≈ 140°, cos θ is negative, and the liquid is depressed below the outside level — which is why a mercury manometer reading is taken from the top of its convex meniscus and a water manometer reading from the bottom of its concave one.

Trap · Laplace applies to walls as well as to films

The same equation appears twice in medicine with different meanings. For a liquid film the unknown is a pressure: Δp = 2γ/r. For a vessel or chamber wall the unknown is a tension: T = Δp·r (tube) or Δp·r/2 (sphere), and the stress is σ = T/t. That second form explains why a dilated left ventricle has to generate more wall tension for the same blood pressure (so it hypertrophies to raise t and bring σ back down), why a large aneurysm ruptures and a small one does not, and why a distended bowel or bladder becomes ischaemic — the wall tension outruns the perfusion pressure.

§ 11

Stokes' law, terminal velocity and the ESR

The ideaDrop something small through a liquid and it does not keep speeding up. The liquid holds it back harder the faster it goes, so within a moment that holding-back — together with the upward push any immersed object already gets — exactly balances its weight, and from then on it simply drifts downward at one steady speed. How fast that steady drift is depends above all on how big the object is, and far more sharply than you would guess: stick several small particles together and the clump sinks much faster than any of them managed alone. That is the entire content of a common blood test, in which a tube of blood stands upright for an hour and someone measures how far the red cells have sunk — they sink faster when inflammation makes them clump, which is why the number is informative even though it points to no disease in particular.

Worked example 9 · The erythrocyte sedimentation rate from first principles

Model a red cell as a sphere of equivalent radius 2.8 μm and density 1100 kg·m⁻³, settling in plasma of density 1025 kg·m⁻³ and viscosity 1.5 × 10⁻³ Pa·s — the room-temperature value, because a Westergren tube stands on the bench at ≈ 20 °C, not at the 1.3 × 10⁻³ Pa·s tabulated for 37 °C in §8. Predict the sedimentation rate in mm·h⁻¹ and check that Stokes' law is legitimate.

  1. Assemble the pieces. r² = (2.8 × 10⁻⁶)² = 7.84 × 10⁻¹² m²; Δρ = 1100 − 1025 = 75 kg·m⁻³; 9η = 1.35 × 10⁻² Pa·s.
  2. Substitute. vt = 2 × 7.84 × 10⁻¹² × 75 × 9.8 / 1.35 × 10⁻² = 1.152 × 10⁻⁸ / 1.35 × 10⁻² = 8.54 × 10⁻⁷ m·s⁻¹.
  3. Convert to clinical units. 8.54 × 10⁻⁷ × 3600 = 3.07 × 10⁻³ m·h⁻¹ = 3.1 mm·h⁻¹ — squarely inside the normal Westergren range (roughly < 15 mm·h⁻¹ in men).
  4. Validity check. Re = ρvD/η = 1025 × 8.54 × 10⁻⁷ × 5.6 × 10⁻⁶ / 1.5 × 10⁻³ ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶. Six orders of magnitude below 1, so the creeping-flow assumption is superb.
  5. Inflammation. Fibrinogen and immunoglobulins rise, neutralising the negative surface charge that normally keeps red cells apart, and the cells stack into rouleaux. If the effective radius roughly triples, then since vt ∝ r² the rate rises about nine-fold — from 3 mm·h⁻¹ to nearly 30 mm·h⁻¹. That r² dependence, not any change in η, is what the ESR is really measuring.

vt = 8.5 × 10⁻⁷ m·s⁻¹ ≈ 3.1 mm·h⁻¹ · Re ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶ ⟹ Stokes valid

Exam point · reading an ESR as a physics problem

Every term in vt = 2r²Δρ g/9η maps onto a clinical variable:

  • r ↑ (rouleaux) → ESR ↑, and quadratically. Acute-phase response, myeloma, pregnancy, anaemia (fewer cells means less mutual hindrance, so also faster).
  • r ↓ or abnormal shape → ESR ↓. Sickle cells, spherocytes and acanthocytes cannot stack, so the ESR stays low even when inflammation is present — a classic trap.
  • Δρ is essentially fixed; red-cell density does not change appreciably in disease.
  • η ↑ or cell concentration ↑ → ESR ↓. Polycythaemia raises both viscosity and crowding, and gives a characteristically low ESR.
  • g → ω²R: a centrifuge at 3000 rpm with a 10 cm rotor generates ω²R/g = (314)² × 0.10/9.8 ≈ 1.0 × 10³, so separation that takes an hour under gravity takes a few seconds — this is why haematocrit is spun, not left to settle.

Trap · vt ∝ r², and what "hindered settling" means

Stokes drag is proportional to r, but weight and buoyancy are proportional to r³, so the terminal velocity goes as r². Double the effective particle radius and it settles four times faster, not twice. Also note that the ESR is a hindered settling process — the cells are at ≈ 45% volume fraction, not isolated in an infinite fluid — so the calculation above gives the right order of magnitude and the right dependences, not a precise prediction. Finally, if ρs < ρf the sign flips and vt is upward: the same equation describes a fat globule creaming to the top of milk, or a gas embolus rising in a vein.
