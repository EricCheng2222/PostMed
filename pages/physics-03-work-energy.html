Physics · Note 03

Work, Energy and Power

Newton's second law works instant by instant: to use it you need the force at every point of the path. Energy methods throw the path away. Work is the bookkeeping device that compresses "a force acting over a distance" into a single signed number, and once you know that gravity and springs give back exactly what they take, most mechanics problems collapse from three coupled equations to one line of arithmetic. The price of that shortcut is that energy never tells you when — only how fast and how far.

§ 01

Work: force × displacement, with a cosine

Definition & formula

Work
The energy transferred to an object by a force acting through a displacement. It is a scalar with a sign — not a vector, but not necessarily positive either.
Joule
The work done when a force of 1 N moves its point of application 1 m along its own direction.
W = F·d = F d cos θ constant force, straight-line displacement
1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²·s⁻²SI unit of work and of every kind of energy
Wnet = ΣWi = Fnet·dnet work = sum of the works of all forces
Wwork done by one force (J)
Fmagnitude of that force (N)
dmagnitude of the displacement of the point of application (m)
θangle between F and d (degrees or rad; cos θ is dimensionless)

Throughout this page bold symbols are vectors (F, d, v) and plain italic-free letters are their magnitudes. Work is the dot product of two vectors, and a dot product is a scalar: it has magnitude and sign but no direction. That single fact is why energy methods are easier than force methods — you add numbers, not arrows.

Read the formula as "the component of the force along the motion, times the distance moved". Everything perpendicular to the motion is discarded, because a sideways force changes where you go, not how fast you go. The cosine is not decoration; it is the whole physical content of the definition.

When work is zero

Three quite different situations all give W = 0, and exams like to mix them:

  • No displacement. Push a wall for an hour: d = 0, so W = 0 no matter how tired you are. (Your muscles still burn fuel — see §10 — but no mechanical work leaves your body.)
  • Force perpendicular to displacement. The normal force on a block sliding along a level floor; the tension in a string during uniform circular motion; the magnetic force on a moving charge. This is why centripetal force never changes speed.
  • No force. Trivial, but it is the reason a puck coasting on frictionless ice keeps exactly the kinetic energy it started with.

Trap · "negative work" does not mean "no work"

Negative work is a real, quantified transfer of energy out of the object. When you catch a falling ball, your hand does negative work on it equal in magnitude to the kinetic energy the ball loses. This is exactly why a long, soft catch hurts less than a short, hard one: the energy removed is fixed, so a larger stopping distance d demands a smaller force F. The same arithmetic is the entire design principle behind crumple zones, airbags, crash helmets and landing technique — a topic you will meet again in Note 04 as the impulse–momentum theorem.

Exam point · which distance goes into the formula?

d is the displacement of the point where the force is applied, not the total path length — with one important exception. For kinetic friction what you need is the length of the sliding path, because friction reverses whenever the motion reverses and therefore always takes energy out. A block that slides 2 m out and 2 m back has net displacement zero but has lost fk × 4 m of mechanical energy.

Worked example 1 · Sorting out the four forces on a dragged toolbox

An 18 kg toolbox is dragged 8.0 m across a level workshop floor by a strap held at 35° above the horizontal with a tension of 60 N. The coefficient of kinetic friction is μk = 0.20. Find the work done by each force, the net work, and the final speed if the box started from rest. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Normal force first — friction depends on it, and the strap is lifting.
    N = mg − T sin 35° = (18)(9.8) − (60)(0.5736) = 176.4 − 34.4 = 142.0 N.
    Note N ≠ mg here. Assuming N = mg is the single most common error in this problem.
  2. Friction force: fk = μkN = (0.20)(142.0) = 28.4 N.
  3. Work by the strap: WT = T d cos 35° = (60)(8.0)(0.8192) = +393 J.
  4. Work by friction: θ = 180°, so Wf = −(28.4)(8.0) = −227 J.
  5. Work by gravity and by the normal force: both are vertical, the displacement is horizontal, θ = 90° → 0 J each.
  6. Net work: 393 − 227 = +166 J.
  7. Final speed from the work–energy theorem (§03): ½mv² = 166 J → v = √(2 × 166 / 18) = √18.4 = 4.3 m·s⁻¹.
WT = +393 J · Wf = −227 J · WN = Wg = 0 · Wnet = +166 J · v = 4.3 m·s⁻¹

One-line hook

Work = (force along the motion) × (distance moved). If it is sideways, it does not count.

§ 02

Variable forces: the area under F–x

Definition & formula

Work done by a variable force
The accumulated Fx dx along the path — geometrically, the signed area between the F–x curve and the x-axis, counting area below the axis as negative.
Spring constant k
The stiffness of an elastic element: the force needed per unit of extension or compression.
W = ∫xixf Fx dx any force law, one dimension
Fs = −k xHooke's law; x measured from the natural length, minus sign = restoring
Wby spring = ½k xi² − ½k xf²negative when you stretch it, positive when it relaxes
Won spring = ½k xf² − ½k xi²work of the external agent; equal and opposite
Fxcomponent of the force along the direction of motion (N)
xextension or compression from the natural length (m)
kspring constant / stiffness (N·m⁻¹)
Wwork (J)

W = Fd cos θ assumes the force never changes. Real forces usually do: a spring gets harder to stretch, drag grows with speed, a muscle weakens as it shortens. The repair is the standard one in physics — chop the path into slices so short that F is effectively constant over each, add up FxΔx, and let the slices shrink. That sum is an integral, and an integral of F against x is the area under the graph. You almost never need calculus to evaluate it: on an exam the graph is made of triangles and rectangles.

The spring is the worked case

A spring pulls back with Fs = −kx. The minus sign says restoring: stretch it (x > 0) and it pulls back (F < 0). To hold it stretched you must supply +kx, a force that grows linearly from zero. Plot that applied force against x and you get a straight line through the origin; the area under it out to extension x is a triangle:

W = ½ · (base) · (height) = ½ · x · (kx) = ½ k x² ideal spring, obeying Hooke's law over the whole range

The factor of ½ is not a fudge — it is the average force. The force ramps from 0 to kx, so its mean value over the stretch is ½kx, and work = (mean force)(distance) = ½kx · x. That reading generalises: for any linearly varying force, use the average of the endpoint values.

Trap · where is x measured from?

In Us = ½kx², x is the displacement from the spring's natural (unstretched) length — never from the floor, never from the equilibrium position of a hanging mass, never from where you happened to put the origin. A second trap: because x is squared, compression by x stores exactly as much energy as extension by x. Elastic potential energy is never negative.

Worked example 2 · A spring-loaded launcher

A toy launcher uses a spring with k = 250 N·m⁻¹, compressed 12 cm. (a) How much energy is stored? (b) How much extra work would it take to compress it to 24 cm instead? (c) If the launcher fires a 50 g projectile horizontally and the spring gives back all its energy, how fast does it leave?

  1. (a) W = ½kx² = ½(250)(0.12)² = (125)(0.0144) = 1.80 J.
  2. (b) At 24 cm: ½(250)(0.24)² = (125)(0.0576) = 7.20 J. The extra work is 7.20 − 1.80 = 5.40 J = exactly 3 × 1.80 J. Doubling the compression quadruples the stored energy, so the second half of the squeeze costs three times the first.
  3. (c) All of Us becomes K: ½mv² = 1.80 J → v² = 2(1.80)/0.050 = 72.0 m²·s⁻² → v = 8.5 m·s⁻¹.
    Equivalently v = x√(k/m) = 0.12 × √5000 = 0.12 × 70.7 = 8.5 m·s⁻¹ — a formula worth recognising, since √(k/m) is the angular frequency ω of the same spring in oscillation.
(a) 1.80 J · (b) 5.40 J extra · (c) 8.5 m·s⁻¹

Exam point · reading areas off any F–x graph

Given a piecewise-linear F–x graph, the work is just the sum of the shapes: rectangles, triangles, trapezoids. Area below the x-axis counts as negative work. This is the standard way an exam tests whether you have understood the definition rather than memorised Fd cos θ — and it is also how muscle physiology plots work: the area enclosed by a pressure–volume loop of the left ventricle is the mechanical work the heart does per beat (about 1 J for the left ventricle at rest, which is the same integral with P and V in place of F and x).

§ 03

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

Definition & formula

Kinetic energy
The energy a body has because of its motion — numerically, the total work needed to bring it from rest to speed v. It is a scalar, never negative, and has no direction.
Work–energy theorem
The net work done on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy. It is the bridge between the force picture and the motion picture.
Wnet = ΔK = ½m vf² − ½m vi² any force, constant or variable; any path
K = ½ m v²non-relativistic: valid while v ≪ c
K = p² / (2m)the form you will want in Note 04 (collisions)
d = v² / (2 μk g)skid to rest on a level surface; mass cancels
Kkinetic energy (J)
mmass (kg)
vspeed (m·s⁻¹)
pmagnitude of momentum, p = mv (kg·m·s⁻¹)
Wnetnet work by all forces (J)
μkcoefficient of kinetic friction (dimensionless)

Where does ½mv² come from? Take the simplest case — a constant net force along the motion — and combine Newton's second law with the constant-acceleration kinematics of Note 01:

vf² = vi² + 2 a dkinematics, constant a
× ½m → ½m vf² − ½m vi² = (m a) d = Fnet dNewton's second law substituted

The right-hand side is the net work; the left-hand side defines the quantity whose change that work equals. So ½mv² is not an arbitrary definition — it is forced on us by demanding that "force × distance" have a clean interpretation. The result then turns out to hold far beyond the assumptions used to derive it: with the integral definition of work it is true for variable forces and curved paths as well.

Exam point · the quadratic is the whole story

K depends on v², not v. Double the speed and you quadruple the kinetic energy, so you quadruple the stopping distance at a given braking force, and you quadruple the energy that has to be absorbed by a body in a collision. This one fact is why trauma severity climbs so much faster than speed, and why dropping a limit from 60 km·h⁻¹ to 30 km·h⁻¹ outside a hospital removes three quarters of the energy a pedestrian would have to absorb, not half. Contrast a fall, where the energy on landing is mgh and therefore grows only linearly with height: a 4 m fall delivers exactly twice the energy of a 2 m fall, at only √2 times the impact speed. Know which variable the quadratic is in.

Worked example 3 · Braking distance and why mass does not appear

A car skids to rest with its wheels locked on a level road, μk = 0.70, g = 9.8 m·s⁻². Find the skid distance at 14 m·s⁻¹ (≈ 50 km·h⁻¹) and at 28 m·s⁻¹ (≈ 100 km·h⁻¹).

  1. Identify the forces that do work. Gravity and the normal force are perpendicular to the motion → zero work. Only friction acts along the road: Wnet = −fkd = −μk m g d.
  2. Apply the theorem. −μk m g d = 0 − ½mv², so d = v² / (2 μk g). The mass cancels — a truck and a motorcycle with the same tyres skid the same distance.
  3. Substitute. 2μkg = 2(0.70)(9.8) = 13.72 m·s⁻².
    At 14 m·s⁻¹: d = 196 / 13.72 = 14.3 m.
    At 28 m·s⁻¹: d = 784 / 13.72 = 57.1 m.
  4. Read the ratio. Doubling the speed multiplied the distance by exactly 4 — and that is before adding the reaction distance, which grows only linearly with speed.
d(14 m·s⁻¹) = 14.3 m · d(28 m·s⁻¹) = 57.1 m · ratio = 4.00, independent of mass

Trap · "net" work means the sum over every force

Wnet = ΔK is a statement about the total. In Worked example 1 the strap did +393 J but the box gained only 166 J, because friction removed 227 J. Students who write "W = ΔK" and then substitute the work of the applied force alone get the wrong answer every time. Either sum all the works, or use the net force in W = Fnet d cos θ — never mix the two.

Trap · when you are not a particle

Stand up from a chair, or jump: your kinetic energy rises, yet the floor's normal force did zero work because the floor never moved. There is no contradiction — the theorem in the form Wnet = ΔK applies to a point particle. For a deformable body the correct statement is the centre-of-mass equation, and the energy actually came from internal chemical energy in your muscles. The external force merely provided the push. Any exam item about jumping, walking or a car accelerating on its tyres is testing this distinction: static friction on a rolling wheel does no work at the contact point, but the engine still burns fuel.

One-line hook

Work is what a force does; kinetic energy is what the body has. The theorem says the first is the change in the second.

§ 04

Conservative and non-conservative forces

Definition & formula

Conservative force
A force whose total work around any closed path is exactly zero — equivalently, one whose work between two points depends only on the endpoints and not on the route taken.
Non-conservative force
A force whose work does depend on the path, so that no potential-energy function can be assigned to it.
∮ F·ds = 0 the definitive test: zero work around every closed loop
WA→B is the same for every path A → Bequivalent statement
Wcons = −ΔU = Ui − Ufonly possible for conservative forces — this is what U means
∮integral taken once around a closed loop (dimensionless operator)
Fthe force being tested, a vector (N)
dsinfinitesimal vector element of the path (m)
Upotential energy associated with the force (J)
Wconswork done by the conservative force (J)

This is the classification that makes potential energy possible at all. If the work a force does from A to B is the same however you travel, then that work is a property of where you are, not of how you got there — so you can tabulate it as a function of position and call it −U. If the work depends on the route, no such function exists, and there is nothing to tabulate.

Applying the closed-loop test

Gravity passes. Carry a book up 2 m and back down 2 m by any route: gravity does −mg(2) going up and +mg(2) coming down. Net zero. It does not matter whether you took the stairs or a spiral ramp, because only the vertical component of each step contributes.

Kinetic friction fails, and fails badly. Friction always opposes the relative sliding, so it does negative work on the way out and negative work on the way back. Round a closed loop of total path length L it does −fkL, which is never zero. Worse, its value depends on how long a route you chose — take the scenic path and you lose more. That path dependence is precisely why "friction potential energy" is a meaningless phrase.

"Non-conservative" does not mean "energy is destroyed"

Total energy is always conserved — that is the first law of thermodynamics and it has no exceptions. What non-conservative forces destroy is only the mechanical bookkeeping: the energy is still there, but it has been dispersed into the random kinetic energy of molecules (thermal energy), into sound, or into permanent deformation, and you cannot get it back as organised motion. The honest name for friction and drag is dissipative.

Trap · non-conservative ≠ dissipative ≠ does work

The normal force, the tension in an inextensible string, and static friction are all non-conservative, yet in most problems they do zero work and dissipate nothing — they simply steer. Ticking "the normal force removes energy from the block on an incline" is a classic wrong answer: N is perpendicular to the motion, so WN = 0, always. Conversely a hand or a motor is non-conservative and does large positive work. Non-conservative means "path-dependent", nothing more.

§ 05

Potential energy: gravitational and elastic

Definition & formula

Potential energy
Energy stored in the configuration of a system because a conservative force acts inside it. It belongs to the system (ball and Earth; both ends of the spring), not to a single object.
Reference level
The arbitrarily chosen position at which U is declared to be zero. Only differences in U have physical meaning.
Ug = m g y near Earth's surface only, where g is effectively constant
ΔU = −Wconsthe definition of potential energy
ΔUg = m g Δy = m g (yf − yi)Δy is the vertical rise, not distance along a slope
Us = ½ k x²ideal spring; x from natural length; always ≥ 0
Uggravitational potential energy (J)
mmass (kg)
ggravitational field strength, 9.8 N·kg⁻¹ = 9.8 m·s⁻²
yheight above the chosen reference level (m)
Uselastic potential energy (J)
kspring constant (N·m⁻¹)
xextension or compression (m)

Potential energy is a piece of accounting genius. Rather than track the work gravity does at every step of a complicated path, you agree once and for all to store "the work gravity would do if the object came back to the reference level" as a number attached to position. Then gravity never appears in your work sum again — it appears as U on the other side of the equation. That is the whole trick, and it works only because gravity is conservative (§04).

The minus sign in ΔU = −Wcons is the one to get right. Lift a book: gravity does negative work, so ΔU is positive and the store fills. Drop it: gravity does positive work, ΔU is negative, the store empties into kinetic energy.

Why the reference level is genuinely arbitrary

Put a 1.5 kg book on a table 0.80 m above the floor. Measuring from the floor, U = (1.5)(9.8)(0.80) = 11.8 J. Measuring from the table top, U = 0. Measuring from the ceiling, U is negative. All three are correct, and every physical prediction is identical, because physics only ever asks for ΔU. If the book falls to the floor, ΔU = −11.8 J in every one of those choices.

Practical rule: put y = 0 at the lowest point the object reaches. Then every U is positive and you never lose a sign. If a problem has a spring as well, the spring's reference level is not free — it is fixed at the natural length by the form ½kx².

Trap · Δy is vertical height, not distance along the incline

A block sliding 4.2 m down a slope inclined at 36.5° falls Δy = 4.2 sin 36.5° = 2.5 m, and mgΔy uses the 2.5, not the 4.2. Friction, by contrast, uses the 4.2 — it acts along the surface. Mixing the two lengths up is the single most productive source of wrong answers in incline problems. A second, subtler trap: Ug = mgy assumes constant g, so it is valid for buildings and mountains but not for satellites, where U = −GMm/r takes over.

Exam point · potential energy belongs to the system

Strictly, gravitational potential energy is a property of the Earth–object system, because it arises from the interaction between the two. Saying "the ball has 20 J of potential energy" is shorthand that examiners tolerate in calculation questions and punish in concept questions. The same holds for a spring: the energy resides in the deformation, shared by both ends, not in one coil.

§ 06

Conservation of mechanical energy

Definition & formula

Mechanical energy
The sum of the kinetic and potential energies of a system: the energy that is available as organised motion or as stored configuration.
Conservation of mechanical energy
If every force that does work is conservative, Emech has the same numerical value at every instant of the motion.
Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf valid only when no non-conservative force does work
Emech = K + Ug + Us = constantdefinition plus the conservation statement
½m vi² + m g yi = ½m vf² + m g yfgravity only; m cancels throughout
v = √(2 g Δh)released from rest, gravity only — independent of mass and of path shape
Emechmechanical energy (J)
Kkinetic energy, ½mv² (J)
Utotal potential energy (J)
Δhvertical drop (m)
vspeed (m·s⁻¹)
g9.8 m·s⁻²

Start from the work–energy theorem and split the net work into two piles — conservative and not:

Wcons + Wnc = ΔKwork–energy theorem, forces sorted
−ΔU + Wnc = ΔK ⟹ Wnc = ΔK + ΔU = ΔEmechusing ΔU = −Wcons

Set Wnc = 0 and mechanical energy is constant. This is the master equation of the chapter: the work of non-conservative forces is exactly the change in mechanical energy. Conservation is the special case, not a separate law.

One line where kinematics needs three

Here is the payoff, in the form exams like best. A ball is thrown at 15 m·s⁻¹ at 40° above the horizontal. Ignoring air resistance, how fast is it moving when it is 3.0 m above the launch point?

The kinematic route needs three steps, all of them chances to slip:

  1. resolve: vx = 15 cos 40° = 11.49 m·s⁻¹, vy0 = 15 sin 40° = 9.64 m·s⁻¹;
  2. vertical motion: vy² = 9.64² − 2(9.8)(3.0) = 92.97 − 58.8 = 34.17 → vy = 5.85 m·s⁻¹;
  3. recombine: v = √(11.49² + 5.85²) = √166.2 = 12.9 m·s⁻¹.

The energy route needs one:

v² = v0² − 2 g h = 225 − 2(9.8)(3.0) = 166.2 → v = 12.9 m·s⁻¹ m cancels; the launch angle never enters

And it delivers a bonus the long route hides: the answer does not depend on the launch angle at all. Any projectile launched at 15 m·s⁻¹ is moving at 12.9 m·s⁻¹ when it passes 3.0 m above the launch point — in whatever direction, provided it is launched steeply enough to get there at all (hmax = v0²sin²θ/2g reaches 3.0 m only for θ ≳ 31°). Energy knows about heights, not about directions.

Worked example 4 · Same speed, different questions

Using the ball above (v0 = 15 m·s⁻¹ at 40°, g = 9.8 m·s⁻²): (a) confirm the speed at h = 3.0 m; (b) find the maximum height; (c) explain why the same method cannot tell you when the ball is at 3.0 m.

  1. (a) ½mv² + mgh = ½mv0². Cancel m: v² = v0² − 2gh = 225 − 58.8 = 166.2 m²·s⁻² → v = 12.9 m·s⁻¹. The kinematic check above agrees to three figures, as it must.
  2. (b) At the top the ball is not at rest — it still has vx = 11.49 m·s⁻¹. Only the vertical part is spent: hmax = vy0²/(2g) = 92.97/19.6 = 4.74 m. Writing 0 = 225 − 2(9.8)h and getting 11.5 m is the classic error — that would be the answer for a ball thrown straight up.
  3. (c) Energy relates speed to position, and it contains no t. To get the time you must go back to y = vy0t − ½gt². Energy answers "how fast" and "how high"; kinematics answers "when".
(a) 12.9 m·s⁻¹ (angle-independent) · (b) 4.74 m · (c) energy carries no time information

Exam point · what energy conservation buys and what it costs

It buys: the speed at any point of an arbitrarily complicated smooth path — a roller coaster, a pendulum, a curved slide, a loop — using only the height difference. A bead released from rest on any frictionless track reaches the bottom at √(2gΔh), whatever shape the track has. It costs: all information about time and about the direction of v. If the question says "how long" or "at what angle", energy alone cannot finish it.

Energy tells you how fast; Newton tells you when. Choose the tool by the question word.

§ 07

Energy accounting when friction is present

Definition & formula

Thermal energy generated by sliding
The mechanical energy converted into disordered molecular motion at a sliding interface. It equals the friction force times the distance the surfaces slide past each other.
Energy accounting
The general statement that mechanical energy in equals mechanical energy out plus everything that leaked. Nothing is lost; it is only relabelled.
Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf + fk d sliding friction present; d = length of the sliding path
ΔEth = fk d = μk N dd is relative sliding distance, not net displacement
Wnc = ΔK + ΔU = ΔEmechgeneral form; Wnc < 0 for friction and drag
fk = μk NN is the normal force, = mg cos θ on an incline
fkkinetic friction force (N)
Nnormal force at the sliding surface (N)
dsliding path length (m)
ΔEththermal energy generated (J)
Wncwork of non-conservative forces (J)
μkcoefficient of kinetic friction (dimensionless)

Almost no real problem is frictionless, and the repair is small: mechanical energy is no longer constant, so put the leak explicitly on the low side of the ledger. Read the master equation as a sentence — what you started with equals what you ended with plus what escaped as heat. Written this way every term is positive and the sign errors disappear.

Worked example 5 · A child on a playground slide

A 25 kg child starts from rest at the top of a straight slide, 2.5 m above the ground, and arrives at the bottom at 5.0 m·s⁻¹. The sliding surface is 4.2 m long. (a) How much mechanical energy was dissipated? (b) What was the average friction force? (c) What is μk? (d) What speed would a frictionless slide have given? Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Set the reference level at the bottom, so Uf = 0 and every number is positive.
  2. (a) Ui = mgh = (25)(9.8)(2.5) = 612.5 J. Kf = ½(25)(5.0)² = 312.5 J.
    ΔEth = 612.5 − 312.5 = 300 J — very nearly half the available energy.
  3. (b) ΔEth = fkd, and d is the 4.2 m along the surface: fk = 300/4.2 = 71 N.
  4. (c) The slide's angle: sin θ = 2.5/4.2 = 0.595 → θ = 36.5°, cos θ = 0.804. N = mg cos θ = (25)(9.8)(0.804) = 197 N. So μk = 71.4/197 = 0.36 — plausible for cloth on plastic.
  5. (d) Frictionless: v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 2.5) = √49 = 7.0 m·s⁻¹. Friction cost 2.0 m·s⁻¹ of exit speed but 300 J of energy — another reminder that energy is quadratic in v, so equal energy losses are not equal speed losses.
(a) 300 J · (b) 71 N · (c) μk ≈ 0.36 · (d) 7.0 m·s⁻¹ frictionless

Trap · fkd uses the path, and it is never recovered

Send a block up a rough incline and let it slide back to the start. Gravity's contribution to the round trip is exactly zero — but friction has acted over twice the ramp length, so the block returns with less kinetic energy than it left with. Concretely, for launch speed v0 the return speed obeys v²/v0² = (sin θ − μk cos θ)/(sin θ + μk cos θ). With θ = 30° and μk = 0.25 that ratio is 0.396, so the block comes back at 63 % of its launch speed. Setting the return speed equal to v0 is the classic mistake.

Exam point · "relative sliding distance" matters when both surfaces move

For a block sliding on a stationary floor the friction force does −fkd on the block and generates +fkd of heat, so the two numbers coincide. If both surfaces move — a block skidding on a moving conveyor, or a book slipping on a sliding tray — then ΔEth = fk × (relative displacement), which is neither body's displacement. That distinction separates a competent answer from a lucky one.

§ 08

Power, and the units it comes dressed in

Definition & formula

Power
The rate at which energy is transferred, or equivalently the rate at which work is done. It is a scalar, and it may be positive or negative depending on the direction of the transfer.
Watt
One joule per second. Power is a rate; energy is an amount. Confusing the two is the most common unit error in the whole chapter.

Two forms, two uses. P̄ = W/Δt answers "how much energy per second on average"; P = Fv answers "how hard is this engine working right now". The second follows from the first in one step: in a short time dt the force moves its point of application by dx = v dt, so dW = F dx = F v dt.

Why P = Fv changes how machines are built

At a fixed power output, force and speed trade off against each other: F = P/v. A cyclist in a low gear pushes hard and moves slowly; in a high gear the same legs deliver the same power but as a smaller force at a higher speed. That single relation is the reason gearboxes exist, the reason a car's acceleration falls away at high speed, and the reason a winch that lifts 500 kg does it slowly.

Combine it with aerodynamic drag and the scaling becomes brutal. Above about 60 km·h⁻¹ the dominant resistance is Fdrag = ½ C ρ A v², so the power needed to hold speed is P = F v ∝ v³. Doubling your cruising speed needs eight times the power, which is why a car's fuel consumption per kilometre rises so steeply on the motorway and why cycling records are set in still air.

Worked example 6 · How powerful is a person running upstairs?

A 68 kg student runs up one flight of stairs, a vertical rise of 3.6 m, in 5.0 s. Find the useful mechanical work, the average mechanical power in watts and in horsepower, and estimate the metabolic power drawn if muscle is 25 % efficient. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Work against gravity. The only thing that changes (apart from a little kinetic energy at the start, which we neglect) is height: W = mgh = (68)(9.8)(3.6) = 2.40×10³ J. Horizontal movement along each step contributes nothing, because gravity is vertical.
  2. Average mechanical power. P̄ = 2399/5.0 = 4.8×10² W.
  3. In horsepower. 480/746 = 0.64 hp. A fit human sustains roughly a fifth of a horsepower for long periods and can peak near one horsepower for a few seconds — the unit was calibrated on horses for a reason.
  4. Metabolic cost. If only 25 % of the chemical energy consumed emerges as mechanical work, the body must draw Pin = 480/0.25 = 1.9×10³ W. That is about twenty times the resting metabolic rate (≈ 100 W, §10), and the other 1.4 kW appears immediately as heat — which is why one flight of stairs makes you warm before it makes you tired.
W = 2.40 kJ · P̄ = 480 W = 0.64 hp · metabolic ≈ 1.9 kW at 25 % efficiency

Worked example 7 · Cruising at constant speed

A car travels at a steady 25 m·s⁻¹ (90 km·h⁻¹) on level road against a total resistive force (drag + rolling) of 480 N. (a) What power must the engine deliver at the wheels? (b) What happens if the driver raises the speed to 50 m·s⁻¹ and drag dominates?

  1. (a) At constant speed the net force is zero, so the driving force equals the resistance: F = 480 N. P = Fv = (480)(25) = 1.2×10⁴ W = 12 kW ≈ 16 hp. Note how modest this is compared with the engine's rated 75–100 kW: most of an engine's power exists for acceleration and hills, not for cruising.
  2. (b) If F ∝ v², doubling v multiplies the force by 4 and the power by 8: P ≈ 8 × 12 kW = 96 kW. The car would be at the limit of its engine, and fuel used per kilometre would be about four times higher.

Trap · kW is a rate, kWh is an amount

A kilowatt-hour is a kilowatt multiplied by an hour: 1000 J·s⁻¹ × 3600 s = 3.6×10⁶ J. It is an energy, and it is what the electricity company actually sells you. "Per hour" appearing in a unit name is a reliable sign of a rate; "hour" multiplying is a reliable sign of an amount. A 2 kW kettle run for half an hour uses 1.0 kWh = 3.6 MJ — enough, incidentally, to lift a 70 kg person 5.2 km if it could all be converted.

Exam point · average versus instantaneous

Questions are written to catch you swapping the two. "A crane lifts 800 kg through 12 m in 30 s" wants P̄ = mgh/Δt = (800)(9.8)(12)/30 = 3.14 kW. "…and what is the power at the moment it is moving at 0.60 m·s⁻¹?" wants P = Fv = (800)(9.8)(0.60) = 4.70 kW. The two differ because the lift includes acceleration and deceleration phases; only for genuinely constant speed do they coincide.

§ 09

Reading a potential-energy curve

Definition & formula

Potential-energy curve
A graph of U against position for a conservative system. Its slope gives the force at every point and its shape classifies every equilibrium.
Equilibrium
A position where the net force is zero — that is, where the U curve is flat.
Fx = − dU/dx one dimension, conservative force only
K(x) = E − U(x) ≥ 0defines the classically allowed region; K = 0 at a turning point
dU/dx = 0condition for equilibrium
d²U/dx² > 0 stable · < 0 unstable · = 0 neutralcurvature test, applied where dU/dx = 0; the = 0 case is only neutral if U is flat over a whole region, otherwise inconclusive
U(x)potential energy as a function of position (J)
Fxforce component along x (N)
Etotal mechanical energy, a horizontal line on the graph (J)
K(x)kinetic energy at position x (J)
xposition (m)

Fx = −dU/dx is just ΔU = −W read backwards, and it is worth checking on the two cases you already know. For a spring, U = ½kx² gives dU/dx = kx, so F = −kx — Hooke's law recovered. For gravity near the ground, U = mgy gives dU/dy = mg, so Fy = −mg, i.e. downward. The formula is not new physics; it is the same physics written so a graph can answer questions.

The minus sign has a picture: the force always points downhill on the U curve, and it is strongest where the curve is steepest. A ball placed on the graph would roll the way the real force pushes. That is a rare case where the intuitive picture is also literally correct.

Exam point · the standard three-part question

Given U(x) as a polynomial, you will be asked to (i) differentiate to get F(x), (ii) set F = 0 to find the equilibria, (iii) classify them. Example: U(x) = 3x² − 12x (J, x in m) gives F = −(6x − 12) = 12 − 6x, which vanishes at x = 2.0 m; since d²U/dx² = +6 J·m⁻² is positive, that point is a stable equilibrium, with Umin = −12 J. Note that a negative U is perfectly acceptable — it is only a consequence of where the reference level sits.

Trap · equilibrium point ≠ turning point

An equilibrium is where the force is zero (dU/dx = 0); a turning point is where the speed is zero (E = U). They are different conditions and usually different places: a pendulum bob has zero speed at the ends of its swing — where the force is largest — and maximum speed at the bottom, where the force along the motion is zero. Where E lies also decides the qualitative behaviour: below the barrier the particle is bound and oscillates in the well; above it, the particle escapes.

This diagram is the reason the section exists at all for medical candidates. The same well appears as the Lennard-Jones curve for two atoms: the bond length is the position of the minimum, the bond energy is the depth of the well, and the curvature at the bottom is the effective spring constant that sets the vibrational frequency measured by infrared spectroscopy. Protein folding funnels, ligand binding wells and membrane-transport barriers are all read the same way — depth means stability, and the height of the barrier between two wells sets how fast the system can hop from one to the other.

§ 10

Efficiency and the human engine

Definition & formula

Efficiency
The fraction of the energy supplied to a device that emerges as the useful output you wanted. A dimensionless number between 0 and 1, usually quoted as a percentage.
Metabolic rate
The rate at which a body converts chemical energy from food, measured in watts (or, clinically, in kcal per day or in METs).

No machine converts everything. The missing fraction is not a design failing that better engineering could remove — it is the second law of thermodynamics expressed as an accounting statement. What matters for problem-solving is remembering which quantity goes on top: useful output over total input, and both must be the same kind of quantity (both energies or both powers).

Human beings run at about 20–25 %

Skeletal muscle converts roughly a fifth to a quarter of the chemical energy it consumes into mechanical work; the rest appears immediately as heat. This is not poor by engine standards — a petrol engine manages 25–30 % — but it has a consequence no engine has to worry about: the waste heat is generated inside a body whose enzymes fail above about 40 °C. Thermoregulation is therefore not a side issue in exercise physiology; it is the binding constraint, which is why evaporative cooling, skin blood flow and hydration dominate endurance performance.

Two numbers anchor the whole picture. A resting adult eating about 2000 kcal a day is dissipating 2000 × 4184 / 86400 = 97 W — a human at rest is a 100 W heater, which is exactly why a crowded lecture room warms up. Of that, roughly 20 W goes to the brain: about a fifth of the resting budget for 2 % of the body mass.

Worked example 8 · How far does a 250 kcal snack take you?

A 70 kg person eats a snack labelled 250 Calories. Muscle is 20 % efficient, g = 9.8 m·s⁻². (a) How much energy is that in joules? (b) In principle, how high could the person climb on it? (c) How much heat is produced? (d) If none of that heat escaped, by how much would core temperature rise (cbody ≈ 3470 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹), and how much sweat must evaporate to shed it (latent heat ≈ 2.43×10⁶ J·kg⁻¹ at skin temperature)?

  1. (a) A food "Calorie" is a kilocalorie: E = 250 × 4184 = 1.05×10⁶ J ≈ 1.0 MJ.
  2. (b) Only 20 % becomes mechanical work: W = 0.20 × 1.046×10⁶ = 2.09×10⁵ J. Climbing means mgh, so h = 2.092×10⁵ / [(70)(9.8)] = 209200/686 = 3.0×10² m — roughly a 100-storey building, on one small snack. Human muscle is remarkably economical; the reason weight loss is hard is not that exercise is inefficient but that food is extraordinarily energy-dense.
  3. (c) The other 80 % is heat: 0.80 × 1.046×10⁶ = 8.4×10⁵ J.
  4. (d) ΔT = Q/(mc) = 836800/[(70)(3470)] = 836800/242900 = 3.4 K. A core temperature of 40.4 °C is genuinely dangerous, so the heat must go. Evaporating sweat: m = 836800/2.43×10⁶ = 0.34 kg ≈ 340 mL. That is the physics behind fluid replacement advice, and behind why exertional heat illness is a humidity problem rather than a temperature problem — high humidity blocks the evaporation, not the sweating.
(a) 1.05 MJ · (b) ≈ 305 m of climb · (c) 8.4×10⁵ J of heat · (d) ΔT ≈ 3.4 K, ≈ 340 mL of sweat

Exam point · food energy densities are worth memorising

Fat yields ≈ 9 kcal·g⁻¹ (37 kJ·g⁻¹); carbohydrate and protein ≈ 4 kcal·g⁻¹ (17 kJ·g⁻¹); ethanol ≈ 7 kcal·g⁻¹ (29 kJ·g⁻¹). Fat wins because its carbon is the most reduced — it has the most C–H bonds to oxidise, exactly the argument made in Biochem note 01. Combined with 1 kcal = 4184 J, these four numbers let you convert any nutrition label into joules and any joule figure into a plausible physiological statement.

Trap · negative work is metabolically cheap, but it is not free

Walking downhill, your quadriceps contract while lengthening (eccentric contraction) and do negative mechanical work — they absorb the energy gravity releases. Measured metabolically, eccentric work costs roughly three to five times less per joule than concentric work, which is why descending feels easier than ascending. It is also why it produces far more delayed-onset muscle soreness: the mechanical loading per active fibre is higher even though the energy cost is lower. The physics point for the exam is narrower and cleaner: W < 0 does not imply Einput = 0. A muscle burns ATP whether it is shortening, lengthening, or holding still against a load with no displacement at all.

One-line hook

You are a 100 W heater that turns one fifth of its fuel into motion. Everything else in this section is a consequence of those two numbers.
