Plant Structure, Transport and Nutrition

A plant is an organism with no heart, no muscles and no nerves that nevertheless moves hundreds of litres of water from the soil to the sky and hundreds of grams of sugar from leaf to root. It does both without spending ATP on the movement itself. Almost every question on this half of the plant block is, underneath, a question about one number — the water potential Ψ — and about the two pipes that Ψ drives flow through: a dead one under tension, and a living one under pressure. Get the sign conventions of Ψ secure and the tall tree, the wilting leaf, the closing stoma, the girdled trunk and the loaded sieve tube all become the same problem asked five ways.

§ 01

The three tissue systems

The ideaA plant is not built organ by organ out of separate materials. Three continuous layers run through the whole body without a break: a skin on the outside, a set of pipes threading through the middle, and packing material filling everything in between. A root, a stem and a leaf are not three different constructions — they are three different arrangements of exactly the same three layers. Once you can name which layer a cell belongs to, most anatomy questions collapse into one question: where does that layer sit in this organ?

Definition & organisation

Tissue system
A tissue, or a set of tissues, that is continuous throughout the plant body — through root, stem and leaf alike. There are exactly three, and every cell in a plant belongs to one of them.
plant body = dermal ⊕ vascular ⊕ ground covering · plumbing · everything else — continuous through every organ
Full definition, symbols and units
Dermal tissue system
The outer covering. In a primary body this is the epidermis, a single layer coated on its outer wall with a cuticle of cutin polyester and waxes. Where secondary growth occurs the epidermis is replaced by periderm.
Vascular tissue system
Xylem conducts water and dissolved minerals upward from the root; phloem conducts sugars and other organic solutes from source to sink in whichever direction that requires. Both also contain supporting and living parenchyma cells.
Ground tissue system
Everything that is neither dermal nor vascular: pith internal to the vascular tissue, cortex external to it. Built from parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma, and responsible for photosynthesis, storage and support.

Dermal: one cell thick and doing four jobs

The epidermis has to be transparent enough to let light through to the mesophyll, waterproof enough to stop the leaf drying out, permeable enough to let CO₂ in, and tough enough to keep pathogens out. It solves this by specialising: ordinary epidermal cells are flat, tightly interlocked and cuticle-covered; guard cells punch adjustable holes through the barrier; trichomes (hairs) stand off the surface to trap a layer of still air, reflect light and deter insects; and in roots, where there is no cuticle and no light, epidermal cells extend root hairs.

A root hair is not a cell

A root hair is a tubular extension of a single epidermal cell — same cytoplasm, same nucleus, no cross wall. This matters because it explains the numbers: a root hair costs almost nothing to build and lives only days to weeks, yet it multiplies absorbing surface enormously. The classic 1937 measurement on a single winter-rye plant grown in roughly 0.05 m³ of soil found some 600 m² of combined root and root-hair surface. A trichome, by contrast, is a cell (or several), and is a shoot structure. Calling a root hair a trichome, or a trichome a root hair, is a standard distractor.

Vascular: two pipes with opposite designs

The two conducting tissues are built on opposite principles, and almost every exam item about them turns on that contrast. Xylem conducts through dead cells. A tracheid or vessel element builds a lignified secondary wall, then undergoes programmed cell death and autolysis, leaving a hollow, rigid, open-ended tube. That is not a limitation but the design: a living cell would have a plasma membrane, and a membrane cannot hold the enormous tensions that § 06 will show xylem sap is under. Phloem conducts through living cells. A sieve-tube element retains its plasma membrane — it must, because phloem transport depends on maintaining a solute gradient across that membrane — but it sacrifices its nucleus, ribosomes, vacuole and Golgi to clear the lumen, and is kept alive by an adjacent companion cell connected to it by unusually dense plasmodesmata.

The vessel/tracheid item, and how it is set

Examiners like this pair because the intuitive answer is wrong. It is not the case that angiosperms have vessels "instead of" tracheids — they have both. Conifers have only tracheids, which is why softwood is anatomically "non-porous" and why a pine is a slower but more reliable conductor. The functional trade-off is worth being able to state in one line: a wide vessel conducts far more water per unit pressure drop, but a wider conduit cavitates more readily — under freezing, under drought, or under mechanical damage — and when a vessel embolises, a much larger share of the plant's conducting capacity is lost at once. Safety versus efficiency, decided by conduit diameter.

Flow through a tube scales as the fourth power of radius (Poiseuille), so doubling conduit diameter multiplies conductance by sixteen. That single fact explains both why vessels evolved and why they are dangerous.

Xylem versus phloem — there is no mnemonic for this, and you do not need one

Every row of the comparison table in § 09 falls out of one sentence you have already read: the water pipe is dead and pulled from the top; the sugar pipe is alive and pushed from the source. Run it forwards and the table rebuilds itself. Dead ⇒ no plasma membrane ⇒ it can hold −2 MPa of tension ⇒ Ψp is negative ⇒ nothing metabolic is involved ⇒ steam-killing and poisons do not stop it ⇒ the energy is solar. Alive ⇒ a membrane, because a solute gradient needs one ⇒ loading is active ⇒ Ψp is positive ⇒ chilling, anoxia and metabolic poisons stop it within minutes ⇒ but only at the two ends, because the transit itself is bulk flow. Memorising fourteen table cells is a way of forgetting thirteen of them. Memorise dead and pulled / alive and pushed and derive the rest in the four seconds it takes to read an option.

Two sentences that sound right and are not

"Collenchyma cells have thick secondary walls that provide rigid support." Wrong on both counts. Collenchyma has a thickened primary wall, unlignified, and it provides flexible support — that is precisely why it, and not sclerenchyma, is the support tissue of growing organs. A lignified wall cannot be extended, so a young stem full of sclerenchyma could not elongate.

"Sclerenchyma cells are living cells with lignified walls." Fibres and sclereids complete their walls and then die; the functioning structure is the wall, not the protoplast. The same logic as xylem — and the same reason a wooden beam works.

The three ground-tissue cells, in wall order

Packing, Corners, Stone — and only the Stone is dead

Three words in order of how much wall has been built, and each one is an English name the tissue already carries. Packing = parenchyma, the packing tissue — a thin wall, and a primary one only; alive, and usually still able to divide. Corners = collenchyma, the corner-thickened cell — thickened unevenly at the corners, and still a primary wall with no lignin; alive, so it can stretch, which is exactly why it supports organs that are still elongating. Stone = sclerenchyma, whose short form is literally the stone cell — thick all the way round, a lignified secondary wall; cannot stretch, so it supports organs that have stopped. Only the Stone is dead: the first two are alive at maturity, the third is not. P, C, S — and the walls build up in that same order, so the sequence cannot be scrambled. Use it as a checker on option text. Any option pairing Corners with a secondary or lignified wall contradicts the word itself — corner thickening is primary-wall thickening — and that is precisely the first sentence quoted in the warning above. What it does not carry: the sclerenchyma split into fibres (long, in bundles — flax, hemp) and sclereids (short, gritty — pear, peach stone), where you meet each cell, and the qualification that sclerenchyma is dead at maturity in most but not quite all cases.

§ 02

Meristems, primary and secondary growth

The ideaAnimals build the whole body once and then mostly stop. Plants never stop: they keep small pockets of permanently embryonic cells and add new body from those pockets for as long as they live. The pockets at the tips add length; the pockets arranged as cylinders down the stem and root add girth. That is the whole of plant growth in two sentences, and it explains why a nail hammered into a trunk stays at the same height forever, why a tree gets fatter every year, and why a palm cannot.

Definition & the two growth modes

Meristem
A tissue of cells that retain the capacity to divide indefinitely. Each division produces one cell that stays meristematic (the initial) and one that goes on to differentiate (the derivative) — so the meristem never exhausts itself.
apical meristem → primary growth (length) · lateral meristem → secondary growth (girth) tips lengthen; cylinders thicken — and only some plants have the cylinders
Full definition, symbols and units
Primary growth
Growth from apical meristems, producing the primary plant body — every tissue of every herbaceous plant, and the young parts of a woody one. All three tissue systems in their primary form arise here.
Secondary growth
Growth in thickness from the two lateral meristems. The vascular cambium produces secondary xylem inward and secondary phloem outward; the cork cambium (phellogen) produces the periderm that replaces the epidermis.
Determinate vs indeterminate
Growth from an apical meristem is indeterminate — it has no built-in endpoint. Leaves, flowers and fruits are determinate: their meristematic activity stops at a defined size. A plant is therefore indeterminate as a whole while being made of determinate parts.

Primary growth: the root tip, zone by zone

The root apex is the cleanest teaching example in plant biology because its zones are laid out in space in the order they occur in time. Right at the tip sits the root cap: a sacrificial helmet of cells that lubricates the advance with mucigel, senses gravity through sedimenting starch grains in specialised statocytes, and is continually shed and replaced from behind. Note what this immediately tells you — the root apical meristem is sub-apical, sitting behind a cap it must also feed, whereas the shoot apical meristem sits genuinely at the tip.

Behind the cap comes the zone of cell division, containing the meristem proper and the quiescent centre. Then the zone of elongation — and this is where the actual advance happens. Cells here may lengthen ten-fold or more by taking up water into a vacuole, not by adding cytoplasm; it is a cheap way to generate force and displacement, and it is what physically pushes the root tip through soil. Finally the zone of differentiation (maturation), where cells take on their final identities and where root hairs appear. Root hairs are absent from the elongating zone for an obvious mechanical reason: a hair anchored in soil while the cell around it is lengthening would simply be torn off.

Two § 02 lists that should not be memorised

The three primary meristems. Protoderm → epidermis. Procambium → primary xylem and primary phloem, because "cambium" is the vascular word in every other context too. Ground meristem → ground tissue, pith and cortex. Each name contains its own product, so there is no mnemonic here and a mnemonic would only compete with the name. The root-tip zones. They are laid out in space in the order a cell meets them in time — born, stretched, finished. Root cap (shed, in front, so the meristem is sub-apical) → zone of division → zone of elongation → zone of differentiation/maturation, with root hairs only in the last one. If you can say why a root hair cannot be built in the elongation zone — it would be torn off a wall that is still lengthening — you have the order and you cannot invert it.

Where lateral roots come from — and why that is not where branches come from

Lateral roots originate endogenously, from the pericycle, deep inside the parent root, and then push out destructively through the cortex and epidermis. Branch shoots originate exogenously, from axillary buds sitting on the surface in leaf axils. The contrast is a favourite one-line question, and there is a functional reason behind it: a lateral root must be plumbed straight into the stele to be any use, and the pericycle is the outermost layer of the stele. Note the trap in the other direction too — the pericycle is inside the endodermis, not outside it.

Secondary growth: how a stem becomes a trunk

Secondary growth begins when a continuous cylinder of vascular cambium forms — partly from procambium left between the primary xylem and primary phloem of each bundle, partly from parenchyma between the bundles that is recruited into the ring. Thereafter each cambial initial divides periclinally (tangentially), and the derivative it leaves on the inside becomes secondary xylem while the one it leaves on the outside becomes secondary phloem.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are examined. First, far more xylem accumulates than phloem — the cambium favours the inward direction, and old secondary phloem is crushed and sloughed away with the outer bark, while secondary xylem is permanent. That is why a tree trunk is essentially a column of wood with a thin skin of functioning phloem, and why wood is secondary xylem. Second, the epidermis cannot keep up with the expanding girth and splits; a cork cambium arises in the outer cortex and lays down cork cells whose walls are impregnated with suberin. Those cells die, and the resulting periderm is the new waterproof covering — punctuated by lenticels, loosely packed regions that leave a gas-exchange path to the living tissue inside.

"Bark" is a bigger word than you think

Bark = all tissues external to the vascular cambium, which means secondary phloem plus periderm — not just the dead outer crust. This is not a pedantic definition; it is the definition on which the girdling experiment of § 09 depends. If you believe bark is only cork, you cannot explain why removing a ring of it kills the tree. And note that the cambium itself is not part of the bark: strip bark cleanly and the cambium is what you have exposed.

Go deeper — reading a tree ring, and heartwood versus sapwood

In a seasonal climate the cambium produces wide, thin-walled vessels in spring — earlywood, optimised for conduction when soil water is plentiful — and narrow, thick-walled cells later in the season — latewood, optimised for strength. The abrupt boundary between one year's latewood and the next year's earlywood is what you see as a ring. One ring per year, therefore, in a climate with one growing season per year. In the wet tropics rings may be absent or unrelated to years, and a drought that stops and restarts growth can produce a false ring, so counting rings is a good estimate rather than a proof.

Ring width records growing conditions, which is the basis of dendrochronology and of the palaeoclimate record. Ring-porous woods (oak, ash) put all their large vessels in the earlywood; diffuse-porous woods (maple, birch) spread them evenly — which is why oak conducts sap at up to about 45 m/h and a conifer, with tracheids only, manages 1–2 m/h.

As a trunk ages, the innermost secondary xylem stops conducting: its vessels embolise permanently and the surrounding parenchyma dies, filling the cells with resins, tannins, gums and pigments. That is heartwood — darker, more decay-resistant, and mechanically the core of the tree, but hydraulically inert. The outer, pale, still-conducting shell is sapwood. A hollow old tree is still perfectly alive, because everything that matters hydraulically is in the outermost few centimetres.

Why a palm trunk is not a tree trunk

Monocots have no vascular cambium, so no true secondary growth. A palm reaches its adult diameter by a primary thickening meristem near the apex before it grows tall, and thereafter the trunk cannot widen; a bamboo culm emerges at final diameter. So: no annual rings, no bark in the technical sense, and no way to repair a girdle. "Woody" and "having secondary growth" are not synonyms — a palm is hard without being woody.

§ 03

Root, stem, leaf — and the monocot split

The ideaEach of the three organs solves a different problem with the same three tissue systems, so each arranges them differently. A root must absorb, so its plumbing is bundled tightly into a central core with a wide absorbing rind around it. A stem must hold the leaves up against bending, so its plumbing is pushed out towards the edge where a beam is strongest. A leaf must catch light and let CO₂ in, so it is flattened into a sheet with air spaces inside. And there is a second axis of variation on top of all that: the flowering plants split early into two groups that lay the same tissues out in different patterns.

Definition & the organ plans

Stele
The vascular cylinder of a root or stem, together with the pericycle that bounds it. In a eudicot root the stele is solid and central; in a stem the vascular tissue is broken into discrete bundles.

Full definition, symbols and units
Endodermis
The innermost layer of the root cortex, one cell thick, whose radial and transverse walls carry the Casparian strip of suberin. It is the gatekeeper of § 05.
Pericycle

Mesophyll

The leaf, read as a gas-exchange organ

A leaf is a compromise device and every feature of it is one side of a trade. The cuticle stops water loss, so CO₂ cannot get in either — hence stomata, which reopen the hole under control. Palisade mesophyll is packed tightly to intercept light, but tightly packed cells impede gas diffusion — hence spongy mesophyll below it, which is up to 30–40 % air space by volume, so that CO₂ can reach a mesophyll surface area several times the external leaf area. The stomata are concentrated on the lower (abaxial) surface of a typical land plant's leaf, which is shaded and cooler, so the vapour gradient across them is smaller.

Two variations on that make good exam items because they follow logically. A floating leaf such as a water lily's has its stomata on the upper surface only, since the lower surface is in water. A fully submerged leaf has none at all, since dissolved CO₂ enters directly and there is nothing to conserve. Neither fact needs to be memorised if you ask what the stoma is for.

Ours, not a standard hook — but this is a genuinely arbitrary character set with no principle behind it, which is exactly where a hook earns its keep. Two exemplars you can picture, four characters each, for the four characters a question can actually show you. Grass is the monocot: bundles scattered through the ground tissue; a fibrous, adventitious root system; parallel venation; floral parts in multiples of three. The rose is the eudicot, and every term is the opposite: bundles in a single ring, which is what lets a continuous vascular cambium form at all; a taproot from the radicle; netted venation; floral parts in four or five — the rose's own five is the anchor, but four counts too. Both clauses run in the same order, so if you can see a grass blade and a rose you have all eight. Three characters left out on purpose, and each is set on its own. Cotyledon number is already in the name. Root anatomy runs opposite to the stem and is the cleanest section discriminator — a monocot root has xylem and phloem alternating around a central pith, a eudicot root has a solid xylem star and no pith. And pollen apertures are one in a monocot but three (tricolpate) in a eudicot: do not let the "threes" in the hook pull you into answering "three" for monocot pollen — that three is the flower.

Go deeper — why "dicot" is not a real group

The flowering plants do not divide into two equal halves. Monocots (Monocotyledoneae) are a genuine clade — all descended from one common ancestor. "Dicots" is simply everything else, which is a paraphyletic grouping: it lumps the true eudicots (roses, legumes, sunflowers, oaks — about three quarters of all angiosperms) together with the magnoliids (magnolia, avocado, black pepper), Amborella, and the water lilies, which branched off before monocots did. Saying "the angiosperms split into monocots and dicots" therefore has the tree wrong.

Eudicots are defined by a single derived character — tricolpate pollen, with three germination furrows — which is why that unglamorous line in the table is the one a systematist cares about. This is note 06's territory, but it shows up in plant-anatomy questions phrased as "which of the following is not a valid taxonomic group", and the answer is dicots.

Modified organs — telling a stem from a root from a leaf

This is pure recall, but it recurs, and there is a principle that makes it recallable: an organ is identified by its structure and origin, not by where it sits or what it looks like. Anything bearing nodes, internodes, axillary buds or scale leaves is a stem, however far underground it is. Anything with a root cap and no nodes is a root, however far above ground it is.

§ 04

Water potential Ψ

The ideaWater does not move because one place is wetter than another, or saltier, or under more pressure. It moves because of a single combined score that folds all of those together, and it always moves from the higher score to the lower one. Dissolving something lowers the score. Squeezing raises it. Once you can write down that score for two places, you can predict the direction of every drop of water in a plant without knowing anything else about it — and you can stop guessing at whether a cell will swell or shrink.

Definition & formula

Water potential Ψ
The free energy of water per unit volume, relative to pure free water at atmospheric pressure and the same temperature and height. Because it is energy per volume it has the units of pressure, and it is quoted in megapascals. The reference state is defined as zero, so almost every biological Ψ is negative.
Ψ = Ψs + Ψp solute potential plus pressure potential; water flows from high Ψ to low Ψ
Full definition, symbols and units
Solute potential Ψs (osmotic potential)
Always zero or negative. Dissolved particles lower the chemical potential of water, so adding solute always makes Ψs more negative. Given by the van 't Hoff relation Ψs = −iCRT, valid for a dilute ideal solution.
Pressure potential Ψp
The hydrostatic pressure, which may be positive or negative. Positive inside a turgid cell (turgor pressure) and inside a loaded sieve tube; strongly negative inside transpiring xylem, where the water is under tension.
The two terms usually dropped
Ψg, the gravitational term, adds +0.01 MPa per metre of height and is ignored below about 10 m but is unavoidable in a tall tree. Ψm, the matric potential, is the binding of water to surfaces — dominant in dry soil, in cell walls and in seeds, and it is always negative.

Three habits make this section safe. First, get the sign convention right and never argue with it. Pure water in an open beaker is the zero. Anything dissolved in it drags Ψ below zero. So the saltier solution has the lower Ψ and water flows towards it — which is the ordinary statement of osmosis, recovered as a special case. Second, water responds to Ψ, not to Ψs. A cell can have a very negative Ψs and still gain no water at all, because a large positive Ψp has cancelled it. That is exactly what a fully turgid cell is: Ψs = −0.9, Ψp = +0.9, Ψ = 0, indistinguishable from pure water as far as water movement is concerned. Third, "concentration" language is a trap in the xylem, where the pressure term dominates completely and there is no membrane for osmosis to happen across at all.

The sign convention and the comparison rule, in one line

Water runs downhill · solute digs the hole · pressure props it up

The first clause is the ordinary English idiom you already own, and it is exactly right once you read downhill as towards lower Ψ: water moves from higher Ψ to lower Ψ, always, and "lower" means more negative. It is the same picture as the staircase figure below. Solute digs the hole — dissolved particles can only drag Ψ downward: Ψs is zero or negative and never positive, so the saltier side is the lower-Ψ side. Pressure props it up — positive hydrostatic pressure pushes Ψ back up, which is why a fully turgid cell at Ψs = −0.9, Ψp = +0.9 sits at Ψ = 0 and gains nothing even from pure water. Three clauses, and between them they contain the three habits above. One place the third clause will mislead you, and it is examined: in the xylem of a transpiring plant Ψp is negative, so there the pressure term digs as well as props. Read props strictly as "positive pressure raises Ψ" and the exception disappears. The line also says nothing about the two terms usually dropped — Ψg, +0.01 MPa per metre of height, and Ψm — which you add only when the stem hands you a tall tree or a dry soil.

You already know this equation from physiology

Ψ = Ψs + Ψp is Starling's equation with the signs rearranged. A capillary drives filtration when hydrostatic pressure exceeds oncotic pressure, and reabsorbs when oncotic wins; a plant cell takes up water when turgor is less than the osmotic pull, and loses it when turgor exceeds it. Plasma albumin is Ψs; capillary hydrostatic pressure is Ψp. Hypoalbuminaemic oedema and a wilting leaf are the same failure — the solute term can no longer hold water against the pressure term. Reading the plant version as the physiology you already have is faster than learning it twice.

Worked example 1 — will this cell swell, shrink, or sit still?

A plant cell has Ψs = −0.70 MPa and a turgor pressure of 0.30 MPa. It is transferred to (a) pure water in an open beaker, and separately to (b) a 0.40 M sucrose solution in an open beaker at 20 °C. Predict the initial direction of water movement in each case, and say where each ends up.

  1. Score the cell first: Ψcell = Ψs + Ψp = −0.70 + 0.30 = −0.40 MPa. This one number is all that matters about the cell.
  2. (a) Pure water, open beaker, so Ψp = 0 and Ψs = 0: Ψsolution = 0.
  3. 0 is greater than −0.40, so water moves into the cell. It keeps entering while Ψcell < 0, and each entering molecule pushes the protoplast harder against the wall, raising Ψp. Movement stops when Ψcell = 0, which requires Ψp ≈ +0.70 MPa. The cell is then at full turgor. Ignoring the slight dilution of the cell sap, turgor has risen from 0.30 to 0.70 MPa.
  4. (b) Sucrose does not dissociate, so i = 1. At 20 °C, T = 293 K: Ψs = −(1)(0.40)(0.00831)(293) = −0.974 MPa, and with the beaker open, Ψp = 0. So Ψsolution = −0.97 MPa.
  5. −0.97 is lower than −0.40, so water leaves the cell. Turgor falls; when Ψp reaches zero the cell is flaccid and Ψcell = Ψs. Water is still leaving, so the protoplast now pulls away from the wall — plasmolysis. It stops when the cell sap has concentrated enough that Ψs reaches −0.97 MPa.
  6. Sanity check on the trap: had you compared solute concentrations alone you would still have got (b) right and (a) right — but you would get the general rule wrong. Put this same cell in a 0.20 M sucrose solution (Ψ = −0.49 MPa) and water still leaves, even though the cell's own Ψs of −0.70 corresponds to a more concentrated interior. Turgor is what decides it.
(a) water in, stops at Ψp = +0.70 MPa · (b) water out, plasmolysis begins once Ψp = 0
Worked example 2 — how thirsty is the air?

At 25 °C, compute the water potential of air at 99 %, 95 % and 50 % relative humidity, and comment on what that means for a leaf sitting at −1.0 MPa.

  1. The prefactor first. RT/V̄w = (8.314 × 298.15) / (18.02 × 10⁻⁶ m³·mol⁻¹) = 1.376 × 10⁸ Pa = 137.6 MPa.
  2. RH = 0.99 → Ψ = 137.6 × ln(0.99) = 137.6 × (−0.01005) = −1.38 MPa.
  3. RH = 0.95 → Ψ = 137.6 × (−0.05129) = −7.06 MPa.
  4. RH = 0.50 → Ψ = 137.6 × (−0.6931) = −95.4 MPa.
  5. Read the consequences. Air that a meteorologist would call "saturated to within one per cent" is already at −1.4 MPa, below a healthy leaf — so a leaf loses water even in a fog. Ordinary room air is a hundred times more negative than any tissue in the plant.
  6. The logarithm is doing the work, and this is the point of the exercise. Halving the humidity from 100 % to 50 % does not halve anything; it takes Ψ from 0 to −95 MPa. There is no realistic atmosphere that is not an overwhelming sink for leaf water.
99 % RH → −1.38 MPa · 95 % → −7.1 MPa · 50 % → −95 MPa · the air always wins

Exam technique · the Ψ item, in four moves and forty seconds

  1. Recognise the type in five seconds — the stem hands you a cell (Ψs and a turgor pressure) and a bathing solution (a molarity, plus the word open or sealed), and asks for direction of movement or for a final Ψp. Nothing conceptual is being tested. Every mark is in bookkeeping, so do not re-read the physiology.
  2. Score each side whole; never compare parts — write two numbers, Ψcell and Ψsolution, each as Ψs + Ψp. Open beaker ⇒ Ψp = 0, so a solution's Ψ is its Ψs. Water then goes to the more negative number. Comparing Ψs with Ψs, or molarity with molarity, is the single commonest way this item is lost, and there is always an option built from exactly that slip.
  3. Ψs = −iCRT, with i counted before anything else — i = 1 for sucrose, glucose and any non-electrolyte; 2 for NaCl and KCl; 3 for CaCl₂ and MgCl₂. T = °C + 273. Then note that RT = 2.43 at 20 °C and 2.48 at 25 °C, so Ψs ≈ −2.5 × i × C done in your head is within about 3 % — always finer than the spacing of five options. Anchor to check the arithmetic against: 0.1 M sucrose at 20 °C = −0.24 MPa.
  4. Bank the saved time by taking the two free eliminations — strike any option that reaches its conclusion by comparing solute concentrations while the two sides differ in pressure; strike any option that says turgor draws water in (pressure raises Ψ, so turgor pushes water out). With one of five gone, answering already beats leaving it blank under −0.25 marking, so this type is never a skip.
  5. Sanity-check against the two end-points — a cell that stops taking up water from pure water has ended at Ψ = 0, so its final Ψp = −Ψs; a cell at incipient plasmolysis has Ψp = 0, so its Ψ = Ψs. Any answer outside that bracket is a sign error, and you can see it without redoing the calculation.

Three sentences about Ψ that are printed in textbooks and still mislead

"Water moves from a region of high water concentration to one of low water concentration." True only when the pressure term is equal on both sides. Put a cell under enough turgor and water leaves it for a solution that is more concentrated than its own contents — the whole point of worked example 1.

"Water potential is the tendency of water to move by osmosis." Osmosis is one mechanism among several. Ψ also predicts bulk flow through the xylem, where there is no membrane; capillary rise, which is a matric effect; and evaporation into air, which is a phase change. It is a thermodynamic bookkeeping quantity, not a description of a mechanism.

"Ψp is the turgor pressure." It is turgor pressure only inside a living walled cell. In the xylem of a transpiring plant Ψp is negative — a tension of one or two megapascals, which is a pressure well below a vacuum. A question that asks for the pressure potential of xylem sap at midday is asking whether you noticed that.

§ 05

Soil to stele: the endodermal checkpoint

The ideaWater entering a root has two ways to travel inward: through the porous walls between the cells, never crossing a membrane; or into the cells and from cell to cell through the tunnels that join them. The first route is fast and free, but it is uncontrolled — anything dissolved in soil water would ride it straight into the plant's plumbing. So the root builds a waterproof seal one cell layer deep, right at the boundary of the plumbing, that forces everything through at least one membrane. That single ring of glue is why a plant can be selective about what it drinks.

Definition & the three routes

Apoplast
The continuum of cell walls, intercellular spaces and dead conducting elements — everything outside the plasma membranes. Water moves through it by bulk flow, driven by pressure, and crosses no membrane, so nothing is selected.
apoplast ⇢ Casparian strip ⇢ symplast ⇢ xylem the strip is what makes the arrow bend — no route into the stele avoids a membrane
Full definition, symbols and units
Symplast
The continuum of cytoplasm, joined cell to cell through plasmodesmata. Entry requires crossing the plasma membrane once, at whichever cell the water first enters; after that, travel is membrane-free.
Transmembrane (transcellular) route
Repeated crossing into and out of successive cells, membrane by membrane. Fast where aquaporin density is high, and the route the plant can regulate minute by minute by gating aquaporins.
Casparian strip
A band of suberin (with lignin) deposited in the radial and transverse (anticlinal) walls of every endodermal cell, and fused to the plasma membrane. It seals the apoplast in the plane of the endodermis the way mortar seals the joints of a brick ring.

Follow one water molecule in from a root hair. It can slip along the porous cellulose walls of the epidermis and cortex without ever meeting a membrane — the apoplast route — and this is genuinely how most of it travels across the cortex, because walls are wide, wet and offer little resistance. Then it reaches the endodermis and the road ends. Suberin fills the radial and transverse walls, welded to the plasma membrane of the endodermal cell, so the only way past is through that cell: across its plasma membrane, into its cytoplasm, and out again on the stele side.

What has the plant bought with this? Selectivity. Every ion that reaches the xylem has been through a transport protein of the endodermal plasma membrane, and the plant therefore decides what it absorbs. Aluminium, sodium in a saline soil, heavy metals — all can be excluded at this checkpoint, and essential ions can be actively accumulated far above their soil concentration. Without the Casparian strip the xylem would simply contain filtered soil solution.

The second job of the strip, which is the one usually missed

The seal works in both directions. Once ions have been pumped into the stele they cannot leak back out through the apoplast, because the same suberin ring blocks the return path. That is what makes root pressure possible: the stele becomes an osmotic compartment whose solute content the plant controls, its Ψ falls, water follows in from the cortex, and because the stele is enclosed the incoming water generates positive hydrostatic pressure in the xylem. A question that asks why root pressure fails in a plant with a damaged endodermis is asking about the return path, not the inward one.

The layer order and the three routes — both name themselves

There is no mnemonic here and building one would cost more than it saved, because the terms are already positional and say so in their own roots. Endo-dermis is "inner skin": the endodermis is the innermost layer of the cortex (內皮層). Peri-cycle is "around the cylinder": the pericycle is the sheath wrapped around the stele and belonging to it — the stele's outermost layer (中柱鞘), and therefore inside the endodermis, never outside it. Given those two names the sequence can only be epidermis → cortex → endodermis → pericycle → stele, and the trap the paper sets here — placing the pericycle outside the endodermis, or crediting the pericycle with the Casparian strip — becomes unstateable. The routes decode the same way: apo- means "away from, outside", so the apoplast is everything outside the plasma membranes, crosses no membrane and selects nothing; sym- means "together", so the symplast is shared cytoplasm, one membrane paid on entry and free travel thereafter. The Casparian strip is then just the plane at which the first is abolished and the second becomes compulsory.

A note on passage cells, which turn up in the more detailed questions. In an older root the endodermis often goes further and deposits a complete suberin lamella over the whole inner face of each cell, sealing it off entirely. A minority of cells — typically those opposite the xylem arms — are left unsuberised. These are the passage cells, and they are where uptake into the stele continues in mature root regions. It is a neat illustration of the general design: the plant restricts a pathway and then leaves deliberate, countable gates in it.

Guttation is not dew, and root pressure is not the answer to tall trees

Guttation is xylem sap pushed out through hydathodes at leaf margins by root pressure, and it happens at night or in the early morning when soil is wet, humidity is high and transpiration has stopped — precisely the conditions under which root pressure builds and has nowhere to discharge. The droplets contain dissolved minerals and sugars; dew is condensed atmospheric water and contains nothing. A stem cut at that moment will bleed, which is the same phenomenon and is how birch and grapevine sap are tapped in spring. (Maple sap is the exception usually miscited here: its flow is driven by stem pressure built up by freeze–thaw cycles in the wood, not by root pressure.)

But root pressure is small — usually 0.05–0.2 MPa, which corresponds to lifting water 5–20 m at absolute best, and it is absent in many conifers and in any plant that is transpiring rapidly, because the xylem is then under tension rather than pressure. Its real biological role is refilling embolised vessels in spring, not raising the transpiration stream. Any answer option that attributes the ascent of sap in a tall tree to root pressure is wrong, and it is wrong for a quantitative reason you can state.

The nephron parallel worth carrying

The endodermis is the plant's tight-junction layer. In a proximal tubule, tight junctions define what may go around cells (paracellular) versus through them (transcellular), and reabsorption is selective because the paracellular route is restricted. The Casparian strip does the identical job in a root: apoplast is paracellular, symplast is transcellular, suberin is the tight junction. And the water channel is literally homologous — plant PIP aquaporins and the human AQP2 of the collecting duct are the same protein family, so nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and a root's hydraulic conductance are variations on one mechanism.

§ 06

Cohesion–tension and the tall tree

The ideaNothing in a plant pushes water upward. Water evaporates from wet walls deep inside a leaf; as it goes, the remaining water is pulled into ever tighter curves in the microscopic pores of those walls, and surface tension turns that curvature into an enormous suction. Because water molecules stick to each other so strongly, the suction is transmitted all the way down an unbroken thread of water to the roots — like pulling on a rope rather than pushing on one. The sun does the work; the plant only decides how wide to open the tap.

Definition & mechanism

Cohesion–tension mechanism
Evaporation from mesophyll cell walls generates negative pressure at curved air–water menisci; the cohesion of water transmits that tension through continuous columns in the xylem; bulk flow follows the pressure gradient from root to leaf. The energy source is solar, not metabolic.
Ψleaf < Ψstem < Ψroot < Ψsoil · and Ψp(xylem) < 0 one continuous descending gradient, with the pressure term negative throughout
Full definition, symbols and units
Cohesion and adhesion
Cohesion is water-to-water hydrogen bonding, which gives a confined water column a tensile strength of tens of megapascals. Adhesion is water-to-wall bonding, which keeps the column attached to the lignified conduit and prevents it slumping.
Meniscus pressure (Young–Laplace)
A curved air–water interface of radius r sustains a pressure difference ΔP = 2γ/r. Because cell-wall pores are of order 5 nm, the menisci there can hold tensions far greater than any a plant actually generates.
Cavitation and embolism
Under excessive tension, or on freezing, dissolved gas comes out of solution and an air bubble breaks the column — cavitation. The gas-filled conduit is an embolism and no longer conducts.

The mechanism is worth stating in the order the events actually occur, because most wrong answers come from getting the order backwards. (1) Water evaporates from the wetted walls of mesophyll cells into the leaf's internal air space. (2) The air–water interfaces retreat into the wall's nanoscale pores and become sharply curved. (3) Surface tension at those curved menisci puts the liquid water behind them under negative pressure. (4) That negative pressure lowers Ψ in the mesophyll wall, which draws water out of the adjacent xylem of the leaf vein. (5) Because the water in the xylem is one continuous, cohering column from leaf to root, the tension propagates down the whole tree within seconds. (6) At the bottom, the lowered Ψ in the root xylem draws water in from the cortex and hence from the soil.

Notice what is not in that list. No pump. No living cell. No ATP. The xylem conduits are dead, which is essential rather than incidental: a living cell's plasma membrane would be torn apart by a tension of two megapascals. This is why a plant killed with a metabolic poison keeps transpiring, and why a cut branch in a vase continues to take up water — facts that would be inexplicable if the ascent were an active process.

Worked example 3 — three ways of raising water, two of which fail

A coast redwood is 100 m tall. Show that (a) atmospheric pressure and (b) capillarity in the vessels cannot account for the ascent, and (c) estimate the tension actually required at the top.

  1. (a) Sucking. A perfect vacuum pump at the top can at best remove all the pressure above the column, leaving atmospheric pressure to push it up. h = Patm/ρg = 101 325 / (1000 × 9.81) = 10.3 m. That is the barometric limit and it applies to any "suction" mechanism at all — including the mercury barometer, and including the reason a hand pump cannot draw from a well deeper than about ten metres. A redwood beats it by a factor of ten, so the water is not being sucked in that sense; it is under genuine negative absolute pressure, a metastable state that only works because the column is confined and free of nucleation sites.
  2. (b) Capillarity. Take a generous vessel radius of 20 µm: h = 2γ cos θ / (ρgr) = (2 × 0.0728) / (1000 × 9.81 × 2×10⁻⁵) = 0.1456 / 0.1962 = 0.74 m. A conifer tracheid at r = 10 µm manages 1.5 m. Capillary rise in the conduits is therefore worth well under a metre and is irrelevant at tree scale.
  3. Where capillarity does matter is the cell wall, where the pores are of order 5 nm. ΔP = 2γ/r = (2 × 0.0728)/(5×10⁻⁹) = 2.91×10⁷ Pa = 29 MPa. The meniscus in a mesophyll wall can hold roughly thirty megapascals of tension — an order of magnitude more than any tree ever demands. That is the real function of the wall pores: not to lift water, but to be the anchor from which the whole column hangs.
  4. (c) The requirement. Gravity alone costs ρgh = 1000 × 9.81 × 100 = 9.81×10⁵ Pa = 0.98 MPa, i.e. the 0.01 MPa per metre in the quick sheet. Add frictional loss through 100 m of conduit — comparable in magnitude — and the soil's own Ψ of about −0.05 MPa, and the leaf xylem at the top must sit near −2 MPa. Measured values in tall conifers are close to this, which is one of the reasons the cohesion–tension theory is accepted.
  5. Note the consequence for tree height: gravity imposes a fixed 0.01 MPa·m⁻¹ toll whether the tree is transpiring or not, so the tallest trees have leaves permanently near their cavitation limit and measurably smaller, more water-stressed leaves at the top than at the bottom.

Exam technique · "what raises water up a tall tree" is a ten-second question

  1. Recognise the stem — a height in tens of metres, a redwood or a sequoia, or the phrase "ascent of sap". You are not being asked to reason. You are being asked whether you can kill three named distractors, and they are the same three every time the item appears.
  2. Kill each with its number, not with an argument — root pressure, 0.05–0.2 MPa, is worth 5–20 m and is absent in a transpiring plant because the xylem is then in tension; capillarity in a 20 µm vessel gives 0.74 m; atmospheric pressure or "suction" is capped at 10.3 m, the barometric ceiling for any mechanism whatever. Notice that the stem specified a height precisely so that each of these fails numerically — that is the tell.
  3. Recognise the survivor by its vocabulary — the correct option contains evaporation, curved menisci or air–water interfaces, cohesion, and an unbroken column. If two options both invoke cohesion–tension, take the one that generates the tension in the mesophyll cell walls of the leaf, not at the root, not "in the xylem" unlocated.
  4. Apply the free filter to any xylem option anywhere on the paper — xylem transport spends no plant ATP and involves no living cell. So "pump", "active transport", "energy from respiration" or "living xylem cells" makes an option wrong before you finish reading it. Hold the mirror image at the same time: an option in which a metabolic poison stops transpiration is wrong; one in which it stops translocation is right.
  5. The dimensional check that costs nothing — gravity alone is 0.01 MPa per metre. A 100 m tree therefore owes ≈1 MPa to gravity before any friction at all, so a leaf near −2 MPa at the top is the right order of magnitude and an option offering −0.2 MPa or −50 MPa is not.

The evidence, in the form the paper likes

Trunk diameter shrinks during the day and recovers at night. If the xylem were pressurised the trunk would swell in the daytime. It narrows, measurably, because tension pulls the conduit walls inward — direct evidence of negative pressure. This is the single most quotable piece of support for cohesion–tension.

The Scholander pressure chamber. Seal a shoot in a chamber with the cut stem protruding, raise the gas pressure until sap just appears at the cut surface; the applied pressure equals the tension that was in the xylem. It works, and it gives numbers consistent with the calculation above.

Killing the plant does not stop the flow. Steam-killing a length of stem, or poisoning it, leaves transpiration running. Chilling or poisoning the phloem, by contrast, stops sugar transport within minutes (§ 09). The contrast between those two experiments is a favourite way of asking whether you know which tissue is alive.

Go deeper — cavitation, and how a conifer survives a hard winter

Water under 2 MPa of tension is metastable: thermodynamically it "wants" to become vapour, and only the absence of a nucleation site prevents it. Introduce a bubble — through a wound, through drought pushing air across a pit membrane, or through freezing, which expels dissolved gas because ice excludes it — and the column snaps. The tension vanishes locally and the conduit fills with water vapour and air. You can hear this happening: drought-stressed stems emit ultrasonic acoustic emissions, one per cavitation event.

The structural defence is compartmentalisation. Conduits are finite in length, and water passes between them only through pits whose pit membrane is unlignified primary wall. That membrane is wettable and its pores are small enough that surface tension holds an air–water meniscus in place: air cannot cross until the tension exceeds the "air-seeding" threshold of the largest pore. So an embolism is trapped in the conduit where it started. Conifers do better still — their bordered pits carry a torus–margo valve, a thickened disc suspended on a porous mesh, which is pushed across and seals the pit aperture the moment the pressure difference across it becomes large. A pine effectively closes a hatch on each dead compartment.

This is the deeper reason the vessel/tracheid trade-off matters. Freeze–thaw cavitation risk scales steeply with conduit diameter, because a wide conduit freezes with larger bubbles that fail to redissolve on thawing. Hence conifers dominate cold and high-altitude forests with narrow tracheids and a valve, while wide-vesselled ring-porous angiosperms such as oak simply write off last year's earlywood and build a new ring each spring — which is why their vessels are all in the earlywood in the first place.

§ 07

Stomata: the guard cell as an osmotic machine

The ideaA stoma is a hole with two sausages around it. To open the hole the plant does not pull the sausages apart — it inflates them. Because each sausage is hooped with inextensible bands like a garden hose, and because the wall facing the hole is stiffer than the wall facing away, inflating it makes it bow outwards rather than fatten, and the hole opens. Everything else about stomatal physiology is the story of how the cell gets water to come in, and the answer is always the same: it makes its inside osmotically attractive by loading it with ions.

Definition & the opening sequence

Stoma (pl. stomata)
A pore in the epidermis bounded by two guard cells, together with any adjacent subsidiary cells. Guard cells are the only epidermal cells with substantial chloroplasts, and unlike other epidermal cells they have no plasmodesmatal connection to their neighbours at maturity — they are osmotically isolated, which is what lets them behave independently.
blue light → H⁺-ATPase → hyperpolarise → K⁺ in → Ψs ↓ → H₂O in → turgor ↑ → open the causal chain, in order; the pump moves protons, never potassium
Full definition, symbols and units
Radial micellation
The cellulose microfibrils of the guard-cell wall run as hoops around the cell's circumference, so the cell cannot increase in girth, only in length. With the two ends attached to each other and the inner (ventral) wall thicker and less extensible, the lengthening cell can only bow outwards — and the pore opens.
The three opening signals
Blue light, sensed by phototropins PHOT1/PHOT2 which phosphorylate and activate the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase; low intercellular CO₂, which is the leaf's own signal that photosynthesis is outrunning supply; and the circadian clock, which opens stomata before dawn in anticipation.
The closing signal
Abscisic acid (ABA), made in roots and in leaf tissue under water stress. It binds PYR/PYL receptors, which inhibit PP2C phosphatases, releasing SnRK2 kinases, which activate the anion channel SLAC1. Anions leave, the membrane depolarises, outward K⁺ channels open, and turgor collapses.

Why the pump moves protons and not potassium

This is the step most often garbled, and it is worth understanding rather than memorising. The guard cell's plasma membrane carries a P-type H⁺-ATPase — the plant equivalent of the animal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Blue light, via phototropin, causes it to be phosphorylated and activated. It exports protons, which does two things at once: it acidifies the wall space, and, because it moves charge, it drives the membrane potential down to as much as −200 mV. That hyperpolarisation is the actual signal. Voltage-gated inward-rectifying K⁺ channels (KAT1, a Shaker-family channel) open only when the membrane is sufficiently negative, and K⁺ then floods in passively, down an electrical gradient the pump created.

So potassium accumulation is secondary active transport: no ATP is spent on K⁺ itself, only on H⁺. Chloride follows, co-transported with protons running back down their gradient; and the guard cell manufactures its own organic anion, malate²⁻, by breaking down stored starch. Every one of those species lowers Ψs, and water follows.

The magnitude is worth a moment. Going from about 100 mM to about 500 mM of a monovalent salt takes the osmolarity from roughly 0.2 to 1.0 osmol·L⁻¹. At 20 °C that is Ψs: −0.49 → −2.43 MPa, a change of nearly 2 MPa. Guard cells run at turgor pressures several times those of ordinary cells, which is why their walls are so heavily built.

The opening chain, in the order the examiner will scramble it

BLUE opens it — Blue light → Lose H⁺ → Uptake K⁺ → Enter H₂O

Ours, not a standard hook — but the word is itself the first step, and the four letters fix the sequence, which is the whole of what this item tests: U cannot come before L, so potassium can never precede protons. Blue light, through phototropin PHOT1/PHOT2, phosphorylates and activates the P-type H⁺-ATPase. Lose H⁺: the pump exports protons, which hyperpolarises the membrane towards −200 mV — and that voltage, not the acid, is the signal. Uptake of K⁺: the inward rectifier KAT1 opens and K⁺ enters passively down the electrical gradient the pump made — secondary active transport, with no ATP spent on potassium. Enter H₂O: K⁺ with its counter-ions drops Ψs by of order 1.5 MPa and water follows; turgor rises, the radially hooped cell can only lengthen, and it bows away from the pore. It carries the blue-light route only. The other two opening signals — low intercellular CO₂ and the circadian clock — are not in it, and low CO₂ is the one the paper likes, because it opens stomata in darkness. It also does not name the counter-ions: Cl⁻ is imported, malate²⁻ is manufactured in situ from stored starch. And closing is not this chain run backwards — closing is ABA ⊣ PP2C ⊣ SnRK2 → SLAC1 → anions out first → depolarise → GORK → K⁺ out.

The textbook sugar story is not wrong, but it is not the mechanism either

Older accounts say guard cells open because photosynthesis in their chloroplasts makes sugar, which lowers Ψs. Guard cells do have chloroplasts — unusually, since ordinary epidermal cells do not — but the chloroplasts' main early contribution is starch degradation supplying carbon skeletons for malate, not net photosynthetic sugar. The rapid morning opening is a K⁺ and anion event; sucrose does accumulate and becomes the dominant osmoticum in the afternoon, by which time K⁺ has already declined. An option that says "guard cells open when photosynthesis raises their sugar concentration" is describing the late phase of a mechanism whose trigger is something else.

Similarly, "stomata open in the light so the plant can photosynthesise" is teleology standing in for a mechanism. The proximate signals are blue light on phototropin and a fall in the intercellular CO₂ concentration. That second signal is the important one to be able to use: a leaf in darkness will still open its stomata if you flush the intercellular spaces with CO₂-free air, and a leaf in bright light will close them if you raise CO₂ — which is why rising atmospheric CO₂ is measurably reducing stomatal conductance and stomatal density in modern plants.

The order-of-events question

The commonest stomatal item on a paper of this type gives five steps and asks for the correct sequence. Two orderings are wrong in ways that look right: putting water entry before ion entry (water is always the last thing to move — it follows Ψ, it never leads), and putting K⁺ uptake before H⁺ export (the proton pump creates the driving force; potassium is a passenger). If you can only remember one thing, remember that the proton pump is step one and water is step last.

Exam technique · answering a five-step sequence without reading five steps

  1. Read only the first item of each option. A correct opening sequence begins with blue light or the H⁺-ATPase. An option that begins with K⁺, with water, or with photosynthesis is already dead. That normally leaves two, and you have spent eight seconds.
  2. Then apply the water rule. Water never leads. Any option in which water moves before the solutes it is following is wrong — and this single rule kills a distractor in § 04, in § 07 and in § 09, so it is worth more than anything else on this page.
  3. Then the ATP rule. The pump moves protons. An option saying K⁺ is pumped in by primary active transport, or that ATP is spent on K⁺, is wrong even though it has the direction right — it is written to be picked by a reader who remembers "potassium" as the headline of the topic.
  4. Recognise the sugar distractor by the time of day. "Guard-cell photosynthesis makes sucrose, which lowers Ψs" is textbook prose lifted almost verbatim, and it is true of the afternoon, not of dawn. If the stem says "at dawn", "on illumination" or "rapid opening", the sugar option is the trap; K⁺ and anions are the trigger and sucrose is the late phase.
  5. On a closing stem, look for anions before potassium. ABA acts on SLAC1, so Cl⁻ and malate²⁻ leave first and depolarise the membrane; only then does GORK let K⁺ out. An option that closes the stoma by "K⁺ efflux causing depolarisation" has the causal arrow reversed and is the standard mirror of the opening error.
  6. Where the time goes. Sequence items are worth fifteen seconds, not sixty-six, provided you work from step 1 of each option rather than reading each chain end to end. Reading five full chains is how a recognition question turns into a two-minute one.
Go deeper — why guard cells are the favourite cell of plant signalling research

A guard cell is a single cell with a visible, quantifiable output — the pore aperture, measurable under a microscope in micrometres — that integrates light quality, CO₂, humidity, temperature, the circadian clock, hormone signals from a distant organ and pathogen-associated molecular patterns. It is, in effect, a whole signalling network with a built-in readout, and the entire ABA core was worked out on it.

The ABA core has an elegant logic: the receptor is a negative regulator of a negative regulator. In the absence of ABA, PP2C phosphatases sit on the SnRK2 kinases and keep them dephosphorylated and inactive. ABA binds a PYR/PYL receptor, and the receptor–ABA complex binds and inhibits the PP2C. Freed from the phosphatase, SnRK2 autophosphorylates, becomes active, and phosphorylates SLAC1 and the NADPH oxidase RBOHF. Anions pour out of the guard cell, the membrane depolarises, GORK carries K⁺ out after them, and the cell deflates. The whole thing was reconstituted in vitro from purified components — receptor, phosphatase, kinase, channel — which is why it is cited as the cleanest hormone-signalling pathway known in plants.

There is also a genuine cross-disciplinary hook: KAT1 and GORK are Shaker-family voltage-gated potassium channels, structurally homologous to the Kv channels that repolarise a cardiac action potential. The same protein architecture that opens a stoma at dawn terminates a ventricular myocyte's plateau — a reminder that ion-channel physiology is one subject with several literatures.

§ 08

Transpiration and the trade it cannot avoid

The ideaA land plant needs carbon dioxide, and the only way to get it is to open holes in an otherwise waterproof skin. Water immediately escapes through the same holes — far faster than CO₂ comes in, because the outward gradient is a hundred times steeper. Every plant on land therefore lives with a forced exchange rate: a few hundred molecules of water lost for each molecule of carbon gained. Nothing about transpiration is a design feature; it is the unavoidable bill for photosynthesis on dry land, and most plant adaptations are attempts to lower the price.

Definition & the exchange rate

Transpiration
Evaporative loss of water vapour from a plant, overwhelmingly through the stomata. Stomatal pores cover about 1–2 % of a leaf's surface yet account for more than 90 % of its water loss; the cuticle accounts for the small remainder.
E / A = 1.6 × ΔwΔc water lost per CO₂ fixed; the 1.6 is how much faster water vapour diffuses in air
Full definition, symbols and units
Why 1.6
Both gases cross the same pore by diffusion, so both are proportional to stomatal conductance. Water is the lighter, smaller molecule and its diffusion coefficient in air is 1.6 times that of CO₂, so a pore that is open enough to admit one mole of CO₂ lets out 1.6 times as much water for a given gradient. The factor is fixed by physics and cannot be engineered away.
Water-use efficiency (WUE)
Carbon fixed per water lost — the inverse of the ratio above. The old agronomic version is the transpiration ratio, grams of water transpired per gram of dry matter accumulated: roughly 500 for C3, 250 for C4 and 50 for CAM.
Vapour pressure deficit (VPD)
The difference between the saturated vapour pressure at leaf temperature and the actual vapour pressure of the air. It, not relative humidity alone, is what drives evaporation — and it rises steeply with temperature, which is why a hot day costs far more water than a merely dry one.

Take the two gradients seriously and the whole subject follows. Inside the leaf, the intercellular air space sits against wet cell walls and is effectively saturated — at 25 °C that is a water vapour mole fraction of about 0.031, or 31 000 ppm. Outside, at 50 % humidity, it is about 15 600 ppm. The outward gradient for water is therefore around 15 000 ppm. For CO₂, ambient air offers about 420 ppm and the leaf interior sits near 280 ppm in a C3 plant, so the inward gradient is a mere 140 ppm — about a hundredfold smaller. Multiply by the 1.6 diffusivity factor and you have the exchange rate. There is no cleverness available: the same hole serves both fluxes.

Worked example 4 — what does a molecule of CO₂ actually cost?

A C3 leaf at 25 °C sits in air at 25 °C. Ca = 420 ppm, Ci = 280 ppm. Compute the water lost per CO₂ fixed at (a) 50 % RH and (b) 20 % RH. Then, for a plant with 0.6 m² of leaf transpiring at 4 mmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ for 12 h, compute the daily water budget.

  1. Internal vapour mole fraction: saturated vapour pressure at 25 °C is 3.17 kPa, so wi = 3.17 / 101.3 = 0.0313.
  2. (a) At 50 % RH, wa = 0.0313 × 0.50 = 0.01565, so Δw = 0.01565. And Δc = (420 − 280) × 10⁻⁶ = 1.40 × 10⁻⁴.
  3. Δw/Δc = 0.01565 / 1.40×10⁻⁴ = 111.8, and multiplying by 1.6 gives 179 mol H₂O per mol CO₂.
  4. (b) At 20 % RH, Δw = 0.0313 − 0.00626 = 0.02503, so Δw/Δc = 178.8 and the cost is 286 mol H₂O per mol CO₂. Drying the air by 30 percentage points raised the bill by 60 % with no change in the plant at all.
  5. Daily budget. 4 × 10⁻³ × 0.6 × (12 × 3600) = 103.7 mol H₂O = 103.7 × 18.02 = 1868 g ≈ 1.9 L per day. Over a 100-day season that is roughly 190 L — consistent with the classic measurement that a single maize plant transpires of order 100–200 L in a season.
  6. Carbon gained, at the 179 mol/mol rate: 103.7 / 179 = 0.58 mol CO₂, which is about 0.58 × 30 = 17 g of carbohydrate. Seventeen grams of sugar for nearly two litres of water — and the plant is a fully functioning, well-watered specimen. That is the trade.
  7. The non-obvious corollary. Halving stomatal conductance halves E immediately, but it does not halve A: with the stomata narrowed, the Calvin cycle keeps drawing CO₂ down, so Ci falls, Δc widens, and assimilation is partly defended. Partial closure therefore improves water-use efficiency. That is the whole logic of midday stomatal closure and of ABA signalling — not shutting down, but trading a little carbon for a lot of water.
179 mol H₂O/mol CO₂ at 50 % RH · 286 at 20 % RH · ≈1.9 L per plant per day · ≈17 g carbohydrate

Exam technique · the water-cost calculation, and the three ways it is thrown away

  1. Run the same five moves every time. (i) wi = saturated vapour pressure at leaf temperature ÷ 101.3 — at 25 °C that is 3.17/101.3 = 0.0313, and the stem usually hands it to you. (ii) Δw = wi × (1 − RH). (iii) Δc = (Ca − Ci) × 10⁻⁶. (iv) divide. (v) multiply by 1.6. Write the chain down before looking at the options.
  2. Slip one, and it is the commonest: the 1.6 is forgotten. The un-multiplied answer is planted as an option every single time. Before committing, check that your number is the larger of two candidates on the list by a factor of roughly 1.6 — if it is the smaller one, you are the one who dropped it.
  3. Slip two: the outside air treated as dry. If you used Δw = wi you ignored the RH the stem gave you. That is what the conspicuously large option is made of.
  4. Slip three: answering with a transpiration ratio. 500 (C3), 250 (C4), 50 (CAM) are grams of water per gram of dry matter — not moles per mole, and not the same calculation. If the stem asks for mol H₂O per mol CO₂ and one of those three numbers is on the list, it is bait.
  5. Order-of-magnitude check. A well-watered C3 leaf in ordinary air costs a few hundred water per CO₂ — about 180 at 50 % RH, about 290 at 20 % RH. An answer near 20, or near 5000, is not a rounding error but a dropped or duplicated factor, and you can see that without redoing anything.
  6. If the stem is qualitative, drop the equation and use the two gradients. Anything that widens Δw raises transpiration; only stomatal aperture moves both fluxes together. That is why temperature beats humidity whenever both are offered — temperature raises wi steeply and non-linearly, so a hot humid day can still carry a high VPD, and an option that treats relative humidity as the whole story is the intended miss.

Transpiration is a cost, not a purpose

You will see it written that plants transpire "in order to" cool the leaf, or "in order to" pull up minerals. Both put the causation backwards. Evaporative cooling is a genuine and useful consequence — a leaf in full sun can be several degrees cooler than it would otherwise be, and leaf temperature does rise sharply when stomata close — but a plant does not open stomata to cool itself, and it will close them and overheat rather than dehydrate.

The mineral-uptake version is more clearly wrong. Mineral uptake happens at the root and is active and selective (§ 05); transpiration only affects the rate at which already-absorbed ions are swept to the shoot. Plants growing in saturated air with essentially no transpiration still accumulate minerals perfectly well, and seedlings in a sealed humid chamber grow. If transpiration were the mechanism of uptake, they could not.

The adaptations follow directly from the equation, and it is worth being able to derive rather than list them. Reduce conductance: thick cuticles, fewer stomata, stomata sunk into pits, stomata lined with trichomes, leaves rolled so the stomatal surface faces an enclosed humid chamber (the bulliform-cell mechanism in grasses). Reduce Δw at the leaf surface: hairs and a thick boundary layer, reflective surfaces to hold leaf temperature down, small or dissected leaves in hot dry air where a thin boundary layer allows convective cooling instead. Or change the timing and the biochemistry entirely, which is what C4 and CAM do — CAM opening stomata only at night, when Δw is at its smallest, and buying a transpiration ratio around 50 for the cost of storing carbon as malate overnight. That is note 09's material, but it is the same equation being solved.

§ 09

Phloem: pressure flow, source to sink

The ideaThe sugar pipe works the opposite way round from the water pipe. Where the xylem is pulled from the top, the phloem is pushed from wherever sugar is being made. Loading sugar into one end of a tube makes water rush in after it and the pressure there shoot up; taking sugar out at the other end lets water leave and the pressure there fall. A pressure difference along a tube full of liquid moves everything in it, sugar included, at whatever speed the difference allows. The plant never pumps the sap — it only pumps sugar, at the two ends, and lets the water do the rest.

Definition & mechanism

Pressure-flow (Münch mass-flow) hypothesis
Active loading of sugar at a source lowers Ψs in the sieve tube; water enters from the adjacent xylem, raising Ψp. Unloading at a sink reverses this locally. Sap then moves by bulk flow down the resulting hydrostatic pressure gradient, carrying its solutes with it. The water is returned to the xylem at the sink.
load at source → Ψp ↑ · unload at sink → Ψp ↓ · ΔΨp ⇒ bulk flow the flow is passive; only the loading and unloading cost energy
Full definition, symbols and units
Source and sink
A source is any organ that is a net exporter of sugar — a mature photosynthesising leaf, or a storage organ that is mobilising its reserves. A sink is a net importer: a root, a fruit, a shoot tip, a developing leaf. The roles are not fixed: a potato tuber is a sink all summer and a source the following spring, and a young leaf is a sink until it is roughly half expanded.
Apoplastic loading
Sucrose exits the mesophyll into the apoplast (SWEET transporters), then is loaded into the companion cell / sieve element by a sucrose–H⁺ symporter (SUC/SUT) driven by the proton gradient the H⁺-ATPase maintains. Secondary active transport — sucrose is concentrated 2–3 orders of magnitude above the mesophyll.
Symplastic (polymer-trap) loading
Used by many trees. Sucrose diffuses through plasmodesmata into intermediary cells and is there converted to raffinose and stachyose, which are too large to diffuse back through the same plasmodesmata. A one-way valve made of molecular size rather than of pumps.

Worked example 5 — where does the pressure come from?

A sieve tube in a source leaf holds 0.50 M sucrose; the xylem beside it is at Ψ = −0.50 MPa. In the root, the sieve tube holds 0.10 M sucrose and the local xylem is at Ψ = −0.15 MPa. Temperature 20 °C. Find the turgor pressure at each end and the pressure difference driving flow.

  1. Sucrose does not dissociate, so i = 1 and T = 293 K. At the source: Ψs = −(0.50)(0.00831)(293) = −1.22 MPa.
  2. Water exchanges freely between the sieve tube and the neighbouring xylem, so at steady state their water potentials are equal: Ψsieve = Ψxylem = −0.50 MPa.
  3. Therefore Ψp = Ψ − Ψs = −0.50 − (−1.22) = +0.72 MPa. The turgor was not "pumped in" — it is what is left over once osmosis has equalised the water potentials.
  4. At the sink: Ψs = −(0.10)(0.00831)(293) = −0.24 MPa, and Ψp = −0.15 − (−0.24) = +0.09 MPa.
  5. ΔΨp = 0.717 − 0.093 = 0.62 MPa from leaf to root. That is the entire motive force, and it is generated by nothing more than a fivefold difference in sucrose concentration between the two ends of a tube.
  6. Now test the mechanism. Poison the companion cells at the source so loading stops. Sucrose in the sieve tube falls, Ψs rises towards zero, water leaves back into the xylem, Ψp collapses — and flow stops within minutes, even though the tube is undamaged and the xylem is working normally. This is precisely what chilling a short length of petiole, or applying a metabolic inhibitor, is observed to do; and it is the sharpest contrast with the xylem, which keeps transporting in a dead stem.

What girdling actually demonstrates

Cut a complete ring of bark from around a trunk, down to but not through the vascular cambium. Because bark is everything external to the cambium (§ 02), you have removed the secondary phloem all the way round while leaving every xylem conduit intact. Then watch, and be precise about what happens and when.

Immediately: nothing. The leaves do not wilt. Water continues to rise through the untouched xylem, transpiration continues, and the canopy looks healthy for weeks. This is the observation that carries the argument — if sugar and water travelled in the same tissue, or if water travelled in the bark, the shoot would collapse at once. Within days: a swelling develops on the upper lip of the cut, where sugars arriving from the leaves have nowhere to go and accumulate as starch, and where the resulting high sink strength stimulates local growth. Within weeks to months: the roots, cut off from all photosynthate, exhaust their reserves, stop growing and die. Only then, with no functioning root system, does the shoot finally wilt and the tree die.

The four conclusions a girdling question is testing

(1) Sugars move in the phloem, water in the xylem — two separate pipes. (2) Sugar movement in a stem is predominantly downward, from leaf to root, since it is the tissue below the ring that starves. (3) Roots are heterotrophic and entirely dependent on shoot photosynthate. (4) Phloem requires tissue continuity: a gap of a few centimetres in a tube that has no pump cannot be bridged.

The commonest wrong answer is that the tree wilts immediately or that the leaves die first. The second commonest is that swelling appears below the cut. Both are the same error — assuming the phloem carries water, or assuming the direction of flow is upward. Note also the practical corollary exploited in horticulture: girdling a single fruiting branch traps sugar above the ring and produces larger fruit on that branch, at the cost of eventually killing it.

What a girdled trunk does, and in what order

Swell above, starve below, wilt last

Ours, not a standard hook, but six words carry the three things this item is always set on — and they run in time order, days to months, which is the second half of what is being asked. Swell above: the swelling forms on the upper lip of the ring, because sugar travels downward from the leaves and piles up against the gap. Starve below: everything below the ring — above all the roots — is cut off from photosynthate and starves, because roots are heterotrophic and hold no reserve that lasts. Wilt last: the canopy is the last thing to fail, weeks to months later, and only because the root system has died first; the xylem was never touched, so water rose normally the whole time. Aimed squarely at the two commonest wrong answers — "the leaves wilt within hours" (which assumes bark carries water) and "the swelling forms below the ring" (which assumes phloem sap moves up). It does not encode why a girdle is fatal at all: for that you need bark = everything external to the vascular cambium from § 02. Nor the horticultural corollary, that girdling one fruiting branch traps sugar above the ring and enlarges the fruit on it.

Then test it against that one property. Most wrong options across § 01, § 06 and § 09 are a perfectly true statement about the other pipe, which is exactly why they read so well.
  2. Four sentence shapes that are always wrong here — "active transport of sap along the sieve tube" (there is no motor in the tube, and the sieve element discarded the machinery that could have been one); "the phloem carries water up to the leaves"; "xylem transport requires ATP"; and "sap moves up and down the same sieve tube simultaneously" (one pressure gradient, therefore one direction — bidirectionality belongs to the plant, not to a tube). Each can be struck without reading the rest of the stem.
  3. Use the poison-and-chilling contrast as a discriminator. Steam-kill or poison a length of stem and the transpiration stream keeps running; chill a few centimetres of petiole and translocation stops within minutes. When a stem describes an experimental manipulation and asks what follows, first decide which pipe it touched.
  4. Sucrose, and the three reasons — because the wrong reason is an option. Non-reducing, so it cannot glycate anything in transit; metabolically inert until an invertase or sucrose synthase acts on it; two hexoses per osmotically active particle. An option justifying sucrose as "the immediate product of photosynthesis" names the right sugar with false biochemistry — the immediate product is triose phosphate — and a half-right option is still wrong.
  5. Where the time goes. The pressure-flow arithmetic deserves the full 66 seconds, because it is short and unambiguous: Ψs from the molarity, Ψ taken from the neighbouring xylem, then Ψp = Ψ − Ψs. Girdling, aphid stylets and source-versus-sink identification should be answered in fifteen — they are recognition, not reasoning. If you are still deliberating on one of those at forty seconds, you have misread which pipe the stem is about; re-read the stem rather than the options.

Three plausible-sounding phloem sentences to reject

"Phloem transport is bidirectional: sap can move up and down the same sieve tube simultaneously." The first half is right at the level of the whole plant — in spring a tuber exports upward while leaves export downward — but within one sieve tube at one time there is one pressure gradient and therefore one direction. Bulk flow cannot be two-way in a single tube.

"Sugars are actively transported along the sieve tube from source to sink." No. Loading and unloading are active; the transit is passive bulk flow. A sieve element has no nucleus, no ribosomes and very few mitochondria — it is not equipped to pump anything along its own length.

"Sucrose is used because it is the product of photosynthesis." The immediate product is triose phosphate, and starch and glucose are commoner storage forms. Sucrose is chosen for three specific reasons: it is a non-reducing disaccharide, so its carbonyl groups are tied up in the glycosidic bond and it cannot glycate proteins or be attacked in transit; it is metabolically inert until an invertase or sucrose synthase acts on it, so it is not consumed en route; and it delivers two hexoses per osmotically active particle, which for a system that runs on osmotic pressure is exactly the efficiency you want.

One more piece of evidence, because it is elegant and it is examinable. Let an aphid insert its stylet into a single sieve tube, then anaesthetise the insect and cut the stylet off. Sap continues to exude from the cut stump on its own, sometimes for days. That exudation under its own pressure is direct proof that the sieve tube is positively pressurised, and the rate of exudation is highest near a source and lowest near a sink — which is the pressure gradient itself, measured. Combine that with 14CO₂ fed to one leaf and autoradiography of the whole plant, and the source-to-sink pattern of translocation can be watched directly.

§ 10

Mineral nutrition and deficiency

The ideaAlmost all of a plant's dry weight comes out of the air and out of water, not out of the soil — the soil supplies a short list of elements in tiny amounts, and every one of them is indispensable. When one runs short, the plant does something very revealing: if it can move the element internally, it strips it out of its old leaves to keep the new ones going, and the old leaves are the ones that show the damage. If it cannot move it, the old leaves keep what they have and the new growth suffers instead. So the position of a symptom on the plant tells you as much as its appearance.

Definition & the list

Essential element
An element that satisfies the Arnon–Stout criteria: the plant cannot complete its life cycle without it, and no other element can substitute for it. Note that this is defined by an experiment — hydroponic culture in a defined solution with one element omitted — not by whether the element is found in plant tissue.

Macronutrient vs micronutrient
The distinction is quantity required, not importance. Macronutrients are needed in relatively large amounts (≥0.1 % of dry mass); micronutrients in trace amounts, and mostly as enzyme cofactors, where a few atoms turn over substrate repeatedly. A plant dies just as certainly without molybdenum as without nitrogen.
Beneficial (but not essential) elements
Sodium — essential for C4 and CAM species only; silicon — strengthens grass and horsetail walls and deters herbivores; cobalt — essential to the rhizobia in a nodule, and therefore indirectly to a legume, but not to plants in general. These are the classic "which of these is not essential" answers.

Start with the single most misread fact in the whole topic. A plant is roughly 96 % carbon, oxygen and hydrogen by dry mass, and all of that came from CO₂ and water. The soil contributes about four per cent. Van Helmont's willow experiment — a tree that gained 74 kg while the soil in its pot lost 60 g — is usually taught as showing that plants are "made of water"; what it really shows is that they are not made of soil, and the carbon he could not account for came from the air. An exam option claiming that soil minerals are the main source of plant biomass is drawing on an intuition almost everyone has and almost nobody has checked.

The one rule that makes deficiency diagnosis derivable

A plant with a limited supply of a nutrient behaves like an organism triaging: it invests in the tissue with the future. If an element can be loaded into the phloem and moved, the plant strips it out of mature leaves and sends it to the growing tip. The old leaves are sacrificed, so they show the symptom. If the element cannot be retranslocated — because it is locked into a structure, or has no phloem transport system — the old leaves keep theirs and the new growth is starved.

Old leaves or young leaves — read it as a mobility test

Symptoms in OLD leaves → the element is phloem-mobile: N, P, K, Mg, Cl, Mo. Symptoms in YOUNG leaves → phloem-immobile: Ca, B, Fe, S, Mn, Zn, Cu. A useful check on any answer you are unsure of: calcium is built into the middle lamella as calcium pectate and boron into wall cross-links, so neither can be extracted from a finished wall — hence both kill the apical meristem first. Nitrogen and magnesium are in chlorophyll and protein, which the plant dismantles routinely — hence both show in old leaves.

Mg versus Fe — the item that comes back every year

Both produce interveinal chlorosis with green veins. They are told apart by which leaves, and the reasoning is worth being able to reconstruct rather than recall. Magnesium is in chlorophyll and is phloem-mobile, so the plant cannibalises old leaves → lower, older leaves. Iron is a cofactor for chlorophyll synthesis and is phloem-immobile once delivered, so it cannot be recovered → upper, younger leaves.

The clinical-flavoured version: iron chlorosis is common on calcareous, high-pH soils even when total soil iron is abundant, because Fe³⁺ hydroxides are essentially insoluble above about pH 7. Adding iron does not fix it; acidifying the soil or supplying a chelate does. The same chemistry underlies iron handling in mammals — free Fe³⁺ is insoluble and toxic, so it must be chelated (transferrin in us, phytosiderophores and reductase-based strategies in plants). Deficiency in the presence of plenty is an availability problem, not a supply problem, in both kingdoms.

All four chlorosis answers, from two letter-pairs

Veins stay green: Mg = Mature, Fe = Fresh · Whole leaf pales: S = Shoot tip, N = the oldest

Ours, not a standard hook — but each half first names a pattern, then splits it by age using an initial letter that gives itself away, which is what makes it survive time pressure. Veins stay green is interveinal chlorosis, and the interveinal pair is the chlorophyll pair: Mg and Fe. Mg = Mature — same letter, the old lower leaves; Fe = Fresh — same letter, the young upper leaves. Whole leaf pales is uniform chlorosis, and the uniform pair is the protein pair, N (in every amino acid) and S (in cysteine and methionine). S = Shoot tip — same letter, the young leaves; which fixes N as the other one, in the oldest leaves, and nitrogen is the commonest deficiency of all. So old + uniform = N; old + interveinal = Mg; young + uniform = S; young + interveinal = Fe. The pattern axis is the chemistry; the age axis is nothing but the phloem-mobility test — N and Mg are mobile, so the plant strips the old leaves; S and Fe are stuck, so the new growth starves. Three things it does not cover, and each has been an answer. Interveinal and young with necrotic flecks is Mn, not Fe. Deficiencies that are not chlorosis at all sit outside the grid entirely: K scorches the margins of old leaves and weakens stems, P turns old leaves dark then purple with anthocyanin, and Ca and B kill shoot and root tips outright — blossom-end rot, tip burn, heart rot. And it gives you the element without the reasoning, which options are often built to test separately: Mg is chlorophyll's central atom, whereas Fe only builds chlorophyll.

Exam technique · diagnosing a deficiency from a photograph or a single sentence

  1. Ask the two questions in this order, and before reading any option. (1) Interveinal or uniform? (2) Old leaves or young? Two binary answers select one cell of the 2 × 2 before an option has had the chance to bias you. Stems are written so that both facts are present — "the lowest leaves", "the growing tip appears normal", "the veins remain green" are placed on purpose, so read for them specifically.
  2. Then take the modifiers, which are single-cue recognitions. Necrotic flecks on interveinal young → Mn. Three widely repeated statements that will not survive scrutiny

"Magnesium deficiency causes chlorosis because magnesium is needed to make chlorophyll." Too weak, and it makes Mg indistinguishable from Fe. Magnesium is chlorophyll's central atom, coordinated by the four pyrrole nitrogens of the porphyrin ring — a chlorophyll molecule without Mg is not chlorophyll. Iron is what is "needed to make" it. That precision is exactly what separates the two answers.

"Micronutrients are less important than macronutrients." The distinction is dose, not importance. Both categories are defined by the same all-or-nothing criterion, and a molybdenum-free plant dies as reliably as a nitrogen-free one — it simply takes about a ten-thousandth as much molybdenum to prevent it (nitrogen ≈1.5 % of dry mass, molybdenum ≈0.0001 %).

"Yellow leaves indicate nitrogen deficiency." Only if the yellowing is uniform and starts in old leaves. Uniform-but-young is sulphur; interveinal-and-old is magnesium; interveinal-and-young is iron or manganese. Four different answers hide behind "yellow leaves", and the question is normally set precisely to see whether you ask the two follow-up questions.

§ 11

Soil, nitrogen fixation and mycorrhizae

The ideaNitrogen is the element plants need most of after the ones they get from air and water, and the atmosphere is four-fifths nitrogen — yet almost every plant on Earth is nitrogen-limited. The reason is a chemical one: the two atoms in N₂ are held together by a triple bond that no plant enzyme can break. Only certain bacteria can, at enormous energetic cost, using an enzyme that is destroyed by oxygen. Everything interesting in this section — root nodules, leghaemoglobin, mycorrhizal partnerships, even carnivorous plants — is a different solution to the same shortage.

Reduction of atmospheric N₂ to ammonia by the bacterial enzyme nitrogenase. Only prokaryotes can do it. No plant, fungus or animal has ever been found to fix nitrogen — a legume does not fix nitrogen; the bacteria living inside it do.

Why soil chemistry decides what a root can get

Clay particles and humus carry a net negative surface charge, so soil holds cations — K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, NH₄⁺ — electrostatically, in an exchangeable pool. Roots pry them loose by cation exchange: the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase pumps protons into the rhizosphere (and CO₂ from root respiration makes carbonic acid, which supplies more), and those protons displace nutrient cations from the colloid surface into solution, where the root can take them up.

Anions have no such anchor. Nitrate, chloride and sulphate stay dissolved and move with the water, which has two consequences that examiners like. First, nitrate reaches roots quickly by mass flow with the transpiration stream, so nitrogen uptake is rarely diffusion-limited. Second, nitrate leaches straight past the root zone into groundwater after heavy rain, which is why nitrogen fertiliser has to be reapplied, why agricultural runoff eutrophies rivers, and why well water in intensively farmed areas is monitored for nitrate — infants who drink it can develop methaemoglobinaemia（變性血紅素血症）, the "blue baby" syndrome, because gut flora reduce nitrate to nitrite which oxidises haemoglobin iron to the ferric state. Phosphate is the opposite extreme: it binds tightly to soil particles, barely moves, and creates a depletion zone within a millimetre or two of the root surface — which is exactly the problem mycorrhizae solve.

Fixation, nitrification, denitrification — three words, three different reactions

Fixation converts N₂ to ammonia, not to nitrate. It is a reduction, it is anaerobic in effect (nitrogenase dies in oxygen), and it is performed only by prokaryotes. Nitrification is the aerobic oxidation of ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate by chemoautotrophic bacteria, which gain energy from it. Denitrification is the anaerobic reduction of nitrate back to N₂ by bacteria using nitrate as a terminal electron acceptor — a loss of nitrogen from the soil, favoured by waterlogging.

The commonest distractor on this topic is "nitrogen fixation produces nitrates". The second commonest is attributing fixation to the legume. The third is forgetting that ammonification — decomposers releasing NH₄⁺ from dead organic matter — supplies far more soil nitrogen in most ecosystems than fixation does.

The nodule, and the oxygen paradox

A compatible partnership begins with chemistry in both directions. The root secretes flavonoids; these activate the bacterial regulator NodD, which switches on the nod genes; the bacterium secretes Nod factors; the plant recognises them with a receptor kinase and responds. A root hair curls into a "shepherd's crook" around the bacteria, an infection thread — a plant-made tube — carries them inward, and cortical cells are triggered to divide and build a nodule around them. Specificity is tight: a given Rhizobium strain nodulates a narrow range of legume hosts, which is why inoculants are sold matched to the crop.

Then comes the contradiction the nodule has to solve. Nitrogenase is destroyed by oxygen. But fixing one N₂ costs 16 ATP, and the bacteroid makes that ATP by aerobic respiration. It needs a great deal of oxygen and it needs almost none — simultaneously.

What leghaemoglobin actually does

The textbook line is that leghaemoglobin "keeps oxygen out of the nodule" or "mops up oxygen to protect nitrogenase". That is half the story and it makes the nodule look like a sealed anaerobic box, which would starve the bacteroid.

Leghaemoglobin is a plant-encoded globin, present in the infected cell's cytoplasm at millimolar concentrations, and it does what haemoglobin does: it binds O₂ reversibly with very high affinity and carries it. The result is that free dissolved O₂ is buffered down to roughly 10 nmol·L⁻¹ — some four orders of magnitude below air-saturated water — while the total O₂ flux delivered to the bacteroid stays high, because the carrier shuttles it. Low concentration, high flux. That is the same trick your blood plays, and the parallel is exact: dissolved O₂ in plasma is a small number; the delivery rate is large because haemoglobin carries it. A nodule is pink for the same reason blood is red, and a nodule that is white or green is either immature or senescent and is not fixing.

The exchange is then straightforward. The plant delivers dicarboxylic acids (malate, succinate) to the bacteroid as fuel; the bacteroid releases NH₃/NH₄⁺ into the symbiosome space; the plant assimilates it immediately through glutamine synthetase and exports the nitrogen up the xylem as amides (asparagine, glutamine) in temperate legumes such as pea and clover, or as ureides (allantoin, allantoic acid) in tropical legumes such as soybean and cowpea. Also worth noting: the whole apparatus is switched off if the soil already contains plenty of nitrate. Nodulation is expensive, and a legume with fertiliser available will not bother.

Mycorrhizae: the other half of almost every root

Most roots you have ever seen were not operating alone. The association is ancient — fossil evidence puts it at the origin of land plants around 400 million years ago, which suggests that colonising land was a joint venture rather than something plants managed themselves. The fungus does one thing supremely well: its hyphae are a fraction of the diameter of a root hair, they extend centimetres into the soil, and they cost the plant far less carbon per unit of absorbing surface than root tissue would.

What a mycorrhiza is actually for

It is easy to write "improves mineral uptake" and be marked wrong for a reason worth understanding. The benefit is concentrated on immobile soil nutrients — above all phosphate, and secondarily zinc, copper and ammonium. Phosphate diffuses so slowly in soil that a root creates a depletion zone around itself within days; hyphae simply reach past it. Nitrate, by contrast, arrives by mass flow with the water and needs no help, so mycorrhizal benefit for nitrate is modest. And note the two things a mycorrhiza is not: it is not a nodule (no bacteria, no nitrogen fixation, no leghaemoglobin), and it is not free — the plant pays 4–20 % of its photosynthate, which is why colonisation is suppressed when soil phosphate is high.

The plants that solve it differently

Three categories get confused with each other, and the distinction is always about what is being taken and from whom.

Epiphytes

Staghorn ferns, most orchids, bromeliads. They grow on another plant purely for position — light, in a forest canopy — and take nothing from it. They photosynthesise fully and absorb water and minerals from rain and debris. Not parasites. This is a standard distractor.

Parasites

They form haustoria that penetrate the host and tap its vascular tissue. Hemiparasites such as mistletoe keep their chlorophyll and tap the host's xylem for water and minerals. Holoparasites such as dodder (Cuscuta), broomrape and Rafflesia have lost photosynthesis entirely and tap the phloem for sugar as well.

Carnivores

Venus flytrap, sundew, pitcher plants, bladderwort. They live in acidic, waterlogged, nitrogen-poor bogs. Their leaves are modified traps and they digest prey with secreted proteases and phosphatases — but for nitrogen and phosphorus, not for carbon or energy. They photosynthesise normally.

Two one-line traps in this last group

"Carnivorous plants obtain energy from the insects they capture." No. Their carbon and energy come from photosynthesis like any other plant; the prey supplies the mineral nutrients their soil cannot. Grow a Venus flytrap in fertile soil and it does fine without ever catching anything — trapping is expensive and it downregulates when nutrients are plentiful.

"Indian pipe (Monotropa) is a parasitic plant that taps tree roots." Almost, and the near-miss is the point. It is white, has no chlorophyll, and does take its carbon from a tree — but indirectly, through a shared mycorrhizal fungus. It is a myco-heterotroph: a plant parasitising a fungus that is in mutualism with a tree. Many orchids do the same as seedlings, since orchid seeds carry almost no reserves and cannot germinate without a fungal partner.

Three § 11 word-families that decode themselves — and the clause that does not

No mnemonic is warranted for any of these, because the terms are already descriptions, and the ladder tells you most of § 11 is second-pass material anyway. The nitrogen verbs: each names its own product — ammonification releases ammonium from dead organic matter, nitrification oxidises that to nitrite and then nitrate, and de-nitrification undoes exactly that, running the same road backwards to N₂ and losing it from the soil. The mycorrhizae: ecto- means outside, so the hyphae never enter a cell — a mantle around the root and a Hartig net between cortical cells; endo- (arbuscular) means inside, so hyphae penetrate the wall and branch into arbuscules, invaginating the plasma membrane without ever breaching it. The parasites: hemi- is half — mistletoe keeps its chlorophyll and needs only water and minerals, so it taps the xylem; holo- is whole — dodder, broomrape and Rafflesia have lost photosynthesis entirely and must take sugar too, so they tap the phloem as well.

One clause is not derivable from any of that, and it is the commonest distractor in the section, so commit it on its own: fixation yields ammonia, never nitrate — nitrogenase hands the plant NH₃, and any nitrate in the soil got there by nitrification, a separate step done by different bacteria. Two companions deserve the same treatment. No eukaryote fixes nitrogen: a legume does not fix it, the bacteria inside it do. And in most ecosystems ammonification supplies more soil nitrogen than fixation does — which is the third distractor, and the one nobody sees coming.

Go deeper — why nitrogen fixation is so expensive, and why it has never been engineered into a crop

The N≡N bond dissociation energy is 941 kJ·mol⁻¹, among the strongest in chemistry, and the difficulty is kinetic as much as thermodynamic: the first intermediates on the path from N₂ to NH₃ are higher in energy than the reactants, so the reaction has an enormous barrier even though it is downhill overall. Nitrogenase gets round this by binding N₂ at the FeMo cofactor and delivering electrons one at a time, each delivery costing the hydrolysis of two ATP by the Fe protein. Sixteen ATP per N₂ is the accounting price of moving eight electrons that way — and one electron pair is unavoidably wasted producing H₂, which is why the balanced equation has an H₂ on the right that students routinely drop.

Industrially, the Haber–Bosch process does the same job at 400–500 °C and 150–300 atm over an iron catalyst. It consumes a little over one per cent of world energy production and it feeds, by most estimates, around half of humanity — a genuine claim for the most consequential chemical process ever industrialised.

Transferring fixation into cereals has been an aspiration for fifty years and remains unachieved, for reasons this section has already given: nif is not one gene but a cluster of around twenty; the enzyme is destroyed by oxygen, so the host must build an oxygen-controlled compartment; and the 16 ATP per N₂ has to be paid out of photosynthate that would otherwise become grain. The successful strategies so far have gone sideways instead — engineering the signalling so that cereals can be colonised by nitrogen-fixing bacteria, or improving the association with endophytes that already do it loosely.
