Membranes, Receptors and Signal Transduction

One idea organises the whole note: a bilayer is a barrier that the cell makes leaky on purpose, and every exception to the barrier is a protein. That gives two currencies. Matter crosses through transporters — and because a concentration gradient is stored free energy, a cell can spend ATP once to build a gradient and then spend that gradient on everything else. Information crosses without the messenger crossing at all: something binds on the outside, an integral protein changes shape, and a relay of catalysts on the inside multiplies one binding event into millions of product molecules. Transport and signalling belong in the same chapter because they are the same trick — a conformational change in a membrane protein, coupled to something that costs or releases energy.

§ 01

The membrane as a two-dimensional fluid

The ideaA membrane is not a wall. It is a thin sheet of oil, two molecules thick, that behaves like a liquid in the two directions along the sheet and like a solid in the one direction across it. Things float about in it and bump into each other freely, but almost nothing gets through it. The proteins sitting in that sheet are what make it useful, and because the sheet is a liquid, they can move, cluster and rearrange — which is exactly what a receptor has to do when it is switched on.

Definition & key facts

Fluid mosaic model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972)
A lipid bilayer acts as a two-dimensional solvent in which proteins are dissolved. Lipids and proteins diffuse laterally within a leaflet; they hardly ever flip across. The bilayer supplies the barrier, the proteins supply every selective function.
bilayer = the barrier · proteins = every exception to it nothing crosses a membrane selectively without a protein doing it

Note 01 built the bilayer from the hydrophobic effect and showed how chain saturation sets the melting point; everything there is assumed here, and none of it is repeated. What matters now is the consequence: because the bilayer is a fluid in the plane and a solid across the plane, a molecule's fate depends entirely on which direction you ask about. Lateral diffusion is fast enough that a receptor and its G protein find each other by random collision several times a second. Transverse movement — flip-flop — is so slow that the two leaflets are effectively separate compartments with different lipid compositions, maintained for the life of the cell.

Which lipid sits on which face

A sugar-coated COIN — Choline Out, Inside Negative

Our own backronym rather than a circulating one — but it collapses six lipids into two chemical families, which is what makes it worth the shelf space. Sugar-coated: the glycolipids ride on the outer face, because sugar always points away from the cytosol. C-O, choline out: the outer leaflet takes the choline-headed lipids, PC (phosphatidylcholine) and SM (sphingomyelin, also a choline head). I-N, inside negative: everything without a choline head stays in — the amino-headed PE and PS together with the anionic PI and PIP₂ — so the cytosolic face is the negatively charged face. The charge half is what earns its keep. It is why PS, once scramblase puts it on the outside, is both the apoptotic "eat me" signal and the platelet's procoagulant surface; why conventional PKC lists phosphatidylserine as its third requirement (§ 09); and why PIP₂ — substrate of PLC (§ 09) and of PI3K (§ 10) — has to be on the inside, where the signal is read. It does not cover cholesterol, which sits in both leaflets, and it says nothing about the flippase / floppase / scramblase machinery that maintains the asymmetry (Go deeper, below).

The clinical case that proves GPI anchors matter

Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria is an acquired PIGA mutation in a haematopoietic stem cell. PIGA builds the GPI anchor, so the whole class of GPI-anchored proteins goes missing from that clone's surface — including the complement regulators CD55 (decay accelerating factor) and CD59 (MAC-inhibitory protein). The result is complement-mediated intravascular haemolysis. One anchor, one enzyme, a whole family of proteins lost: the cleanest demonstration that how a protein is attached to a membrane is a property worth knowing, and the reason eculizumab (anti-C5) works.

Cholesterol does not simply "increase" or "decrease" fluidity

This is the single most reliably mis-answered membrane fact on any paper. Cholesterol is a rigid, flat, four-ring plate with a small hydroxyl head. It inserts between phospholipids with its OH near the ester carbonyls and its ring system alongside the first few carbons of the acyl chains. Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions:

  • Above Tm, where the chains are disordered and flailing, the rigid ring system restricts their motion. Cholesterol therefore decreases fluidity and increases order.
  • Below Tm, where the chains would pack into a tight, crystalline gel, cholesterol physically gets in the way of that packing. It increases fluidity and prevents the gel state.

The right one-line summary is that cholesterol is a fluidity buffer: it broadens and eventually abolishes the sharp gel→fluid phase transition, holding the membrane in an intermediate "liquid-ordered" state over a wide temperature range. An exam option that says only "cholesterol increases membrane fluidity" or only "cholesterol decreases membrane fluidity" is incomplete by construction — the correct option will name the temperature relative to Tm, or will use the word modulates.

Three lipid confusions worth separating now

Fluidity vs permeability. Cholesterol makes a membrane less permeable to small solutes even while it prevents gelling, because it fills the packing defects that solutes squeeze through. Fluid and leaky are not synonyms.

Unsaturation vs chain length. Both raise fluidity, but one cis double bond does far more than shortening the chain by two carbons — the kink is a packing defect, whereas a shorter chain merely has less surface to interact over. Trans double bonds pack almost like saturated chains, which is why trans fats behave like saturated fats in a membrane.

Lipid rafts. Sphingolipid- and cholesterol-rich microdomains that are thicker and more ordered than the surrounding bilayer, and which concentrate GPI-anchored proteins and certain signalling components. They are a real but subtle phenomenon; an exam option calling them "regions of increased fluidity" has it backwards.

Exam technique · membrane options you can kill before you finish the stem

  1. Any cholesterol option naming one direction only is dead — "increases fluidity" and "decreases fluidity" are each exactly half the rule, and a well-built option set offers both, so you strike two of five in one move. The survivor names the temperature relative to Tm, or uses the words modulates, buffers, or broadens the transition.
  2. "Rafts are more fluid" is backwards — sphingolipid- and cholesterol-rich microdomains are thicker and more ordered than the bilayer around them, and they concentrate GPI-anchored proteins. One-line elimination, and it recurs across papers.
  3. Never let fluidity and permeability move together — cholesterol fills packing defects, so it makes a membrane less leaky at the same time as it stops it gelling. Any option that treats fluid and leaky as one axis is wrong by construction, whichever direction it picks.
  4. Use the extraction method as a free classifier — detergent → integral; high salt, EDTA or a pH change → peripheral; PI-specific phospholipase C → GPI-anchored. If the stem tells you how the protein came off, it has already told you the class, and you can answer without recognising the protein at all.
  5. One direction check covers every other fluidity item — anything that disrupts packing raises fluidity (a cis double bond, a shorter chain, a higher temperature); anything that lets chains lie flat against each other lowers it (saturation, a trans double bond, extra length, cooling). Do not memorise a list; ask "does this make the chains pack better or worse?"
  6. Where the time goes — these are ten-second questions. Answer on sight and bank the time for the § 04–§ 06 arithmetic, which genuinely needs the full 66 seconds.

§ 02

What crosses unaided: permeability

The ideaTo get through the middle of a membrane a molecule has to leave water behind and dissolve in oil for a moment. Anything that is happy in oil does this easily; anything that is happy in water finds it very expensive. A charged particle is the extreme case, because water clings to a charge extraordinarily tightly, and stripping that water off costs more energy than the cell has to spare. So the ranking of what leaks across a bare bilayer is simply a ranking of how much each molecule minds being dried off.

Definition & formula

Permeability coefficient P
The proportionality constant between the net flux of a solute across unit area of membrane and the concentration difference driving it. Units of velocity (cm·s⁻¹, or m·s⁻¹ in SI). It is a property of the solute-and-membrane pair, not of the solute alone.
J = P ([S]out − [S]in) simple diffusion: flux is linear in the gradient, never saturates, has no Km
Full definition, symbols and units
Partition coefficient K
The equilibrium ratio of a solute's concentration in olive oil (or octanol) to its concentration in water. Overton's rule: permeability tracks K almost linearly across many orders of magnitude.
Born (dehydration) penalty
The free-energy cost of moving a charge from a medium of high dielectric constant (water, ε ≈ 80) to one of low dielectric constant (hydrocarbon, ε ≈ 2). It scales as z²/r, so small and highly charged ions are worst off.

Three features distinguish simple diffusion from everything else in this note, and examiners test all three. It is not saturable — double the gradient and you double the flux, forever, because there is no binding site to fill. It shows no structural specificity — two molecules of similar size and polarity cross at similar rates, and one cannot competitively inhibit the other. And it is not inhibited by anything that inhibits a protein. A transport process that saturates, that distinguishes D-glucose from L-glucose, or that is knocked out by a specific inhibitor, is by definition protein-mediated.

The same is true of every amino acid and every ion. That single observation is why §§03–05 exist.

Why steroid hormones get their own receptor class

Look at the top row. Steroids are large, but they are also apolar, so their partition coefficient is high and they cross membranes freely — including the nuclear envelope. A molecule that can walk into the cytosol unaided has no need of a cell-surface receptor and no need of a second messenger, and that is exactly why cortisol, oestradiol, testosterone, aldosterone, thyroxine and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D use intracellular receptors that act directly as transcription factors (§ 11). One caveat you should be able to state: thyroid hormone is an exception to the mechanism, not to the conclusion — it is a charged amino-acid derivative, and it enters cells largely through MCT8 and OATP transporters rather than by free diffusion, yet its receptor is still nuclear. Peptide hormones — insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, growth hormone — cannot cross, so they must signal from the outside. Solubility predicts receptor location, and receptor location predicts the speed of the response. If you can reconstruct that chain you never have to memorise which hormone uses which system.

Notice what this section does not get: a 口訣 for the permeability table. There is no mnemonic for it and you do not need one, because the table is a single argument read downwards — add polarity, then add size, then add charge, and each addition costs orders of magnitude. Given any two solutes you can rank them in three seconds by asking which of them minds being dried off more, and a memorised word would only be a second thing to get wrong. The same applies to the hormone list in the box above: solubility predicts receptor location, and receptor location predicts speed. Hold that one sentence plus its two named exceptions — thyroid hormone, which needs MCT8 to get in yet still uses a nuclear receptor, and the over-lipophilic drugs below, which partition so well they never leave the membrane — and the list looks after itself.

The trap: "lipid-soluble = permeable" is only half a rule

Permeability is P ≈ KD/d, so it depends on partitioning and on diffusing once inside. A very large lipophilic molecule partitions well but crawls, and a molecule that partitions too well never leaves the membrane at all — which is precisely how amiodarone and other highly lipophilic drugs accumulate in membranes and acquire enormous volumes of distribution and half-lives of weeks. Maximum flux needs an intermediate partition coefficient, not a maximal one.

§ 03

Facilitated diffusion: channels, carriers and aquaporins

The ideaIf a molecule cannot cross the oil, the cell builds a hole for it — but a hole with a lock on it. There are two designs. One is a tube: open it and things pour through at enormous speed, but you have no control over which way, only over whether. The other is a revolving door: it binds one passenger, closes behind them, opens on the far side and lets them out, which is far slower but far fussier about who gets in. Both are strictly downhill; neither can push anything against its gradient. They only make an existing gradient usable.

Definition & formula

Facilitated diffusion (passive-mediated transport)
Protein-mediated movement of a solute down its electrochemical gradient. No ATP, no coupling to another solute; ΔG < 0 throughout. What the protein supplies is not energy but a path — it lowers the activation barrier of crossing, exactly as an enzyme lowers the barrier of a reaction.
v = Vmax[S]Kt + [S] carriers saturate, so they obey Michaelis–Menten kinetics with a transport constant Kt
Full definition, symbols and units
Channel
A continuous aqueous pore, gated open or shut. Once open it does not bind and release each ion, so turnover is near the diffusion limit: 10⁷–10⁸ ions·s⁻¹. Selectivity comes from a short filter region, not from a binding site with a Kt.
Carrier (transporter, permease)
Binds solute on one face, undergoes a conformational change so the site now faces the other side, and releases — the alternating-access mechanism. Never open to both sides at once. Turnover 10²–10⁴ s⁻¹, saturable, stereospecific, competitively inhibitable.
Uniport
A carrier that moves one solute species only, in whichever direction the gradient points. The GLUT family is the canonical example.

Why the GLUT family has four different Km values

This is the best worked illustration in all of transport of why a Km is a design decision rather than a number to memorise. Blood glucose sits near 5 mM when fasting and rises to roughly 8–10 mM after a meal. A transporter whose Kt is far below 5 mM is already saturated at every physiological concentration: it delivers a constant flux and is completely blind to whether you have eaten. A transporter whose Kt is far above 5 mM is operating on the linear part of its curve, so its flux is nearly proportional to blood glucose: it is a sensor. One fuel: the erythrocyte has no mitochondria and runs on glucose alone, and it (with the blood–brain barrier and most other cells) gets GLUT1. Do not memorise the four Kt values separately — they follow from the job, which is the whole point of this section. The two tissues that must never starve get the lowest Kt (GLUT3 1.4, GLUT1 1.5) and so run saturated at 5 mM; the two cells that must measure glucose get the highest (GLUT2 17) and so track it linearly; GLUT4 sits at ≈5 mM, right on plasma glucose, and is regulated by number rather than affinity. Take the two-way clause literally — GLUT2 is the partner of SGLT1/SGLT2 in § 05 — and take the fifth one narrowly: GLUT5 moves fructose only, which is exactly why it is planted in glucose option sets.

Worked example 1 — why the liver notices a meal and the red cell does not

Blood glucose rises from 5.0 mM (fasting) to 10.0 mM (after a meal). Take GLUT1 with Kt = 1.5 mM and GLUT2 with Kt = 17 mM. By what factor does each transporter's rate increase? What fraction of Vmax is each running at?

  1. Use v/Vmax = [S]/(Kt + [S]) throughout — the ratio is all we need, so Vmax itself never enters.
  2. GLUT1 at 5 mM: 5/(1.5 + 5) = 5/6.5 = 0.769 — already at 77 % of maximum.
  3. GLUT1 at 10 mM: 10/(1.5 + 10) = 10/11.5 = 0.870. Factor 0.870/0.769 = 1.13.
  4. GLUT2 at 5 mM: 5/(17 + 5) = 5/22 = 0.227 — only 23 % of maximum, well down on the linear part of the curve.
  5. GLUT2 at 10 mM: 10/(17 + 10) = 10/27 = 0.370. Factor 0.370/0.227 = 1.63.
  6. So doubling blood glucose barely changes red-cell uptake (+13 %) but raises hepatic and β-cell uptake by 63 %. Push it further: at a hypoglycaemic 3 mM, GLUT1 still runs at 0.667 of Vmax while GLUT2 collapses to 0.150. The erythrocyte and the brain are protected; the liver and the β cell obediently stop taking glucose up.
GLUT1 ×1.13 (0.769 → 0.870) · GLUT2 ×1.63 (0.227 → 0.370)

The examinable moral: a low Km makes a transporter or enzyme a constant supplier; a high Km makes it a sensor. The same logic explains why hexokinase (Km ≈ 0.1 mM) and glucokinase (Km ≈ 10 mM) coexist, and why glucokinase sits in exactly the two cells that also carry GLUT2.

Facilitated diffusion is not active transport, however saturable it looks

A carrier shows saturation kinetics, stereospecificity and competitive inhibition — all of which look like enzyme behaviour and tempt students to call it active. The test is thermodynamic, not kinetic: can it move a solute against its electrochemical gradient? GLUT1 cannot, and at equilibrium the glucose concentrations on the two sides are equal. In a red cell, GLUT1 never reaches that equilibrium only because hexokinase phosphorylates glucose the moment it arrives, keeping free intracellular glucose low. The gradient is maintained by metabolism, not by the transporter.

Exam technique · classify the transport first, then do the Kt arithmetic

  1. Three cues mean "a protein is doing it" — saturation (there is a Vmax), stereospecificity (D-glucose moves, L-glucose does not), and competitive inhibition or a named blocker. Any one of the three kills "simple diffusion" as an option without further thought.
  2. Then one more cue separates passive-mediated from active — ask only this: does the solute end up further from equilibrium than it started? "Concentrates it", "against its gradient", "accumulates 100-fold" → active. Then: the protein hydrolyses ATP itself → primary; another ion falls downhill alongside → secondary. Saturation kinetics is not evidence of activity, and that is the trap this whole section is built around.
  3. Ratio questions: cancel Vmax and stop — evaluate [S]/(Kt + [S]) at each concentration and divide the two results. Vmax never enters, so a stem that withholds it is not incomplete. Two additions, two divisions, one more division — twenty seconds, not sixty.
  4. Sanity-check the direction of the response before you trust your answer — a transporter whose Kt is well below the ambient concentration is already near-saturated and can barely respond; one whose Kt is well above it responds almost proportionally. Any option that has the low-Kt transporter responding more strongly is backwards, and it is the commonest distractor here.
  5. Treat "increases the affinity" as guilty — insulin moves GLUT4 by number (vesicle fusion), not by Kt. An option in which a hormone changes a transporter's Km should be the first one you look at sceptically.
  6. The turnover numbers need no mnemonic — channel 10⁷–10⁸, carrier 10²–10⁴, pump 10²–10³ s⁻¹. That ordering is just how much work each does per solute molecule: a hole does nothing, a revolving door has to turn, a pump has to turn and run a chemical reaction. Rank them by that and the exponents come back on their own.

Aquaporins, and the reason a kidney needs them

Water crosses a bare bilayer at about 10⁻³ cm·s⁻¹ — fast in the table above, but hopelessly slow for a nephron that must reabsorb ~180 L a day, or a red cell that must swell and shrink in seconds. Aquaporins are tetramers in which each monomer is an independent pore conducting roughly 10⁹ water molecules per second, single file. Two design features are examinable. A narrow ar/R constriction excludes anything larger than water. And two opposed NPA motifs (asparagine–proline–alanine) meet at the centre and force the passing water molecule to reorient — which breaks the hydrogen-bond chain and so blocks the Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism. Aquaporins conduct water but not protons, which is essential: a membrane freely permeable to H⁺ would short-circuit every proton gradient in the cell, including the one that makes ATP.

Why a water channel refuses protons

NPA = No Protons Allowed

A backronym of ours rather than a circulating 口訣 — but the three letters are the real motif, which is what makes it cheap. Two opposed N-P-A (asparagine–proline–alanine) loops meet at the centre of the pore and force each passing water molecule to reorient; that breaks the hydrogen-bond chain which Grotthuss proton hopping depends on. The ar/R constriction is the separate, upstream filter, and it works on size alone: nothing bigger than water gets past it. Carry the reason with the letters, because the reason is what is examined — a membrane freely permeable to H⁺ would short-circuit every proton gradient in the cell, ATP synthase included. The hook says nothing about the architecture: an aquaporin is a tetramer of four independent pores, each conducting ~10⁹ water molecules per second, so an option describing "a single central channel formed by the four subunits" sounds plausible and is wrong.

The clinical hook is AQP2 in the renal collecting duct. Vasopressin (ADH) binds the V₂ receptor, a Gs-coupled GPCR; cAMP rises, PKA phosphorylates AQP2, and AQP2-laden vesicles fuse with the apical membrane. Water is then reabsorbed and urine concentrates. Lose the receptor or the channel and you get nephrogenic diabetes insipidus: litres of dilute urine that desmopressin cannot fix, because the failure is downstream of the hormone. Contrast central diabetes insipidus, where ADH itself is missing and desmopressin works perfectly. This one example ties § 03 to § 08 and is exactly the shape of a 115-style vignette.

Go deeper · how a potassium channel is 10 000× selective and still runs at the diffusion limit

The paradox is real: Na⁺ (ionic radius 0.95 Å) is smaller than K⁺ (1.33 Å), so a channel cannot exclude sodium with a sieve. The KcsA structure showed how it is done. The selectivity filter is a stretch of backbone with the signature sequence T-V-G-Y-G, arranged so that its carbonyl oxygens point into the pore at precisely the spacing of the oxygens in K⁺'s hydration shell.

  • A K⁺ ion entering the filter sheds its water and is re-coordinated by carbonyl oxygens at almost exactly the same distance. The energetic cost of dehydration is repaid, so passage is nearly free.
  • A Na⁺ ion is too small to reach all those oxygens at once. The filter cannot compensate its dehydration, so the barrier stays high and sodium is rejected — not by size, but by failure to substitute for water well enough.
  • Speed comes from having several K⁺ sites in a row: ions queue up and electrostatic repulsion between them pushes the leading ion out. Selectivity lives in the chemistry of the filter; throughput lives in multi-ion occupancy. They are independent, which is why there is no trade-off.

Gating is a separate question from selectivity, and channels are classified by it: voltage-gated (an S4 helix studded with arginines that moves in the field — NaV, KV, CaV), ligand-gated (the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, a pentamer that is simultaneously a receptor and a channel; the IP₃ and ryanodine receptors on the ER), and mechanically gated (Piezo, and the hair-cell channels of the inner ear).

Note the exam-relevant asymmetry: an ion channel is gated, not pumped. Opening a channel never determines the direction of flow — the electrochemical gradient does. This is why a K⁺ channel hyperpolarises a cell and a Na⁺ channel depolarises it, and why the answer to "what happens when this channel opens?" is always "the membrane potential moves towards that ion's equilibrium potential" (§ 06).

§ 04

Primary active transport and the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase

The ideaPushing something uphill costs energy, and the amount it costs can be calculated exactly from how steep the hill is. Two things make a hill: a difference in concentration, and — if the thing being pushed is charged — a difference in voltage. Add those two costs together and you know what the pump has to pay. A pump that hydrolyses one has a fixed budget, so its stoichiometry is not an arbitrary fact to memorise; it is the largest number of ions the budget will stretch to.

The only two numbers in §§ 04–06 that are not derivable

RT counts up in threes · F is Faraday

Our own hook, not a standard one, and it is deliberately small because almost nothing else in these three sections has to be memorised. RT counts up in threes: at 37 °C RT = 2.58 kJ·mol⁻¹, whose digits climb 2, 5, 8 — which is enough to catch the usual slips (2.85, 2.55), and if you ever want to check the figure it is simply R × T = 8.314 × 310 K. F is Faraday: 96.5 kJ·mol⁻¹·V⁻¹ is nothing but the Faraday constant 96 485 C·mol⁻¹ read in this chapter's units — 96.5 kJ per mole per volt. Everything you can be asked to compute grows out of that pair: 2.303 RT/F = 61.5 mV, the Nernst coefficient; RT/F = 26.7 mV; and one Na⁺ re-entering the cell releases 2.58 ln(12/145) + 96.5 × (−0.070) = −6.43 − 6.75 = −13.2 kJ·mol⁻¹, which is the unit price everything in § 05 is quoted in. Two cautions. 61.5 mV is 61.5 only at 37 °C — a paper set at 25 °C wants 59.2, and you can regenerate either figure from the pair rather than storing both. And the ion concentrations (Na⁺ 145/12, K⁺ 4/140, Cl⁻ 110/10, Ca²⁺ 1.8 mM/100 nM) are deliberately not in the hook, because a well-written stem hands them to you; if you want one anchor for them, note that Na⁺ and K⁺ are near mirror images — each sits at about 140 mM on the side it is concentrated on.

Exam technique · any transport-energetics number, in six moves

  1. Always write it inward, then flip if you need to — ΔG = RT ln([S]in/[S]out) + zFΔψ with Δψ = −0.070 V. Hold that one version. For outward movement change the sign of the whole expression rather than trying to remember a second formula and a second convention.
  2. Two terms, and one of them sometimes vanishes — for an uncharged solute z = 0 and the electrical term disappears completely. An option that makes glucose transport voltage-sensitive, or that charges a neutral solute for crossing a potential, dies on that alone without any arithmetic.
  3. Reuse four numbers instead of deriving them each time — one Na⁺ out costs +13.2 kJ·mol⁻¹ (6.4 chemical + 6.8 electrical, almost equal halves); one K⁺ in costs +2.4, cheap because the voltage helps; one Ca²⁺ out costs +38.8; a 3 Na⁺ : 2 K⁺ cycle therefore costs 44.4. Run backwards, each Na⁺ falling in releases 13.2.
  4. The feasibility check kills options with no calculation at all — one ATP delivers about 50 kJ·mol⁻¹ inside a cell. Any stoichiometry needing more than 50 per ATP is impossible, which is how 4 Na⁺ : 2 K⁺ (57.6) dies in five seconds. Apply the same test to any invented pump a stem offers you.
  5. ln, not log₁₀ — this is the planted error — the formula is RT ln(ratio) with RT = 2.58. Using log₁₀ makes every answer 2.303× too small, and it characteristically makes a symporter look as though one fewer sodium would do. If your answer lands just under a whole number of Na⁺, check this before anything else.
  6. Do not memorise stoichiometries — derive them or read them off the name — 3 out, 2 in is a result (worked example 2 below), not a fact; NCX's 3 : 1 is forced, because 38.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ is more than two sodiums (26.4) can supply; and several names carry their own numbers (NKCC2 = Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻, NCC = Na⁺-Cl⁻). Electrogenicity is not a list either: count the charges crossing each way and see whether they cancel.
Worked example 2 — the price of one Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase cycle, and why the stoichiometry cannot be 4:2

Take a typical cell at 37 °C: [Na⁺]out = 145 mM, [Na⁺]in = 12 mM, [K⁺]out = 4 mM, [K⁺]in = 140 mM, Vm = −70 mV. Find the free energy needed for one 3 Na⁺ : 2 K⁺ cycle, compare it with the ~50 kJ·mol⁻¹ that ATP hydrolysis actually yields inside a cell, and test whether a 4 Na⁺ : 2 K⁺ pump could work.

  1. One Na⁺ outward. Chemical: RT ln(145/12) = 2.579 × 2.492 = +6.43 kJ·mol⁻¹. Electrical: moving a +1 charge from −70 mV to 0 mV, so zFΔψ = (+1)(96.5)(+0.070) = +6.75 kJ·mol⁻¹. Total +13.18 kJ·mol⁻¹. Note the two halves are almost equal — the voltage matters as much as the concentration difference, which is exactly what students forget.
  2. One K⁺ inward. Chemical: RT ln(140/4) = 2.579 × 3.555 = +9.17 kJ·mol⁻¹. Electrical: a +1 charge moving from 0 to −70 mV, so (+1)(96.5)(−0.070) = −6.75 kJ·mol⁻¹. Total +2.41 kJ·mol⁻¹ — cheap, because the voltage helps.
  3. The cycle. 3(13.18) + 2(2.41) = 39.54 + 4.83 = 44.37 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  4. Feasibility. ATP hydrolysis under cellular conditions releases about 50 kJ·mol⁻¹ (the standard value, −30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹, applies only at 1 M ATP, and , which no cell is anywhere near). 44.4 < 50, so the pump runs — at about 44.4/50 = 89 % thermodynamic efficiency.
  5. Why not 4 Na⁺ : 2 K⁺? 4(13.18) + 2(2.41) = 52.72 + 4.83 = 57.6 kJ·mol⁻¹ — more than ATP can pay. Such a pump would run backwards, synthesising ATP while sodium leaked in.
cycle costs 44.4 kJ·mol⁻¹ · ATP yields ≈50 · a 4:2 pump would need 57.6 and is impossible

This is the single most useful calculation in membrane biochemistry, because it turns "3 out, 2 in" from a memorised pair of digits into a result you can rederive. It also explains the pump's metabolic weight: a quarter to a third of a resting cell's ATP goes on it, and considerably more in neurons, where every action potential has to be paid for.

Every other ABC protein is an active transporter. Go deeper · the E1/E2 cycle, and how one pump binds two ions with opposite affinities

A pump must bind its cargo tightly on the side it collects from and release it on the side it delivers to. Since binding tightly and releasing easily are contradictory, the affinity itself has to change during the cycle — and the energy of ATP hydrolysis is what pays for that change. For the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase:

  1. E1 faces the cytosol with high affinity for Na⁺. Three Na⁺ bind.
  2. ATP phosphorylates Asp369, forming the acyl phosphate. The protein is now E1-P with the sodium ions occluded — buried, connected to neither side. This occluded state is what makes leakage impossible.
  3. E1-P relaxes to E2-P, which faces outward with low Na⁺ affinity. The three Na⁺ are released into a 145 mM solution — thermodynamically the hard step, and the one the phosphate paid for.
  4. E2-P has high affinity for K⁺. Two K⁺ bind from outside and trigger dephosphorylation.
  5. Loss of the phosphate returns the enzyme to E1, whose K⁺ affinity is low, so the two K⁺ fall off into the cytosol. ATP binding accelerates this last transition.

Ouabain and digoxin bind the E2-P state from the extracellular side, at the site that would otherwise accept K⁺ — which is why hypokalaemia potentiates digoxin toxicity (less K⁺ to compete for the site) and why hyperkalaemia blunts it. Note that this is a mechanistic prediction from the cycle, not a fact to learn separately.

SERCA runs the identical E1/E2 logic with two Ca²⁺ per ATP, and the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase with one H⁺ out and one K⁺ in — electroneutral, which is why it can generate a pH gradient of about 2.5 million-fold (pH 7.4 in the parietal cell, pH 1 in the lumen — 6.4 pH units) without generating an impossible voltage.

§ 05

Secondary active transport: the sodium gradient as currency

The ideaOnce one pump has spent to build a steep sodium gradient, that gradient is itself a store of energy — like water held behind a dam. Any protein that lets sodium fall back in can harness the fall to drag something else uphill alongside it. Nothing in that second protein burns ATP; it merely couples one downhill journey to one uphill journey and takes its cut. The cell effectively converts ATP into a single universal currency and then spends the currency everywhere.

Worked example 3 — how hard can a symporter pull? SGLT1 against SGLT2

SGLT1 (intestine, late proximal tubule) couples 2 Na⁺ to one glucose; SGLT2 (early proximal tubule) couples 1 Na⁺. Using Δμ̃Na = −13.18 kJ·mol⁻¹ and RT = 2.579 kJ·mol⁻¹ at 37 °C, find the maximum glucose gradient each can build at equilibrium, and explain the division of labour.

  1. At equilibrium the energy released by the sodium falling in exactly equals the energy stored in the glucose gradient: RT ln([G]in/[G]out) = n × 13.18 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  2. SGLT2, n = 1: ln(ratio) = 13.18/2.579 = 5.111, so ratio = e5.111 = 166.
  3. SGLT1, n = 2: ln(ratio) = 26.36/2.579 = 10.22, so ratio = e10.22 = 2.8 × 10⁴.
  4. Doubling the coupling did not double the concentrating power — it squared it (166² ≈ 2.8 × 10⁴), because the stoichiometry sits inside an exponential.
  5. The physiology follows immediately. The early proximal tubule faces a filtrate at nearly plasma glucose concentration: there is bulk to move and no gradient problem, so SGLT2 is built for high capacity at low cost (1 Na⁺ per glucose) and reabsorbs ~90 % of the filtered load. The late segment faces a filtrate that has already been stripped, so it needs raw pulling power, and SGLT1 pays a second sodium to get it. The intestine, which must scavenge the last of a meal, uses SGLT1 for the same reason.
SGLT2 (1:1) → 166-fold · SGLT1 (2:1) → 2.8 × 10⁴-fold

Clinical payoff: SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) block the high-capacity, low-power transporter, so a large glucose load escapes into the urine — roughly 60–80 g/day out of a filtered load of about 160 g/day. They lower glucose by an insulin-independent route, which is why they do not cause hypoglycaemia on their own, and the obligatory osmotic diuresis explains both the blood-pressure and heart-failure benefits and the risk of genital mycotic infection and euglycaemic ketoacidosis.

Worked example 4 — digoxin, quantitatively: how a 6 mM change in Na⁺ raises calcium

The Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger is the cardiac myocyte's main route for exporting calcium: 3 Na⁺ in for 1 Ca²⁺ out. Digoxin partially inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, raising [Na⁺]in from 12 mM to 18 mM. Show why intracellular calcium must rise, and by how much. ([Ca²⁺]out = 1.8 mM, resting [Ca²⁺]in = 100 nM, Vm = −70 mV.)

  1. What exporting one Ca²⁺ costs. Chemical: RT ln(1.8 × 10⁻³ / 1 × 10⁻⁷) = 2.579 × ln(18 000) = 2.579 × 9.798 = 25.27 kJ·mol⁻¹. Electrical: a +2 charge from −70 mV to 0, so (2)(96.5)(0.070) = 13.51 kJ·mol⁻¹. Total 38.77 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  2. What 3 Na⁺ supply normally. 3 × 13.18 = 39.54 kJ·mol⁻¹. That clears 38.77 with just 0.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ to spare — the exchanger runs almost exactly at equilibrium. (Two sodiums would give only 26.4 and could not do the job at all: the 3:1 stoichiometry is forced.)
  3. After digoxin. With [Na⁺]in = 18 mM, each Na⁺ now yields RT ln(18/145) + F(−0.070) = −5.38 − 6.75 = −12.13 kJ·mol⁻¹, so three supply 36.40 kJ·mol⁻¹ — less than the 38.77 required.
  4. Find the new equilibrium. The exchanger stalls until calcium has risen far enough that the cost falls to what is available: RT ln(1.8 × 10⁻³/[Ca²⁺]in) = 36.40 − 13.51 = 22.89 kJ·mol⁻¹, so ln(ratio) = 8.878, ratio = 7 175, [Ca²⁺]in = 1.8 × 10⁻³/7 175 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ M = 251 nM.
  5. A 50 % rise in intracellular sodium produces a 2.5-fold rise in resting calcium. More calcium is loaded into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, more is released per beat, and contractility rises — positive inotropy without any change in heart rate.
[Na⁺]in 12 → 18 mM gives [Ca²⁺]in 100 → ≈250 nM (2.5×)

Two things fall out of the arithmetic. First, digoxin's therapeutic index is narrow because the exchanger was already at equilibrium — there is no buffer, and a little extra inhibition produces a lot of extra calcium, which is exactly what triggers delayed afterdepolarisations and arrhythmia. Second, hypokalaemia potentiates toxicity: with less extracellular K⁺ competing for the pump's external site, more digoxin binds (see the E1/E2 panel in § 04). Correcting potassium is therefore part of treating digoxin toxicity.

"No ATP is used" does not mean "not active"

Secondary active transport is the definitional trap of this whole topic. SGLT1 hydrolyses nothing, yet it concentrates glucose 28 000-fold against a gradient — plainly active. Conversely, GLUT2 in the same cell hydrolyses nothing and moves glucose downhill only — plainly passive. The distinction is whether the transported solute ends up further from equilibrium than it started, not whether the protein touches ATP. And note the corollary: add ouabain and SGLT1 stops within minutes, because the gradient it lives on decays. Every secondary transporter is ATP-dependent at one remove.

Sym, anti, uni

Symport = sympathetic, they travel together, same direction. Antiport = antisocial, they pass each other going opposite ways. Uniport = travelling alone, and therefore never able to do work. Only the first two can be active, because only they have a partner whose downhill journey can pay for an uphill one.

§ 06

Membrane potential

The ideaLet potassium — and only potassium — leak out of a cell. Each ion that leaves takes a positive charge with it, so the inside becomes negative, and that negativity pulls the next potassium back. Very quickly the pull balances the push and net movement stops, even though potassium is still far more concentrated inside. The voltage at which that balance occurs is fixed by the concentration ratio alone. A real cell leaks several ions at once, so its voltage settles nearest to whichever ion it leaks most — which at rest is potassium.

The membrane potential at which the electrical force on an ion exactly cancels its concentration gradient, so net flux is zero. It depends only on the concentration ratio and the charge — not on permeability, not on how many channels are open, and not on any other ion.

Worked example 5 — the four equilibrium potentials, and where the resting potential really comes from

EK −95 · ECl −64 · ENa +67 · ECa +131 mV

Now read the resting potential off those numbers. Vm at rest is −70 mV, which sits close to EK and nowhere near ENa. That is the whole explanation: at rest the membrane is studded with open K⁺ leak channels and almost no open Na⁺ channels, so PK ≫ PNa and the potential is dragged towards potassium's equilibrium. It does not reach −95 mV because the small residual sodium leak keeps pulling it positive. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase contributes only about −4 to −10 mV directly (its 3:2 stoichiometry exports one net charge per cycle); its overwhelming contribution is indirect — it maintains the concentration ratios that set EK in the first place. An exam option claiming the pump "generates the resting potential" is the classic half-truth.

The three questions this section actually gets asked

1. "A cell is placed in a solution with raised extracellular K⁺. What happens to Vm?" Raise [K⁺]out from 4 to 8 mM and EK becomes 61.5 log₁₀(8/140) = −76.4 mV instead of −95.0 mV. Because Vm follows EK, the cell depolarises. This is the whole of hyperkalaemic cardiac toxicity, and the reason its ECG changes (peaked T waves, then widened QRS) are an emergency.

2. "Which ion's equilibrium potential is unchanged by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase stopping?" None of them, eventually — but the immediate effect is on Vm, not on Eion, because the concentration ratios take many minutes to decay while the direct electrogenic contribution disappears at once.

3. "A channel opens. Which way does the ion go?" Always the same reasoning: compare Vm with Eion, and the ion moves in whichever direction drives Vm towards Eion. Opening K⁺ or Cl⁻ channels pulls Vm down (hyperpolarisation, inhibition); opening Na⁺ or Ca²⁺ channels pulls it up (depolarisation, excitation). That single rule answers every question about GABAA, nicotinic receptors, GIRK channels and KATP channels.

Nernst is for one ion; Goldman is for the cell

EK = −95 mV is the potential the cell would have if it were permeable to potassium alone. The actual Vm is a permeability-weighted compromise between all the equilibrium potentials, and it moves whenever the relative permeabilities change — which is the entire mechanism of an action potential. At the peak of a spike, PNa briefly exceeds PK by more than an order of magnitude and Vm races up towards +67 mV without quite reaching it. Nothing about the ion concentrations changes measurably during a single action potential; only the permeabilities change. Writing "sodium floods in and raises intracellular sodium" is the classic error.

Exam technique · Nernst and driving force under time pressure

  1. Out over in, and write the 61.5/z down first — E = (61.5/z) log₁₀([ion]out/[ion]in) mV at 37 °C. Putting the coefficient on paper before you touch the logarithm is the whole fix for the single most-missed step on this calculation: dividing by 2 for Ca²⁺. The undivided answer is always offered.
  2. Fix the sign before you compute anything — an ion concentrated outside has a positive E (Na⁺ +67, Ca²⁺ +131); one concentrated inside has a negative E (K⁺ −95). For an anion z is negative, so chloride's inward gradient gives a negative E (−64). Option sets routinely carry the same magnitude with both signs, so this check is a free elimination.
  3. Order-of-magnitude check: 61.5 mV per ten-fold gradient, divided by z — calcium's 10⁴-fold gradient on a divalent ion is about 4 × 30.75 ≈ +123 mV. So an answer near +262 has forgotten z, and an answer near +30 or +60 has counted one decade instead of four.
  4. Driving force is Vm − Eion, and its sign is the answer — negative means the cation flows in. And if the stem asks "what happens when this channel opens", compute nothing at all: Vm moves towards that ion's E. K⁺ or Cl⁻ → hyperpolarise, inhibit. Na⁺ or Ca²⁺ → depolarise, excite. That one rule answers GABAA, nicotinic, GIRK and KATP stems alike.
  5. Kill the pump half-truth — "the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase generates the resting potential" is wrong by construction: its direct electrogenic contribution is only −4 to −10 mV. It maintains the concentration ratios that set EK, which is a different sentence, and the difference between the two sentences is usually the entire question.
  6. Kill "sodium floods in and raises intracellular sodium" — during a single action potential only the permeabilities change; bulk concentrations do not move measurably. Any option explaining a spike by a concentration change can be struck without reading the rest of the stem. It is lifted from real textbook prose, which is precisely why it looks safe.

Where this becomes clinical: the pancreatic β cell. Its resting potential is held negative by an ATP-sensitive potassium channel, KATP (a Kir6.2 pore with an SUR1 regulatory subunit). Glucose enters through GLUT2, glucokinase phosphorylates it, metabolism raises the ATP/ADP ratio, and ATP closes KATP. Removing the dominant potassium permeability lets Vm drift up towards the other ions — the cell depolarises, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, calcium enters and insulin granules fuse. So insulin secretion is a membrane-potential story from beginning to end, and it explains three drug classes at once:

  • Sulfonylureas (glibenclamide, gliclazide) bind SUR1 and close the channel regardless of glucose — effective, but able to cause hypoglycaemia because the trigger is no longer glucose-dependent.
  • Diazoxide opens KATP, hyperpolarises the cell and suppresses insulin — used in congenital hyperinsulinism.
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists (§ 08) work through cAMP and amplify a signal that glucose must have already started, which is precisely why they do not cause hypoglycaemia on their own.

Activating mutations of Kir6.2 keep the channel open and cause neonatal diabetes that responds to sulfonylureas rather than insulin; inactivating mutations cause congenital hyperinsulinism. Both are predictable straight from the mechanism.

§ 07

The architecture of a signal, and amplification

The ideaA hormone arrives at a cell in vanishingly small amounts — a few thousand molecules for a cell containing billions of protein molecules — and yet it changes what that cell does completely. It can only manage that by handing the message to a chain of catalysts, each of which acts on many copies of the next. One binding event becomes a hundred activated switches, then a hundred thousand small molecules, then millions of altered enzymes. Every pathway in the rest of this note is the same shape; only the parts differ.

Definition & key facts

Signal transduction
Conversion of an extracellular chemical message into an intracellular chemical change of a different kind. The message itself normally never enters the cell; what crosses the membrane is a conformational change.

Specificity
Complementarity between ligand and receptor binding site. It is why adrenaline acts on the heart and not on the liver's glucagon receptor, and why one hormone can have opposite effects in two tissues that carry different receptor subtypes.

The factor by which the number of affected molecules increases across a step. Total gain is the product of the per-step gains, because each step is catalytic. This is why cascades are built from enzymes and not from stoichiometric binding partners.

Affinity, occupancy and why potency is not efficacy

Receptor binding is a simple equilibrium, R + L ⇌ RL, with Kd = [R][L]/[RL]. Rearranged, the fraction of receptors occupied is Y = [L]/([L] + Kd) — the same rectangular hyperbola as Michaelis–Menten, with Kd playing the role of Km. Three facts follow that examiners like:

  • A low Kd means high affinity. The number is a dissociation constant, so it runs backwards from intuition. Hormones typically have Kd in the nanomolar to picomolar range, which is what allows them to work at circulating concentrations no cell could otherwise detect.
  • Occupancy is a bad switch. Going from 10 % to 90 % occupancy takes an 81-fold change in concentration. Biological switches therefore add cooperativity, kinase cascades, or thresholding steps (such as PKA's requirement for four molecules) to sharpen the response.
  • EC₅₀ is often much lower than Kd. When a maximal response needs only a fraction of the receptors occupied, the surplus are called spare receptors, and the dose–response curve sits to the left of the binding curve. That is why blocking most of a receptor population can leave the response almost intact — and why an option equating "receptor occupancy" with "response" is usually wrong.
Worked example 6 — occupancy, and then the arithmetic of a cascade

(a) A hormone binds its receptor with Kd = 20 nM. Its plasma concentration is 5 nM. What fraction of receptors is occupied? (b) One occupied receptor activates 50 Gs molecules before it is phosphorylated and switched off. Each activated adenylyl cyclase makes 1 000 cAMP before its Gsα hydrolyses GTP. Four cAMP molecules are needed to release the two catalytic subunits of one PKA holoenzyme. Each catalytic subunit phosphorylates 200 molecules of phosphorylase kinase. How many phosphorylase kinase molecules does one hormone molecule account for?

  1. (a) Y = 5/(5 + 20) = 5/25 = 0.20 — 20 %. A tempting wrong route is [L]/Kd = 5/20 = 25 %; that ratio is not an occupancy and exceeds 1 whenever [L] > Kd, which should tell you immediately that it cannot be a fraction.
  2. (b) Step 1. 1 receptor → 50 active Gsα, each switching on one adenylyl cyclase. Gain ×50.
  3. Step 2. 50 × 1 000 = 5.0 × 10⁴ cAMP. Gain ×1 000.
  4. Step 3. 5.0 × 10⁴ ÷ 4 = 1.25 × 10⁴ PKA holoenzymes activated, releasing 2 × 1.25 × 10⁴ = 2.5 × 10⁴ catalytic subunits. Gain ×0.5 — the only step in the whole cascade that reduces the molecule count.
  5. Step 4. 2.5 × 10⁴ × 200 = 5.0 × 10⁶ phosphorylase kinase molecules. Gain ×200.
  6. Overall: 50 × 1 000 × 0.5 × 200 = 5.0 × 10⁶. And two catalytic layers still follow — phosphorylase kinase activating glycogen phosphorylase, which then releases glucose 1-phosphate — which is how textbooks arrive at figures of order 10⁸ product molecules per hormone molecule.
(a) Y = 20 % · (b) 5.0 × 10⁶ molecules, overall gain 5 × 10⁶

Two lessons. First, gains multiply, so adding one modest step matters more than improving an existing one. Second, look again at step 3: requiring four cAMP to activate one PKA costs molecule count but buys something better — a threshold. Because activation depends on roughly the fourth power of [cAMP], PKA ignores small fluctuations and responds sharply once cAMP crosses a level. Amplification and thresholding are different jobs, and a well-built cascade contains both. In the real cell the arithmetic is also capped by abundance: a hepatocyte does not contain 10⁸ phosphorylase molecules, so the cascade saturates rather than running to the number the multiplication predicts.

What makes a "second messenger"

Four properties, and they are the discriminators in a well-written option set: it is small and diffusible, so it spreads the signal through the cell; it is made or released rapidly in response to the first messenger; it is destroyed or sequestered equally rapidly, so the signal is transient; and it acts by binding regulatory sites on proteins rather than by being consumed. cAMP, cGMP, IP₃, DAG, Ca²⁺ and PIP₃ all qualify. A hormone does not, and neither does a protein kinase. DAG is one borderline case: a genuine second messenger, but not water-soluble, so it stays in the membrane and recruits its target to the membrane rather than diffusing to it. Nitric oxide is the other, and it is genuinely contested — it satisfies all four criteria inside the cell that makes it, but because it crosses membranes it acts mainly on a different cell, so most sources class it as an intercellular (first, paracrine) messenger and reserve "second messenger" for the cGMP it causes to be made. Carry both readings: an option contrasting NO with cAMP is almost always testing that NO needs no receptor and no transporter to reach its target.

§ 08

GPCRs, G proteins and the cAMP–PKA axis

The ideaThe largest family of receptors in the body all work the same way. The receptor is a protein that snakes across the membrane seven times; when its ligand binds outside, the bundle of helices shifts and the inside face changes shape. That new shape grabs a three-part switch protein and forces it to swap a spent nucleotide for a fresh one, which turns the switch on. The switch then drifts away, activates an enzyme, and — crucially — turns itself off after a few seconds by chewing up the nucleotide it is holding. Built-in timers are what make the signal transient rather than permanent.

What PKA actually does, and why the same hormone means different things in different tissues

PKA phosphorylates serine and threonine residues in the consensus R-R-X-S/T-Φ. Its substrate list is the answer to most "adrenaline does what?" questions:

  • Phosphorylase kinase → activated → activates glycogen phosphorylase → glycogenolysis.
  • Glycogen synthase → phosphorylated → inactivated. One kinase, two substrates, opposite signs: the cell switches breakdown on and synthesis off in a single step, which is why reciprocal regulation costs nothing extra.
  • Hormone-sensitive lipase and perilipin → activated → lipolysis.
  • Phospholamban in cardiac muscle → phosphorylated → stops inhibiting SERCA → calcium is pumped back into the SR faster → the heart relaxes faster (lusitropy) as well as contracting harder.
  • CREB at Ser133 in the nucleus → recruits the coactivator CBP → transcription of genes with a cAMP-response element, including PEPCK for gluconeogenesis. This is the slow arm of a fast pathway.

Specificity comes from two places. First, tissues express different receptor subtypes, so adrenaline raises cAMP in the liver (β₂) and lowers it in a platelet (α₂). Second, A-kinase anchoring proteins (AKAPs) tether PKA next to particular substrates, so a local rise in cAMP phosphorylates the nearby targets and not the distant ones. The messenger is the same everywhere; the wiring is not. That is the honest answer to "how can one second messenger produce so many different responses?", and it is a favourite stem.

Cholera, pertussis and the two ways to break a switch

Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates an arginine in Gsα, abolishing its GTPase. Gsα is stuck in the GTP-bound state, adenylyl cyclase runs continuously, and in the intestinal epithelium the resulting PKA activity phosphorylates CFTR, which pours Cl⁻ into the lumen with Na⁺ and water following osmotically — litres of isotonic "rice-water" stool with no blood and no inflammation, because nothing has been damaged. Treatment is oral rehydration with glucose plus salt, and the reason it works is § 05: SGLT1 is untouched, so glucose drags sodium (and water) back across.

Pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates a cysteine near the C-terminus of Giα, the very surface that contacts the receptor. Gi can no longer be loaded with GTP, so the inhibitory arm is dead — and because the brake is off, cAMP again rises. The two toxins have opposite molecular actions and the same directional effect on cAMP. That is exactly why the pair is examined together.

Cholera and pertussis, and why both raise cAMP

Cholera jams the GAS pedal · pertussis cuts the brake cable · the car speeds up either way

The accelerator-and-brake picture for Gs and Gi is the usual one; the spelling trick is ours, and it is what makes the assignment stick. GAS pedal — G-A-S contains Gs, the stimulatory arm: cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gsα and abolishes its GTPase, so the throttle is jammed open and adenylyl cyclase never switches off. Brake cable — Gi is the inhibitory arm: pertussis toxin modifies the very C-terminal surface of Giα that the receptor must touch, so Gi can never be loaded with GTP and the cable is cut. A jammed throttle and a cut brake do the same thing to the speed: cAMP rises either way. The image deliberately does not encode the chemistry — cholera modifies an arginine in Gsα, pertussis a cysteine in Giα — so hold that pair separately if the paper asks for it. What the image does protect is the double negative: "pertussis toxin inhibits Gi" and "pertussis toxin lowers cAMP" are not the same sentence, and the second one is an option in almost every version of this question.

Four confusions inside this section

"The G protein is phosphorylated when activated." No. It exchanges GDP for GTP. Nothing is phosphorylated and no ATP is spent at that step; the GTP is hydrolysed later, and that is the off switch, not the on switch.

"Only Gα signals." Free Gβγ is a signal in its own right — it opens GIRK potassium channels (which is how M₂ receptors slow the sinoatrial node), inhibits voltage-gated calcium channels at nerve terminals, and activates GRKs and some adenylyl cyclase isoforms.

"cAMP activates PKA by phosphorylating it." cAMP is an allosteric ligand for the regulatory subunits; it is not consumed and it transfers no phosphate.

"Caffeine works by raising cAMP synthesis." Methylxanthines (caffeine, theophylline) inhibit phosphodiesterases, so cAMP is destroyed more slowly. Blocking degradation and stimulating synthesis both raise a messenger, but only the second requires a receptor — which is why a PDE inhibitor works on a cell with no receptor at all.

Exam technique · any GPCR stem, in five boxes

  1. Fill the boxes in whichever direction the stem runs — ligand → receptor subtype → G protein → effector → second messenger → kinase. Fill the four you know and the fifth is the answer. The table above is built to be read backwards as well as forwards, and examiners ask it both ways within the same paper.
  2. Recognition rules that skip the table entirely — "smooth muscle contracts" or "a gland secretes" → Gq, because contraction needs calcium. "Heart rate falls", "transmitter release is suppressed", or an opioid, an α₂ agonist or adenosine in the stem → Gi, and often free Gβγ opening a GIRK channel rather than the cAMP fall itself. "β-anything, glucagon, GLP-1, PTH, V₂, TSH/LH/FSH/ACTH" → Gs.
  3. The toxin item is a double negative, not a fact — both toxins raise cAMP. The option that lowers cAMP after pertussis toxin is the designed trap and is chosen more often than the correct answer; recognising the shape is worth more than recalling the toxin.
  4. "Which of these is a second messenger" is a definition test — small, diffusible, made fast, destroyed fast, and it binds a regulatory site rather than being consumed. A hormone never qualifies, and neither does a kinase. DAG qualifies but does not diffuse. If NO and cGMP are both on offer, cGMP is the safe answer, because most sources reserve the term for it and class NO as an intercellular messenger.
  5. Three distractor sentences that are always wrong — "the G protein is phosphorylated when activated" (it exchanges GDP for GTP; nothing is phosphorylated at that step), "cAMP activates PKA by phosphorylating it" (an allosteric ligand transfers no phosphate), "caffeine raises cAMP by stimulating adenylyl cyclase" (it inhibits phosphodiesterase). Each reads exactly like textbook prose, which is why it is in the option list.
  6. Where the time goes — with QISS in place a receptor-to-messenger item is a fifteen-second question; spend the saved minutes on § 04–§ 06. And never leave one blank: even placing the receptor in its family eliminates two options, and with five options that makes answering strictly better than a gap, even at −0.5.
§ 09

The Gq branch: PLC, IP₃, DAG, Ca²⁺ and PKC

The ideaThe second great GPCR pathway does something clever: it takes a minor lipid already sitting in the inner face of the membrane and cuts it in two. One half is water-soluble and floats off into the cytosol carrying the message to the calcium store; the other half is greasy and stays exactly where it was. The pathway therefore produces two messengers at once — one that travels and one that marks a spot — and the enzyme they jointly activate has to meet both, which means it can only be switched on at the membrane, at the moment the signal arrives, and nowhere else.

Definition & key facts

Phospholipase C-β (PLCβ)
Activated by Gqα·GTP. It hydrolyses phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂) in the inner leaflet, cleaving the bond between glycerol and the phosphorylated inositol head group. Two products, two fates.
PIP₂ → IP₃ (into the cytosol) + DAG (stays in the bilayer) a single cut makes one diffusible messenger and one localised one

Diacylglycerol (DAG)

Protein kinase C (PKC)

Why does the cell go to the trouble of holding cytosolic calcium at 100 nM — four orders of magnitude below the concentration on either side of it — when maintaining that gradient costs SERCA, PMCA and NCX running continuously? Precisely because it is expensive and steep. A messenger is useful in proportion to how far it can swing, and calcium can rise a hundredfold within milliseconds simply by opening a channel; no synthesis is required, which makes it the fastest second messenger available. The cost is that calcium is also toxic when sustained — it precipitates phosphate, activates proteases and phospholipases, and triggers apoptosis — which is why every calcium signal is a brief spike or an oscillation, never a plateau.

Which product goes where

IP₃ — Into the cytosol, because three phosphates on an inositol ring make it about as water-soluble as a molecule can be. DAG — stays in the Double layer, because it is two fatty acids on a glycerol and has nowhere better to go. If an option says DAG "diffuses to the endoplasmic reticulum", it is wrong on chemistry alone; if it says IP₃ "recruits PKC to the membrane", the two have been swapped.

Following the pathway to a real response: α₁-adrenergic vasoconstriction

Noradrenaline binds the α₁ receptor on a vascular smooth-muscle cell. Gqα·GTP activates PLCβ; PIP₂ is cut; IP₃ opens the sarcoplasmic reticulum's IP₃ receptors and cytosolic calcium jumps. Four Ca²⁺ bind calmodulin, Ca²⁺–CaM activates myosin light-chain kinase, MLCK phosphorylates the regulatory light chain of myosin, and the cell contracts. DAG meanwhile recruits PKC, which phosphorylates further targets and prolongs the response. Every step is a step you can name — and this is exactly the chain that phenylephrine exploits as a nasal decongestant and vasopressor, and that prazosin (α₁ blocker) interrupts. Note that smooth muscle has no troponin: its calcium sensor is calmodulin, not troponin C. That contrast with skeletal muscle is a reliable single-best-answer discriminator.

Two drugs and one tumour promoter that prove the mechanism

Phorbol esters (TPA/PMA, from croton oil) are structural mimics of DAG that bind the same C1 domain of PKC — but unlike DAG they are not metabolised, so PKC stays on indefinitely. They are not carcinogens (they do not mutate DNA) but they are powerful tumour promoters, and the reason is exactly this: a signalling switch that should be transient has been made permanent. Every oncogenic mechanism in this note has the same shape.

Lithium inhibits inositol monophosphatase, the enzyme that performs the last dephosphorylation in the recycling of IP₃ back to free inositol. Cells that are signalling hardest through Gq deplete their inositol first and so cannot resynthesise PIP₂ — which gives a plausible use-dependent explanation for lithium's selectivity in bipolar disorder, and explains why the inositol-depletion hypothesis is the one you are expected to be able to state.

The calcium-release confusions

IP₃ receptor vs ryanodine receptor. Both are ER/SR calcium-release channels. The IP₃R is opened by IP₃ and dominates in non-muscle cells and smooth muscle. The RyR is opened by calcium itself (calcium-induced calcium release) and, in skeletal muscle, by direct mechanical coupling to the dihydropyridine receptor. Malignant hyperthermia is a RyR1 mutation; volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine trigger uncontrolled release, and dantrolene blocks it.

Where the calcium comes from. In cardiac muscle the trigger calcium enters from outside through L-type channels and then amplifies itself through RyR2 — so cardiac contraction genuinely depends on extracellular calcium, and calcium-channel blockers work. In skeletal muscle the DHPR–RyR1 coupling is mechanical, so contraction continues in calcium-free medium. Same ion, same store, completely different dependence — a classic discriminating stem.

Where each kind of muscle gets its calcium

One touch · two steps · three phosphates

Our own line rather than a circulating one, and it settles the discriminator this section is built around by letting each receptor's own number carry its mechanism. One touch — RyR1, skeletal: the DHPR is coupled to RyR1 mechanically, a single direct contact, so skeletal muscle keeps contracting with no extracellular calcium at all. Two steps — RyR2, cardiac: cardiac muscle needs both — trigger calcium first enters from outside through L-type channels, then amplifies itself through RyR2, which is why cardiac contraction genuinely fails in a calcium-free medium and why calcium-channel blockers work. Three phosphates — IP₃, smooth: in smooth muscle and in non-muscle cells the store is opened by IP₃, the three-phosphate inositol, acting on the IP₃ receptor — the pathway this section has just traced from α₁ all the way to contraction. Read the third clause narrowly: it names what opens the store, not the only source, because smooth muscle also takes calcium in from outside — which is exactly why dihydropyridines relax a vessel. Two things the line does not carry, and both are examined: malignant hyperthermia is a RyR1 mutation triggered by volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine and blocked by dantrolene; and smooth muscle has no troponin, so its calcium sensor is calmodulin driving MLCK.

§ 10

Receptor tyrosine kinases, insulin and the Ras–MAPK cascade

The ideaGrowth factors use a different trick from hormones that act in seconds. Their receptors are single-pass proteins whose inner half is itself an enzyme, but one enzyme molecule on its own can do nothing. The ligand's job is simply to bring two receptor molecules together; once they are side by side, each phosphorylates the other. Those new phosphates are not the message — they are addresses. A whole set of cytoplasmic proteins carry domains that recognise phosphorylated tyrosine, so within seconds the inner face of the membrane has become a noticeboard where several different pathways are pinned up at once.

Definition & key facts

Receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)
A single-pass membrane protein with an extracellular ligand-binding domain and a cytosolic tyrosine kinase domain. Activation requires dimerisation followed by trans-autophosphorylation — each monomer phosphorylates tyrosines on its partner, first in the activation loop (raising catalytic activity) and then in the tail (creating docking sites).
ligand → dimerise → trans-autophosphorylate Tyr → SH2/PTB docking → branch no second messenger is required at all; the membrane itself becomes the scaffold
Full definition, symbols and units
SH2 and PTB domains
Protein modules that bind phosphotyrosine in a sequence-specific way: SH2 reads the three residues C-terminal to the phospho-Tyr, which is how one receptor recruits different partners at different sites. SH3 domains, by contrast, bind proline-rich sequences and carry no phosphate specificity at all.
Ras
A small monomeric G protein, farnesylated at its C-terminus and therefore anchored to the inner leaflet. Same GDP/ logic as Gα, but the GEF (Sos) and GAP (p120-RasGAP, NF1/neurofibromin) are separate proteins rather than parts of the receptor.
MAP kinase cascade
Three tiers: MAPKKK (Raf) phosphorylates MAPKK (MEK), which phosphorylates MAPK (ERK). MEK is a dual-specificity kinase — it must phosphorylate both a threonine and a tyrosine in ERK's activation loop, which is a coincidence requirement that suppresses noise.
Insulin receptor

Insulin, step by step

Insulin binds the α subunits of its already-dimeric receptor. The β subunits' kinase domains trans-phosphorylate; IRS-1 and IRS-2 dock and are themselves phosphorylated on many tyrosines, becoming an extended scaffold. PI3-kinase binds through its SH2-containing regulatory subunit and phosphorylates PIP₂ to PIP₃, which is a new lipid mark on the inner leaflet. Two PH-domain kinases, PDK1 and Akt (PKB), are pulled to that mark by the PIP₃ itself — a beautiful mechanism, because it converts a chemical signal into a spatial one. Once co-localised, PDK1 phosphorylates and activates Akt, and Akt then does the work:

  • Phosphorylates AS160, releasing GLUT4-containing vesicles so they fuse with the plasma membrane. Muscle and adipose glucose uptake rise within minutes — by changing transporter number, not affinity, exactly as § 03 promised.
  • Phosphorylates and inhibits GSK3. Since GSK3's job was to phosphorylate and inactivate glycogen synthase, inhibiting the inhibitor switches glycogen synthesis on.
  • Phosphorylates PDE3B in adipocytes, which destroys cAMP and so shuts down hormone-sensitive lipase — insulin's antilipolytic effect, and the reason ketogenesis is suppressed after a meal.
  • Phosphorylates FoxO1, excluding it from the nucleus and switching off transcription of the gluconeogenic enzymes PEPCK and glucose-6-phosphatase. This is the slow arm, and it opposes the PKA/CREB arm of glucagon precisely.

PTEN is the phosphatase that removes the 3-phosphate from PIP₃ and thus terminates the whole branch. It is one of the most commonly deleted tumour suppressors in human cancer — which tells you both that the pathway is growth-promoting and that its off-switch matters as much as its on-switch.

Why Ras mutations are oncogenic — and why the answer is not "it binds GTP more tightly"

Ras cycles between an inactive GDP form and an active GTP form, exactly like Gα. Its intrinsic GTPase is very slow — on its own Ras would stay active for tens of minutes — so the off-switch is supplied almost entirely by a GAP, which accelerates hydrolysis by around five orders of magnitude. The GAP works by inserting an arginine finger into the active site and stabilising the transition state, and it needs Ras's own glycine 12, glycine 13 and glutamine 61 to be exactly where they are. Substitute any of them with a bulkier residue and the arginine finger no longer fits, or the catalytic water is no longer positioned:

  • The mutant Ras still binds GTP normally and still binds GDP normally.
  • Its intrinsic GTPase is only modestly impaired.
  • But GAP-stimulated hydrolysis is abolished, so in the cell — where the GAP does virtually all the work — Ras is permanently GTP-bound and Raf is permanently engaged. Growth-factor signalling continues with no growth factor present.

That is the mechanism, and it is what a well-written option set discriminates. "Binds GTP with higher affinity", "cannot bind GDP", "is constitutively phosphorylated" and "dimerises spontaneously" are all wrong, and all tempting. KRAS is mutated in over 90 % of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas, ~40 % of colorectal cancers and ~30 % of lung adenocarcinomas — which makes this single mechanism one of the highest-yield facts in the whole of molecular medicine.

RTK confusions that cost marks

"RTKs phosphorylate serine and threonine." No — tyrosine, by definition. Almost all the downstream kinases (Raf, MEK's substrate ERK, Akt, PKA, PKC) are Ser/Thr kinases; MEK is the odd one out because it must do both.

"The insulin receptor dimerises when insulin binds." It is already an α₂β₂ tetramer held by disulfide bonds. EGFR and PDGFR genuinely do dimerise on ligand binding; the insulin receptor is the standard exception, and that is exactly why it appears in questions.

"Ras is a G protein, so it has βγ subunits." Ras is a small monomeric GTPase: no β, no γ, and its GEF and GAP are separate proteins rather than the receptor itself. The shared logic (GDP off, GTP on, GEF, GAP, lipid anchor) is what makes them a family; the subunit composition is what distinguishes them.

Exam technique · pathway-level reasoning and drug-target option sets

  1. Sort the options by level before you read them properly — outside the cell (trastuzumab, cetuximab, panitumumab), receptor kinase domain (gefitinib, erlotinib, osimertinib), cytoplasmic kinase (imatinib on BCR-ABL), MAPKKK (vemurafenib and dabrafenib on B-Raf V600E), MAPKK (trametinib). Any stem that names a mutation is asking one question and only one: is the drug above or below the lesion?
  2. The inference the paper is really after — a jammed switch makes everything upstream of it irrelevant, so KRAS-mutant colorectal cancer does not respond to anti-EGFR antibodies. Apply that one sentence to any "downstream activating mutation" stem and you will not need to have memorised the drug list at all.
  3. Kill the affinity answer on Ras — the mutant binds GTP and GDP essentially normally and its intrinsic GTPase is only modestly impaired; what is destroyed is GAP-stimulated hydrolysis. "Binds GTP more tightly", "cannot bind GDP", "is constitutively phosphorylated" and "dimerises spontaneously" are four wrong options, and each one sounds entirely reasonable.
  4. Kill on residue chemistry — a receptor tyrosine kinase phosphorylates tyrosine, by definition. Nearly every kinase downstream (Raf, Akt, PKA, PKC, ERK) is Ser/Thr, and MEK is the one dual-specificity exception. An option handing serine phosphorylation to an RTK is a free elimination.
  5. Remember which receptor is the exception — EGFR and PDGFR genuinely do dimerise on ligand binding, but the insulin receptor is already an α₂β₂ disulfide-linked tetramer, so "insulin causes its receptor to dimerise" is wrong. That sentence is true of every other receptor in the same table, which is exactly what makes it a good distractor.
  6. You do not need to rote-learn the chain — read it off the logic instead. An SH2 domain (Grb2) grabs the new phosphotyrosine; its partner is a GEF (Sos); a GEF loads a GTPase (Ras); and a GTPase switches on a cascade named for its own tiers, MAPKKK → MAPKK → MAPK, which is Raf → MEK → ERK. The order is forced by the names; only the names themselves are new. Hold the two branches instead: PI3K → PIP₃ → Akt is the metabolic arm, Grb2 → Ras → ERK the mitogenic one.
Go deeper · JAK–STAT, and the kinases that are not receptors

Cytokine receptors — for erythropoietin, growth hormone, prolactin, leptin, the interferons and most interleukins — look like RTKs but have no kinase domain of their own. Instead each carries a constitutively associated Janus kinase (JAK). The logic is otherwise identical:

  1. Ligand dimerises the receptor, bringing two JAKs together.
  2. The JAKs trans-phosphorylate each other, then phosphorylate tyrosines on the receptor tails.
  3. STAT proteins dock at those phosphotyrosines through their own SH2 domains and are phosphorylated in turn.
  4. Phospho-STATs release, dimerise by reciprocal SH2–phosphotyrosine binding (each STAT's SH2 domain grips the other's phosphotyrosine), translocate to the nucleus and act as transcription factors.
  5. Termination: SOCS proteins, induced by the STATs themselves, bind and inhibit the JAKs — a textbook negative feedback loop.

The path from receptor to gene is the shortest of any surface-receptor system, which is why an option describing "a transcription factor that is phosphorylated at the membrane and then goes straight to the nucleus" is JAK–STAT and not MAPK.

Clinically: JAK2 V617F is a constitutively active mutant found in nearly all polycythaemia vera and about half of essential thrombocythaemia and primary myelofibrosis — the erythropoietin receptor signals with no erythropoietin, which is why the EPO level is low. Ruxolitinib is a JAK inhibitor, tofacitinib and baricitinib are used in autoimmune disease, and loss-of-function JAK3 or γ-chain mutations cause severe combined immunodeficiency.

Non-receptor tyrosine kinases are the third variant: Src, Abl, Btk, ZAP-70, Lck. They have SH2 and SH3 domains and are recruited to receptors rather than being receptors. BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukaemia is the paradigm — the translocation fuses ABL's kinase domain to BCR's dimerisation domain, so the kinase is permanently dimerised and permanently on. Once again: an oncogene is a signalling switch that has lost its off state.

§ 11

Nitric oxide, nuclear receptors, and switching the signal off

The ideaTwo systems remain, and they sit at opposite ends of the speed range. One uses a gas: a cell makes it, it drifts through membranes into the neighbouring cell, and it is gone within seconds — no receptor on any surface, no transporter, no second cell needed to pass it along. The other uses molecules greasy enough to walk into the nucleus by themselves, where the receptor is the transcription factor, so the response takes hours and lasts days. And underneath both sits the point that is easy to forget: a signal that cannot be switched off is not a signal at all, so every pathway in this note has a dedicated mechanism for ending itself.

Definition & key facts

Soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC)
A cytosolic heterodimer carrying a haem group. Nitric oxide binds the haem iron, which breaks the Fe–histidine bond, distorts the protein and raises catalytic activity several hundred-fold. It converts to cGMP, which activates protein kinase G.

The NO axis, and the two drug classes that must never be combined

Acetylcholine relaxes an artery — but only if the endothelium is intact. That observation, which looked paradoxical for decades, is the whole pathway in miniature. ACh acts on M₃ receptors on the endothelial cell (Gq, so calcium rises, § 09); Ca²⁺–calmodulin activates eNOS; NO is produced and, being a small uncharged gas, simply diffuses through both membranes into the underlying smooth-muscle cell; there it binds the haem of sGC; cGMP rises; PKG activates myosin light-chain phosphatase and lowers cytosolic calcium, so myosin light chains are dephosphorylated and the cell relaxes. Strip the endothelium away and there is nothing to make the NO, so the same acetylcholine now acts directly on smooth-muscle M₃ receptors and constricts. Two cells, two receptors, opposite answers.

  • Organic nitrates (glyceryl trinitrate, isosorbide) are NO donors. They dilate veins preferentially, so preload falls, ventricular wall stress falls and myocardial oxygen demand falls — which is how they relieve angina. Continuous exposure causes tolerance (partly depletion of the bioactivating aldehyde dehydrogenase-2, partly compensatory neurohormonal activation), hence the nitrate-free interval.
  • Sildenafil, tadalafil inhibit PDE5, the phosphodiesterase that destroys cGMP in the corpus cavernosum and the pulmonary vasculature — which is why the same drug treats erectile dysfunction and pulmonary arterial hypertension.
  • Together they are contraindicated, and the reason is mechanistic rather than empirical: one drug increases cGMP production while the other blocks its destruction. Attacking both ends of one pathway is multiplicative, not additive, and the result is profound refractory hypotension.
  • Sildenafil's cross-inhibition of PDE6 — the retinal phosphodiesterase downstream of transducin (§ 08) — produces the transient blue-tinged vision, which is a rather satisfying confirmation that the Gt pathway really does run on cGMP.

The receptor guanylyl cyclases complete the picture. Atrial stretch releases ANP, which binds GC-A and raises cGMP in vascular smooth muscle and in the kidney: vasodilation plus natriuresis, opposing the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. Sacubitril blocks neprilysin, the enzyme that degrades natriuretic peptides, and so amplifies this arm — the reason sacubitril/valsartan works in heart failure. And GC-C in the gut is the mirror image of cholera toxin: a bacterial enterotoxin raises cGMP instead of cAMP, but PKG phosphorylates the same CFTR, and the patient again loses litres of water.

Nuclear receptors: slow, powerful, and mechanistically different

A steroid needs no receptor on the surface, no G protein and no second messenger, because it can cross the membrane itself (§ 02). The consequence is a completely different set of kinetics. There is a latency of hours, because mRNA and then protein must be made; the response cannot be terminated by hydrolysing a messenger, only by the eventual turnover of that protein; and every effect is ultimately a change in the amount of some enzyme or channel rather than a change in its activity.

Which receptors are Type I needs no separate storage: it is the five steroid receptors GR, MR, AR, PR, ER, and everything else (TR, RAR, RXR, VDR, PPAR, LXR, FXR) is Type II. The one row the three Is do not cover is the most examined of the lot — what happens with no ligand. A Type I receptor is simply inactive; a Type II receptor actively represses, recruiting NCoR/SMRT and histone deacetylases, which is why an unliganded TR silences its genes and why hypothyroidism is more than the absence of stimulation.

Three clinical mechanisms in the 115 style

Glucocorticoids. Dexamethasone crosses the membrane, binds GR, displaces hsp90, and the dimer enters the nucleus. Two mechanisms follow, and separating them explains the whole risk–benefit profile. Transactivation at GREs induces annexin A1 (lipocortin), which inhibits phospholipase A₂ — so arachidonate is not released and both prostaglandins and leukotrienes fall. (NSAIDs block only cyclo-oxygenase, so leukotrienes are untouched: that is the discriminator.) Transactivation also drives gluconeogenic and catabolic genes, which is where hyperglycaemia, muscle wasting and osteoporosis come from. Transrepression works by the receptor tethering to NF-κB and AP-1 and blocking them without binding DNA itself, and this is where most of the anti-inflammatory benefit lies. Separating the two arms is the entire rationale for "dissociated" steroid development.

Tamoxifen. A selective oestrogen receptor modulator, and the mechanism is the helix-12 story above. Tamoxifen (activated by CYP2D6 to endoxifen) binds ER but leaves helix 12 in the wrong position, so the AF-2 coactivator groove is misshapen. Whether the resulting complex activates or represses therefore depends on which coregulators that tissue contains: in breast, corepressors dominate and tamoxifen is an antagonist; in endometrium and bone, coactivators dominate and it behaves as a partial agonist — hence the increased endometrial-carcinoma risk alongside preserved bone density. Raloxifene has a different profile (bone agonist, breast and endometrial antagonist), and aromatase inhibitors such as anastrozole are not SERMs at all: they cut oestrogen synthesis, which only works after the menopause.

GLP-1 receptor agonists. GLP-1 is an incretin released by intestinal L cells after a meal. Its receptor is a class B GPCR coupled to Gs, so cAMP rises in the β cell and activates PKA and Epac2, which enhance calcium handling and granule exocytosis. But look at where this enters the pathway of § 06: cAMP amplifies a secretory signal that glucose metabolism must already have started by closing KATP. If glucose is low, KATP stays open, the cell stays polarised, and there is nothing for cAMP to amplify — which is exactly why liraglutide and semaglutide do not cause hypoglycaemia as monotherapy while sulfonylureas, which close the channel directly, do. The other effects (delayed gastric emptying, glucagon suppression, hypothalamic appetite suppression) come from GLP-1 receptors elsewhere. Native GLP-1 has a half-life of about two minutes because DPP-4 clips it, which is why the drugs are either DPP-4-resistant analogues or DPP-4 inhibitors such as sitagliptin.

A pathway that only turned on would be useless: the cell must be able to report a change, and it must be able to recover. Every stage of every pathway in this note therefore has a specific reverse.

The last two traps

β-arrestin is not only an off-switch. Once bound, it also acts as a scaffold that assembles its own MAPK signalling complex on the internalised receptor. A ligand that preferentially drives one or the other output is a biased agonist, and this is currently the most active area of GPCR pharmacology. An option stating that arrestin "terminates all signalling from the receptor" is out of date.

Do not confuse desensitisation with downregulation. Desensitisation is fast (seconds to minutes) and is a covalent, reversible modification of existing receptors. Downregulation is slow (hours to days) and is a genuine fall in receptor number through degradation and reduced synthesis. Both produce tolerance; only the second requires new protein synthesis to reverse, which is why recovery from chronic agonist exposure takes days.
