Physics · Note 13

Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction

Electricity acts on charge; magnetism acts on moving charge. That one clause generates almost everything here: because the force depends on velocity, it must be perpendicular to it, so it can steer but never speed up — and because a current is moving charge, currents both feel magnetic fields and make them. Induction is the same idea read backwards: move a conductor through a field, or change the field through a loop, and the magnetic force on the carriers becomes an EMF. Get the perpendicularity and the bookkeeping of flux right and the rest of the chapter is arithmetic.

§ 01

The magnetic field and the missing monopole

The ideaA magnet has no equivalent of a lone electric charge. You can hold a single positive charge on its own, but nobody has ever found a lone north pole: cut a magnet anywhere you like and both halves come away with two ends again, all the way down to a single atom. The reason is that magnetism is made by charge going round in circles, and a circle always has two faces. This section also settles how the strength of a magnetic field is measured in the first place — not by what it does to something sitting still, which is nothing at all, but by the hardest push it can give something moving through it.

An electric field is defined by the force on a charge at rest: E = F/q. That definition fails for magnetism, because a stationary charge in a magnetic field feels nothing at all. You have to set the charge moving, and then the force depends on how fast it moves and in which direction. So the magnetic field is defined by the maximum force per unit charge per unit speed — the value obtained when the velocity is perpendicular to the field.

The second formula in the box is the deeper statement. The net magnetic flux out of any closed surface is exactly zero, which is a compact way of saying: no isolated magnetic pole has ever been found. Every magnetic field line that leaves a region comes back. Cut a bar magnet in half and you do not get a north-pole object and a south-pole object; you get two smaller complete magnets. Keep cutting down to a single iron atom and you still have a dipole, because the magnetism came from circulating charge (orbital motion and electron spin) in the first place, and a current loop is intrinsically two-ended.

Exam point · the electric analogy that fails

For electricity, ∮ E · dA = qenc/ε₀ — non-zero, because charge can be isolated. For magnetism the right-hand side is zero, always. Multiple-choice items love the pair: "which of Maxwell's equations expresses the absence of magnetic monopoles?" The answer is Gauss's law for magnetism, not Faraday's law.

§ 02

The force on a moving charge

The ideaA magnetic field does nothing at all to a charge that is standing still. Set the same charge moving and it feels a push — but the push comes out sideways, at right angles both to the way the charge is travelling and to the field itself. Because the push is always sideways, it can turn the particle but can never make it go faster or slower, any more than the string on a whirling stone can speed the stone up. That one restriction settles a surprising number of questions before you calculate anything.

Definition & formula

Magnetic (Lorentz) force on a charge
The force a magnetic field exerts on a charge in motion; its direction is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, and its magnitude is proportional to the component of velocity perpendicular to the field.
F = |q| v B sin θ θ = angle between v and B; the force law itself is exact at any speed — the non-relativistic assumption enters later, in r = mv/(|q|B)

The sin θ is not decoration. Only the component of velocity perpendicular to B produces force: a charge sliding exactly along a field line (θ = 0) feels nothing, and a charge crossing the lines at right angles (θ = 90°) feels the maximum. That is why v sin θ = v⟂ is the physically meaningful factor.

The right-hand rule, stated so it cannot be misapplied

Point the fingers of your right hand along v, curl them toward B through the smaller angle; your thumb now points along v × B. For a positive charge the force is along the thumb. For a negative charge (an electron, a Cl⁻ ion) the force is exactly opposite to the thumb — reverse it at the very end, never by using your left hand and never by reversing v or B.

Memory hook

"v then B, thumb gives the push — then flip for a minus." Order matters: v × B = −(B × v), so getting the two vectors the wrong way round gives you a force pointing 180° wrong.

The single most examined trap: magnetic forces do no work

Because F ⟂ v, the dot product F·v is zero at every instant, so the power delivered is zero and the work over any path is zero. A magnetic field can never change a particle's speed or kinetic energy. It changes direction only. If a question says a particle "accelerates" in a magnetic field, that is true — it has centripetal acceleration — but it does not speed up. Anything that genuinely speeds a particle up (a cyclotron, a betatron, an MRI gradient) is doing it with an electric field somewhere in the mechanism.

Worked example 1 · How big is a magnetic force, really?

A proton (q = +1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg) moves at 2.0×10⁶ m·s⁻¹ at 30° to a uniform 0.50 T field. Find the magnetic force, and compare it with the proton's weight.

  1. Only the perpendicular velocity component matters: v⟂ = v sin30° = 2.0×10⁶ × 0.500 = 1.0×10⁶ m·s⁻¹.
  2. F = qv⟂B = (1.60×10⁻¹⁹)(1.0×10⁶)(0.50) = 8.0×10⁻¹⁴ N.
  3. Weight: mg = (1.67×10⁻²⁷)(9.8) = 1.6×10⁻²⁶ N.
  4. Ratio: 8.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 1.6×10⁻²⁶ ≈ 5×10¹².

F = 8.0×10⁻¹⁴ N — about 5×10¹² times the proton's weight

That ratio is the reason gravity is silently dropped in every charged-particle problem in this chapter. It is not laziness; it is twelve orders of magnitude.

§ 03

Circular and helical motion in a uniform field

The ideaA sideways push that never lets up bends a moving particle round into a circle. The striking part is the timing. A faster particle sweeps out a wider circle, but it takes exactly as long to get round as a slow one, because the extra distance and the extra speed cancel each other out — so every particle of a given kind laps at the same rate, whatever its energy. If the particle is not moving squarely across the field, the part of its motion that runs along the field is simply left alone and carries on unchanged, which stretches the circle out into a corkscrew.

Definition & formula

Cyclotron motion
The uniform circular motion executed by a charged particle whose velocity is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field, with the magnetic force supplying the entire centripetal force.

Set the magnetic force equal to the centripetal requirement and one factor of v cancels immediately: qvB = mv²/r gives r = mv/(qB). Read it as radius is momentum divided by charge times field — a stiffer (more energetic, heavier) particle bends less; a stronger field bends it more. This single relation is why a bubble-chamber photograph can be read as a momentum measurement, and why the magnet in a mass spectrometer sorts by mass.

Now compute the period. T = 2πr/v, and substituting r = mv/(qB) makes v cancel again: T = 2πm/(qB). This is the surprise of the chapter. A fast particle travels a bigger circle in exactly the same time as a slow one. Doubling the speed doubles the radius, doubles the circumference, and leaves the lap time untouched. That coincidence is what makes the cyclotron possible at all: one fixed-frequency oscillator can accelerate a particle for hundreds of turns without ever needing retuning.

Exam point · what the cyclotron frequency does and does not depend on

f = qB/(2πm) depends on charge-to-mass ratio and field only. It is independent of speed, of kinetic energy, and of radius. Standard values worth carrying: an electron gives 28 GHz per tesla; a proton gives 15.2 MHz per tesla. (The proton's NMR frequency, 42.58 MHz·T⁻¹, is a different quantity — spin precession, not orbital motion — but the two are often set side by side to catch you out.)

Helical motion: split the velocity, do not fight it

If v is not perpendicular to B, resolve it. The component v∥ = v cos θ along the field feels no force at all and is carried forward unchanged, at constant velocity. The component v⟂ = v sin θ across the field does the circling. Superimpose a uniform drift on a circle and you get a helix — radius set by v⟂, and pitch (the advance per turn) set by v∥ multiplied by the same old period. Charged particles from the solar wind spiral down Earth's field lines this way, which is why aurorae appear at the poles.

Worked example 2 · Electron in a millitesla field: radius, frequency, and the helix

An electron (m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, |q| = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C) moves at 3.0×10⁶ m·s⁻¹ in a uniform 1.0 mT field. Find (a) the radius and cyclotron frequency if v ⟂ B, and (b) the radius and pitch if v is instead at 60° to B.

  1. (a) Radius. r = mv/(|q|B) = (9.11×10⁻³¹ × 3.0×10⁶) / (1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.0×10⁻³)
  2. Numerator = 2.73×10⁻²⁴; denominator = 1.60×10⁻²²; r = 1.7×10⁻² m ≈ 1.7 cm.
  3. Frequency. f = |q|B/(2πm) = 1.60×10⁻²² / (2π × 9.11×10⁻³¹) = 1.60×10⁻²² / 5.72×10⁻³⁰ = 2.8×10⁷ Hz ≈ 28 MHz (consistent with the 28 GHz·T⁻¹ rule of thumb). Period T = 1/f = 3.6×10⁻⁸ s.
  4. (b) Helix. v⟂ = 3.0×10⁶ sin60° = 2.60×10⁶ m·s⁻¹, v∥ = 3.0×10⁶ cos60° = 1.5×10⁶ m·s⁻¹.
  5. r = m v⟂/(|q|B) = (9.11×10⁻³¹ × 2.60×10⁶)/(1.60×10⁻²²) = 1.5×10⁻² m ≈ 1.5 cm.
  6. Pitch = v∥T = 1.5×10⁶ × 3.58×10⁻⁸ = 5.4×10⁻² m ≈ 5.4 cm. Note the frequency is unchanged — only the radius shrank.

(a) r ≈ 1.7 cm, f ≈ 28 MHz  ·  (b) r ≈ 1.5 cm, pitch ≈ 5.4 cm

Trap · "the field slows it down"

In part (b) the speed is still 3.0×10⁶ m·s⁻¹ after any amount of time in the field — the helix has constant speed. Students who write "v⟂ decreases as the particle spirals in" are importing an intuition from friction or from an electron spiralling into a nucleus. In a uniform field with no radiation losses, the helix neither tightens nor loosens.

§ 04

Velocity selector, mass spectrometer, cyclotron

The ideaThree machines, and all three are built out of the two sections you have just read rather than out of anything new. The first sets an electric push, which does not care how fast a particle is going, against a magnetic push, which does — so only particles travelling at one particular speed sail straight through. The second then bends that speed-sorted beam and lets the size of the curve report how heavy each particle is, which is how you tell one isotope from another. The third exploits the constant lap time: give the particle a small shove every time it comes past, in step, hundreds of times over, and it leaves moving enormously faster than it arrived.

Definition & formula

Velocity selector (Wien filter)
A region with perpendicular electric and magnetic fields arranged so that the electric and magnetic forces cancel for exactly one speed; only particles at that speed pass undeflected.

The velocity selector is the cleanest illustration of the difference between the two forces. The electric force qE does not care how fast the particle is going; the magnetic force qvB is proportional to speed. Set them opposite and there is exactly one speed at which they balance — v = E/B. Slower particles are dominated by the electric force and deflect one way; faster ones are dominated by the magnetic force and deflect the other. Notice what has cancelled: q appears on both sides, so the filter selects on speed alone, regardless of charge, sign or mass.

A mass spectrometer bolts an analyser onto the back of that filter. All ions leave the selector at the same speed v = E/B; in the analyser field B′ each then bends with r = mv/(qB′). Since v and B′ are common to all of them, r ∝ m/q: the detector position is the mass-to-charge axis of the spectrum. In practice the detector sits on the diameter, so two isotopes land 2Δr apart.

Worked example 3 · Separating the neon isotopes

A velocity selector uses E = 3.0×10⁵ V·m⁻¹ crossed with B = 0.15 T. The beam then enters an analyser field B′ = 0.50 T. Singly ionised ²⁰Ne⁺ and ²²Ne⁺ (1 u = 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C) are present. How far apart do they strike the detector?

  1. Selected speed: v = E/B = 3.0×10⁵ / 0.15 = 2.0×10⁶ m·s⁻¹.
  2. Denominator common to both: qB′ = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.50 = 8.0×10⁻²⁰.
  3. ²⁰Ne⁺: m = 20 × 1.66×10⁻²⁷ = 3.32×10⁻²⁶ kg; r₁ = (3.32×10⁻²⁶ × 2.0×10⁶)/(8.0×10⁻²⁰) = 6.64×10⁻²⁰/8.0×10⁻²⁰ = 0.830 m.
  4. ²²Ne⁺: mass is 22/20 = 1.10× larger and r ∝ m, so r₂ = 1.10 × 0.830 = 0.913 m (check: 3.652×10⁻²⁶ × 2.0×10⁶ / 8.0×10⁻²⁰ = 0.913 m).
  5. Both strike the plate at a distance 2r from the entry slit, so the separation is 2(r₂ − r₁) = 2(0.913 − 0.830) = 0.166 m.

Landing points ≈ 17 cm apart (r₁ = 0.83 m, r₂ = 0.91 m)

The cyclotron, and where it stops working

Two hollow D-shaped electrodes sit in a uniform vertical field, with an alternating voltage across the gap between them. Inside a dee there is no electric field, so the particle merely coasts round a semicircle; every time it crosses the gap the polarity has reversed and it is accelerated again. Because the period is speed-independent, the oscillator can be set once, at f = qB/(2πm), and left alone. Each kick raises the speed, which raises the radius, so the path is a spiral of ever-larger semicircles — but always with the same lap time. At the outer radius R the speed is v = qBR/m, so K = ½mv² = q²B²R²/(2m).

Worked example 4 · A proton cyclotron

A cyclotron for protons has B = 1.5 T and dee radius R = 0.50 m (m = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C). Find the oscillator frequency and the extraction energy.

  1. f = qB/(2πm) = (1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.5)/(2π × 1.67×10⁻²⁷) = 2.40×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.049×10⁻²⁶ = 2.29×10⁷ Hz ≈ 22.9 MHz.
  2. Exit speed v = qBR/m = (1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.5 × 0.50)/1.67×10⁻²⁷ = 1.20×10⁻¹⁹/1.67×10⁻²⁷ = 7.2×10⁷ m·s⁻¹.
  3. K = ½mv² = ½ × 1.67×10⁻²⁷ × (7.19×10⁷)² = ½ × 1.67×10⁻²⁷ × 5.17×10¹⁵ = 4.3×10⁻¹² J.
  4. In electronvolts: 4.31×10⁻¹² / 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ = 2.7×10⁷ eV ≈ 27 MeV.

f ≈ 22.9 MHz  ·  K ≈ 4.3×10⁻¹² J ≈ 27 MeV

That exit speed is 0.24c, where the relativistic mass increase is already about 3%. The lap time therefore lengthens slightly with each turn and the beam drifts out of step with the fixed oscillator. This is the hard ceiling of the classical cyclotron (~25 MeV for protons); beyond it you must sweep the frequency (synchrocyclotron) or ramp the field (synchrotron). Medical cyclotrons that make ¹⁸F for PET run at 10–18 MeV, comfortably inside the classical regime.

Trap · which field does the accelerating?

The magnetic field in a cyclotron supplies no energy whatsoever. All 27 MeV comes from the electric field in the gap, delivered in a few hundred small instalments. The magnet's only job is to be a very reliable return spring. Any answer that says "the magnetic field accelerates the protons" is wrong by §02.

§ 05

Forces on currents, parallel wires, and the ampere

The ideaA current is nothing but charge on the move, so a wire carrying one feels exactly the sideways push of §02 — except that the push is now shared out along the whole wire and ends up shoving the metal itself. Lay two wires side by side and each one sits in the field the other makes, so each feels its neighbour. The result is the opposite of what you learn to expect from static charge: wires whose currents run the same way pull together, and wires whose currents run opposite ways push apart. That attraction is so reliable it was once used to define the unit of current.

Definition & formula

The sum of the magnetic forces on all the drifting charge carriers in the wire, transmitted to the lattice as a single force on the conductor itself.

Go deeper · where F = BIL comes from — adding up the force on every carrier

The wire formula is not a new law — it is the charge formula added up. Let the wire have cross-section A, carrier density n, and carrier charge q drifting at vd. A length L contains nAL carriers, each feeling q vd B, so the total is F = (nAL)(q vd B) = (n q vd A)(LB). The bracket n q vd A is precisely the current I. Hence F = BIL. The carriers are pushed sideways, pile up against the lattice, and drag the whole wire with them — which is also the origin of the Hall effect.

Parallel wires and the definition of the ampere

Wire 1 makes a field B₁ = μ₀I₁/(2πd) at the location of wire 2 (§07). Wire 2 then feels F = B₁I₂L, giving F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd). Applying the right-hand rule twice shows the direction: parallel (same-direction) currents attract; antiparallel currents repel. This is opposite to the electrostatic habit of "like repels like", and it is examined constantly. The physical picture: the field of wire 1 at wire 2 points one way, and reversing I₂ reverses the force, so the two cases must differ in sign.

Exam point · the old and the new ampere

Put I₁ = I₂ = 1 A and d = 1 m into the formula: F/L = (2×10⁻⁷ × 1 × 1)/1 = 2×10⁻⁷ N·m⁻¹. From 1948 until 2019 this was the definition of the ampere, which is why μ₀ was exactly 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m·A⁻¹ — it was a defined constant, not a measured one. Since the 2019 SI revision the ampere is fixed instead by declaring the elementary charge e = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ C exactly, and μ₀ has become an experimentally determined quantity that equals 4π×10⁻⁷ to about one part in 10¹⁰. For every calculation in this course, 4π×10⁻⁷ (or the handy shortcut μ₀/2π = 2×10⁻⁷) is exact enough.

Worked example 5 · Force between two parallel feeders

Two long parallel wires 8.0 cm apart carry 12 A and 5.0 A in the same direction. Find the force per metre, and the total force on a 1.5 m length. Attractive or repulsive?

  1. F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd). Use μ₀/2π = 2.0×10⁻⁷.
  2. F/L = (2.0×10⁻⁷ × 12 × 5.0)/0.080 = (1.2×10⁻⁵)/0.080 = 1.5×10⁻⁴ N·m⁻¹.
  3. Over 1.5 m: F = 1.5×10⁻⁴ × 1.5 = 2.3×10⁻⁴ N.
  4. Same direction ⟹ attraction.

F/L = 1.5×10⁻⁴ N·m⁻¹ · F = 2.3×10⁻⁴ N · attractive

Tiny at these currents — but the force scales as the product of the currents, so two busbars carrying a 50 kA fault current at this same 8.0 cm spacing feel about 4×10⁷ times as much: F/L = (2.0×10⁻⁷ × 5.0×10⁴ × 5.0×10⁴)/0.080 ≈ 6.3×10³ N·m⁻¹, over six kilonewtons per metre. That is why busbars are bolted down, and the same I² pinch is what crushes the plasma column in a fusion Z-pinch.

Trap · a closed loop in a uniform field feels no net force

Add up I L × B around a closed loop in a uniform field: the L vectors sum to zero, so the net force is zero. A current loop in a uniform field does not translate. It rotates (§06). Loops are pulled bodily only by non-uniform fields — which is exactly why a magnet attracts a paperclip (field gradient) and why the MRI projectile hazard depends on B·∇B rather than on B alone.

§ 06

Torque on a current loop: motors and galvanometers

The ideaPut a loop of current in a magnetic field and opposite sides of the loop get pushed opposite ways. The loop therefore does not go anywhere — it twists. Left alone it would behave like a compass needle: swing round until it lines up with the field, and stop. The trick behind every electric motor is to reverse the current at exactly the moment it lines up, so the twist never runs out and the loop keeps turning. A moving-needle meter uses the same twisting effect held back by a spring, so how far the needle swings tells you how much current is passing.

Take a rectangular loop of sides a and b in a uniform field, with the field in the plane of the loop. The two sides parallel to B feel no force. The two sides perpendicular to it feel F = BIa in opposite directions, separated by b — a couple. Torque = BIa · b = BIA. Multiply by N turns and, for a general orientation, by sin θ, and you have the hero formula. The derivation used a rectangle, but the result τ = μB sin θ holds for any planar loop, because any shape can be tiled by rectangles whose internal edges cancel.

Sign-convention trap · θ is measured from the normal, not from the plane

When the plane of the coil contains B, the normal is perpendicular to B, so θ = 90° and the torque is maximum. When the plane is perpendicular to B (field threading straight through the loop), θ = 0 and the torque is zero. Students routinely invert this because "parallel" sounds like alignment. Write down which vector you are measuring from before substituting, every time.

Worked example 6 · Torque and energy of a coil

A 50-turn rectangular coil measuring 4.0 cm × 3.0 cm carries 0.60 A in a uniform 0.25 T field. Its normal makes 60° with the field. Find μ, the torque, the maximum possible torque, and the orientation energy.

  1. A = 0.040 × 0.030 = 1.2×10⁻³ m².
  2. μ = NIA = 50 × 0.60 × 1.2×10⁻³ = 3.6×10⁻² A·m².
  3. μB = 3.6×10⁻² × 0.25 = 9.0×10⁻³ N·m — this is already the maximum torque (θ = 90°).
  4. τ = μB sin60° = 9.0×10⁻³ × 0.866 = 7.8×10⁻³ N·m.
  5. U = −μB cos60° = −9.0×10⁻³ × 0.500 = −4.5×10⁻³ J.

μ = 3.6×10⁻² A·m² · τ = 7.8×10⁻³ N·m · τmax = 9.0×10⁻³ N·m · U = −4.5 mJ

Why a motor needs a commutator

The torque μB sin θ drives the loop toward alignment — and then reverses, because past alignment the sine changes sign. Left alone the coil would oscillate about θ = 0 and stop. A split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil at the instant it passes through alignment, which flips μ and keeps the torque pushing the same way round. That single mechanical trick is the whole difference between a coil that twitches and a DC motor. The torque still varies as |sin θ| over each half-turn, so real motors use many coils at staggered angles to smooth it out.

A moving-coil galvanometer solves the same problem differently. Curved pole pieces and a soft-iron core make the field radial, so wherever the coil sits, B is always in the plane of the coil: θ is permanently 90° and τ = NIAB exactly, with no sine. A hairspring supplies a restoring torque kφ, so at equilibrium NIAB = kφ and the deflection φ is strictly proportional to I. That is why an analogue meter has a linear scale — the radial field was engineered to remove the sine.

Exam point · what a dipole actually is

A current loop, a bar magnet, an orbiting electron and a proton spin are all μ in a field. The same three formulas apply: torque μB sin θ, energy −μB cos θ, and no net force in a uniform field. In MRI the last one is replaced by precession, because a proton's moment is tied to angular momentum: instead of swinging into alignment like a compass needle, it precesses about B at the Larmor frequency (§11).

§ 07

Fields made by currents · Ampère's law

The ideaEverything so far has asked what a magnetic field does to a current. This section asks the reverse question — where the field came from in the first place. The answer is that every current carries its own magnetic field with it, wrapped around the wire in rings. Bend the wire into a circle and all those rings crowd through the middle together; wind it into a coil a thousand times and the inside becomes a strong, even field. That is how every magnet in a laboratory is built, and there is a shortcut that lets you work out the strength directly whenever the arrangement is symmetrical enough.

Definition & formula

Ampère's law is the magnetic twin of Gauss's law, and it is useful for the same reason and under the same restriction: it is always true but only solvable when symmetry lets you pull B outside the integral. The recipe is always the same three moves — argue from symmetry what direction B must have, choose a path on which its magnitude is constant, then read off B.

The three symmetry arguments worth knowing

Long straight wire. The wire looks identical if you rotate about it or slide along it, so B can only depend on r, and (since field lines never end) it must run in circles around the wire. Take a circle of radius r as the path: B is parallel to dl everywhere and constant in magnitude, so ∮B·dl = B(2πr) = μ₀I and B = μ₀I/(2πr). Note the 1/r falloff — slower than the 1/r² of a point charge, because the source is an infinite line.

Long solenoid. Outside a long solenoid the field is very nearly zero (the returning flux is spread over all of space); inside it is uniform and axial. Take a rectangular path with one long side of length ℓ inside along the axis, one outside, and two short crossing sides. The outside side contributes nothing (B ≈ 0); the two crossing sides contribute nothing (B ⟂ dl). Only the inside leg survives: Bℓ = μ₀(nℓ)I, so B = μ₀nI. Length cancels — the field depends on turns per metre, not on total turns.

Where Ampère's law fails you. There is no symmetry that lets you compute the field at the centre of a single circular loop with Ampère's law; that result, B = μ₀I/(2R), comes from integrating the Biot–Savart law. Knowing which tool produced which formula is itself an exam-worthy distinction.

Worked example 7 · Wire, loop, solenoid — same current, three fields

A current of 10 A flows in the first two arrangements. Find (a) the field 5.0 cm from a long straight wire, (b) the field at the centre of a single circular loop of radius 5.0 cm, and (c) the field inside a solenoid of 800 turns wound over 0.40 m carrying 3.0 A. Use μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m·A⁻¹ (μ₀/2π = 2.0×10⁻⁷).

  1. (a) B = μ₀I/(2πr) = (2.0×10⁻⁷ × 10)/0.050 = 2.0×10⁻⁶/0.050 = 4.0×10⁻⁵ T = 40 μT — comparable to Earth's field.
  2. (b) B = μ₀I/(2R) = (4π×10⁻⁷ × 10)/(2 × 0.050) = 1.257×10⁻⁵/0.100 = 1.26×10⁻⁴ T ≈ 126 μT.
  3. Sanity check on (b): the loop-centre formula is π times the straight-wire formula at the same distance, and indeed 40 × π = 126 μT. Bending the wire into a ring brings all of it close to the centre, so the field is bigger.
  4. (c) n = 800/0.40 = 2000 m⁻¹; B = μ₀nI = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 2000 × 3.0 = 1.257×10⁻⁶ × 6000 = 7.5×10⁻³ T = 7.5 mT.

(a) 40 μT · (b) 126 μT · (c) 7.5 mT

Three geometries, comparable currents, and a spread of nearly 200× from the bare wire to the solenoid — the winding, not the current, is what buys you field. Getting to the 1.5 T of an MRI magnet from here requires either an iron core (which multiplies μ₀ by a relative permeability of thousands) or a superconducting winding carrying hundreds of amperes with no resistive heating — which is what a scanner actually uses.

Exam point · scaling questions are free marks

Most field questions never need μ₀ at all, only the proportionality: straight wire B ∝ I/r, loop centre B ∝ NI/R, solenoid B ∝ NI/ℓ, far-field dipole B ∝ 1/z³. "Triple the distance from a wire" divides B by 3, not by 9 — the inverse-square instinct from electrostatics is the intended trap.

§ 08

Magnetic flux and Faraday's law

The ideaThink about how much of a magnetic field actually passes through a loop of wire — not how strong the field is, but how much of it threads the hole. That quantity is what the rest of the note is about. The discovery that made electricity practical is this: a steady amount does nothing whatsoever, so a coil parked inside the strongest magnet ever built reads zero volts forever. A voltage appears only while the amount is changing, and the faster it changes the bigger the voltage. That is why generators have to spin.

Definition & formula

The amount of magnetic field passing through a surface: field strength × the area presented perpendicular to it. Picture it as the number of field lines threading a loop.

Flux is the bookkeeping variable of the whole second half of this chapter. Note the cos θ: it is measured from the normal to the loop, exactly as in §06. Flux is maximum when the field threads straight through the loop (θ = 0, Φ = BA) and zero when the field skims along the plane of the loop (θ = 90°). A flat loop lying on a table in the Earth's field links the vertical component of that field, not the whole of it.

Faraday's law says the induced EMF tracks dΦ/dt. Two consequences follow immediately and are both examined. First, a steady flux, however large, induces nothing — park a coil inside a 3 T magnet and it will read zero volts forever. Second, the EMF depends on how fast, not how far — pull a magnet out of a coil twice as quickly and you double the EMF while the total flux change stays the same. (The charge that flows, Q = ∫I dt = NΔΦ/R, depends only on the flux change and not on the speed — that is the principle of the ballistic galvanometer.)

Exam point · the rotating-coil generator

Spin a coil at constant angular velocity ω so that θ = ωt. Then Φ = BA cos ωt and ε = −N dΦ/dt = NBAω sin ωt. Three readings to take away: the output is sinusoidal (this is why mains electricity is AC), the peak EMF εmax = NBAω grows with rotation speed, and the EMF peaks when the flux is zero — a quarter cycle out of phase, because EMF tracks the rate of change, not the value. "The EMF is greatest when the flux is greatest" is the classic wrong answer.

§ 09

Lenz's law and motional EMF

The ideaWhen a changing field drives a current, that current makes a field of its own — and it always arranges itself to fight whatever you did to cause it. Push a magnet toward a metal ring and the ring pushes back; pull it away and the ring tries to hold on. This is not an extra rule of nature bolted on for tidiness, it is energy conservation in disguise: if the ring helped you along instead of resisting, you could get electricity out of a single nudge and never stop. So generating electricity always costs work, and the work you put in is exactly what comes out the other end.

Definition & formula

The induced current flows in whichever direction makes its own magnetic field oppose the change in flux that produced it. It is the minus sign in Faraday's law, spelled out.

Why the minus sign has to be there

Suppose Lenz's law ran the other way — that the induced current reinforced the change that made it. Push a magnet slightly toward a coil and the coil would pull it in harder; that would speed the magnet up, which would increase dΦ/dt, which would increase the current, which would pull harder still. You would extract unlimited energy from a nudge. Lenz's law is conservation of energy wearing an electromagnetic costume. Induction always resists you, and the electrical energy you get out is exactly the mechanical work you put in.

A reliable four-step recipe for the direction

  1. Decide the direction of B through the loop (into the page, downward, along the axis…).
  2. Decide whether the flux in that direction is increasing or decreasing.
  3. The induced current's own field inside the loop must point opposite to the change — opposing B if the flux is rising, reinforcing B if it is falling.
  4. Use the right-hand grip rule to convert "field inside the loop points this way" into a current sense (clockwise / counter-clockwise as seen from a stated viewpoint).

Applied to three standard cases: a loop pulled out of a field region — flux through it is falling, so the current flows to maintain it (same sense as the original field, by the grip rule). A loop shrinking inside a steady field — area falling, flux falling, same conclusion. A switch closed in a neighbouring circuit — flux suddenly rising, so the induced current runs the opposite way round to the primary current, and only during the switching transient.

Go deeper · motional EMF derived twice — from the carriers, and from Faraday's law

Motional EMF, derived twice

Route 1 — force on the carriers. A rod of length L moves at speed v across a field B. Every free electron shares the rod's velocity, so each feels a magnetic force qvB along the rod. Charge piles up at one end until the electric field it creates cancels the magnetic push: qE = qvB, so E = vB inside the rod, and the potential difference across its ends is ε = EL = BLv.

Route 2 — Faraday's law. If the rod slides on rails a distance x from the closed end, the circuit encloses area A = Lx and flux Φ = BLx. Then |ε| = dΦ/dt = BL dx/dt = BLv. Same answer, from a completely different starting point — which is the reason to trust it.

Worked example 8 · The full energy audit of a sliding rod

A rod of length 0.30 m slides on frictionless rails at a constant 2.0 m·s⁻¹, perpendicular to a uniform 0.40 T field. The circuit's total resistance is 0.50 Ω. Find the EMF, the current, the force needed to maintain constant speed, and check the energy balance.

  1. ε = BLv = 0.40 × 0.30 × 2.0 = 0.24 V.
  2. I = ε/R = 0.24/0.50 = 0.48 A.
  3. Magnetic force on the rod: F = BIL = 0.40 × 0.48 × 0.30 = 0.058 N, directed backwards. At constant speed the applied force must equal it: 0.058 N forward.
  4. Mechanical power in: P = Fv = 0.0576 × 2.0 = 0.115 W.
  5. Electrical power out: P = I²R = (0.48)² × 0.50 = 0.2304 × 0.50 = 0.115 W. They agree exactly — as Lenz's law requires.

ε = 0.24 V · I = 0.48 A · F = 0.058 N · P = 0.115 W in and out

Trap · "opposes the flux" vs "opposes the change in flux"

The induced current opposes the change, not the field. If the flux through a loop is downward and decreasing, the induced current makes a downward field inside the loop — reinforcing the existing field in order to resist its loss. Answers that always oppose the field itself get half the direction questions backwards.

§ 10

Eddy currents, self-inductance and magnetic energy

The ideaNothing in the last section required a neat loop of wire. Change the field through a solid lump of metal and currents swirl about inside it, dragging on whatever caused the change — which is how some trains brake without anything touching, and how a hob heats a pan through cold glass. A coil does the same thing to itself: its own changing current fights its own change, so a coil behaves in a circuit rather as mass behaves in mechanics, resisting any attempt to alter how fast things are moving. Building the field up takes work, and that work does not sit in the metal — it sits in the field, waiting to be handed back.

Eddy currents: induction in a solid block

Nothing in Faraday's law requires a wire. Change the flux through a slab of aluminium and currents circulate inside the metal, in whatever closed paths the geometry allows. By Lenz's law they oppose the change, so a metal plate swinging between the poles of a magnet is braked sharply and stops as though moving through treacle — magnetic braking, the mechanism behind contactless brakes on trains and roller-coasters and the damping of laboratory balances. It is frictionless (nothing touches) and self-limiting: the force is proportional to speed, so it fades smoothly to zero rather than jolting to a stop, which is also why eddy brakes cannot hold a stationary vehicle.

The same currents are a nuisance in transformer and motor cores, where they simply waste energy as heat. The cure is to break up the conducting paths: cores are built from thin laminations separated by insulating varnish, or from ferrite, so that large eddy loops cannot form. Run the process deliberately at high frequency and the heat becomes the point — that is an induction cooktop, and it is also the origin of the RF heating limit (SAR) in MRI.

Self-inductance: a circuit's electrical inertia

A coil's own current makes flux through its own turns. Change the current and that flux changes, and Faraday's law produces an EMF in the coil that — by Lenz — opposes the change. The constant of proportionality is L. Two features of L = μ₀N²A/ℓ deserve attention: the N² (one factor because N turns make N times the field, another because N turns each link that flux), and the fact that L is pure geometry — it depends on shape, size and turns, never on the current flowing.

Mechanically, L is inertia. A resistor limits current; an inductor limits the rate of change of current. Close a switch on an LR circuit and the current does not jump to V/R but climbs exponentially with time constant τ = L/R: at t = τ it has reached 63% of its final value, at 3τ about 95%, and it is treated as complete after ~5τ. Open the switch and the collapsing field can generate an enormous back-EMF (dI/dt is huge), which is why unloading an inductor makes a spark and why every relay coil in electronics has a flyback diode across it.

Exam point · the LR circuit at t = 0 and t = ∞

At the instant a switch closes, an inductor behaves like an open circuit (it forbids a sudden current). After a long time, with the current steady, dI/dt = 0 so the back-EMF vanishes and it behaves like a plain wire (a short). Those two limits answer most LR multiple-choice questions without any exponentials. Note that this is the mirror image of a capacitor, which starts as a short and ends as an open circuit.

Where the energy sits

Building up a current against the back-EMF takes work, and that work is stored: U = ½LI². But stored where? Not in the wire — in the field. Substitute the solenoid results L = μ₀n²Aℓ and B = μ₀nI into ½LI² and the volume Aℓ factors out, leaving an energy per unit volume u = B²/(2μ₀). This is the exact magnetic analogue of the electric u = ½ε₀E², and it is the statement that fields are physical things carrying energy — the idea that eventually makes electromagnetic waves (note 14) possible.

Worked example 9 · Inductance, stored energy, and where it lives

A solenoid of 500 turns is wound over a length of 0.25 m with cross-sectional area 4.0 cm². It carries 2.0 A and is in series with a 5.0 Ω resistor. Find L, the stored energy, and the LR time constant; then confirm the energy by using the energy density instead.

  1. n = 500/0.25 = 2000 m⁻¹; A = 4.0 cm² = 4.0×10⁻⁴ m².
  2. L = μ₀n²Aℓ = (4π×10⁻⁷)(2000)²(4.0×10⁻⁴)(0.25). The bracket product (4.0×10⁶)(4.0×10⁻⁴)(0.25) = 400, so L = 1.257×10⁻⁶ × 400 = 5.0×10⁻⁴ H = 0.50 mH.
  3. U = ½LI² = ½ × 5.03×10⁻⁴ × (2.0)² = 1.0×10⁻³ J = 1.0 mJ.
  4. τ = L/R = 5.03×10⁻⁴/5.0 = 1.0×10⁻⁴ s = 0.10 ms.
  5. Cross-check via the field. B = μ₀nI = 1.257×10⁻⁶ × 2000 × 2.0 = 5.03×10⁻³ T; u = B²/(2μ₀) = (5.03×10⁻³)²/(2 × 1.257×10⁻⁶) = 2.53×10⁻⁵/2.51×10⁻⁶ = 10.1 J·m⁻³.
  6. Interior volume = Aℓ = 4.0×10⁻⁴ × 0.25 = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m³, so U = 10.1 × 1.0×10⁻⁴ = 1.0×10⁻³ J — the same 1.0 mJ.

L = 0.50 mH · U = 1.0 mJ · τ = 0.10 ms · u = 10 J·m⁻³

For scale: an MRI magnet at 1.5 T stores u = (1.5)²/(2 × 1.257×10⁻⁶) ≈ 9.0×10⁵ J·m⁻³. Over a bore volume of a few cubic metres that is megajoules — which is why a magnet quench (sudden loss of superconductivity, dumping that energy as heat into the helium) is a violent event rather than an inconvenience.

§ 11

Transformers, AC transmission, and induction in medicine

The ideaWind two separate coils onto the same iron ring and the changing field made by one reaches straight into the other, handing power across with no electrical connection at all. Give the two coils different numbers of turns and the voltage comes out changed in the same proportion — which is why electricity is sent across the country at enormous voltage and tiny current, since it is the current, not the voltage, that wastes energy heating the cables. The same handover happens in a hospital: a scanner listens for the faint voltage that spinning atomic nuclei induce in a coil, and a stimulating coil held against the scalp induces enough voltage inside the head to make nerves fire, without anything touching the brain.

A transformer is Faraday's law used twice. The alternating primary current drives an alternating flux Φ round the iron core; each primary turn experiences −dΦ/dt and so does each secondary turn, so Vp = NpdΦ/dt and Vs = NsdΦ/dt. Divide, and Φ cancels: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np. Energy conservation does the rest — if the device is ideal it cannot create power, so raising the voltage must lower the current in exactly the same ratio.

Trap · a transformer does nothing on DC

Connect a battery to the primary and the secondary reads zero, except for a single blip at switch-on and another at switch-off. Steady current means steady flux means dΦ/dt = 0 means no EMF. This is the honest answer to "why is the grid AC?" — not because AC is intrinsically better, but because only a changing flux can be transformed, and voltage transformation is what makes long-distance transmission viable. (Modern HVDC links exist precisely because power electronics can now do the conversion without a transformer.)

Worked example 10 · Why the grid runs at high voltage

100 kW must be delivered along a line of total resistance 0.50 Ω. Compare the resistive loss if it is sent at 250 V with the loss if a transformer steps it up to 25 kV. Then find the turns ratio needed to bring 25 kV back down to 240 V, and the primary current when the 240 V side supplies 2.0 A.

  1. At 250 V: I = P/V = 100 000/250 = 400 A; Ploss = I²R = (400)² × 0.50 = 160 000 × 0.50 = 8.0×10⁴ W = 80 kW — 80% of the power, i.e. the load could never be supplied.
  2. At 25 kV: I = 100 000/25 000 = 4.0 A; Ploss = (4.0)² × 0.50 = 8.0 W — 0.008%.
  3. Raising the voltage by 100× cut the loss by 100² = 10⁴, because Ploss ∝ 1/V² at fixed delivered power.
  4. Turns ratio: Ns/Np = Vs/Vp = 240/25 000 = 1/104 — a step-down of about 104 : 1.
  5. Primary current: ideal, so VpIp = VsIs: Ip = (240 × 2.0)/25 000 = 480/25 000 = 0.019 A.

Magnetic resonance imaging

MRI uses every idea on this page. A superconducting solenoid supplies a static field B₀ of 1.5–3.0 T. Hydrogen nuclei — overwhelmingly the protons of water and fat — carry a magnetic moment tied to their spin, so instead of simply aligning they precess about B₀ at the Larmor frequency f = γB₀/2π, which for ¹H is 42.58 MHz per tesla: 63.9 MHz at 1.5 T, 127.7 MHz at 3.0 T — squarely in the radio band. A slight population imbalance between the two spin states leaves a small net magnetisation along B₀.

A resonant RF pulse tips that magnetisation away from the axis, and it then precesses in the transverse plane. A rotating magnetic moment is a changing flux through the receiver coil, so by Faraday's law it induces an EMF — that induced voltage is the MR signal. Position is encoded by gradient coils, which add a field varying linearly with position so that the resonance frequency becomes a map of location. Contrast comes from the relaxation times T₁ and T₂, which differ between tissues, not from the field itself. Three consequences of the physics show up as clinical practice: the switching gradients push on each other through IL×B forces and produce the scanner's hammering noise; a sufficiently fast dB/dt induces enough current in tissue to stimulate peripheral nerves, which sets a regulatory ceiling on gradient slew rate; and RF absorption is limited by the specific absorption rate to avoid heating. The projectile hazard, meanwhile, depends on the gradient of B (§05), which is why the danger zone is at the mouth of the bore rather than at its centre.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

TMS is Faraday's law used as a therapy. A capacitor bank discharges several kiloamperes through a figure-of-eight coil held against the scalp, producing a peak field of order 1–2 T at the coil face that rises and falls in roughly 100 μs. That gives dB/dt ≈ 10⁴ T·s⁻¹, and the changing flux induces an electric field of order 100 V·m⁻¹ in the cortex a few centimetres away — enough to depolarise axons and fire neurons. Nothing touches the brain and no current is passed through the skin, because induction does not need a conducting path from the source; the skull's high resistance, which so blurs electrical stimulation, is simply irrelevant to a magnetic field. Single pulses over the motor cortex produce a measurable twitch and are used to map cortical function and measure central conduction time; repetitive TMS is an approved treatment for medication-resistant depression. The figure-of-eight geometry matters for the same reason the solenoid formula does — two adjacent loops with opposite currents superpose to give a sharp field maximum under the junction, which is what buys spatial focality.

Exam point · which law does each device use?

Motor and galvanometer: force/torque on a current (§05–06). Generator, transformer, induction hob, eddy brake, TMS and the MRI receive chain: Faraday's law (§08–09). Mass spectrometer and cyclotron: circular motion of charge (§03–04). A question that names a device is usually asking only which of those three boxes it belongs in.
