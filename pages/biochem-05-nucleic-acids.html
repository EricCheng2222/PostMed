Biochemistry · Note 05

Nucleic Acids: DNA, RNA and the Central Dogma

One property organises this whole note: a nucleic acid chain is directional. The two ends of the polymer are chemically different, so a sequence read one way is not the sequence read the other way. Every rule that follows — why synthesis is always 5′→3′, why the strands are antiparallel, why a lagging strand has to exist, why there is a reading frame at all, why a deletion of one base is catastrophic and a deletion of three is survivable — is a consequence of that single asymmetry. Add the second structural fact, that the information sits on the inside of the helix while the charge sits on the outside, and replication, transcription, translation and the drugs that interrupt them stop being lists.

§ 01

The nucleotide

The ideaEverything on this page is built out of one repeating unit, and that unit has three pieces: a flat ring that carries the message, a sugar that the ring is bolted onto, and a phosphate that will become the joint to the next unit along. The three pieces have completely separate jobs. Almost every rule later in the note turns out to be a rule about the sugar or the phosphate rather than about the message-carrying ring, so the first habit worth building is simply naming which of the three a question is actually about.

Definition & key facts

Nucleoside
A nitrogenous base joined to a pentose sugar by an N-glycosidic bond — N9 of a purine or N1 of a pyrimidine to C1′ of the sugar.
nucleotide = base + pentose + phosphate drop the phosphate and you have a nucleoside; drop the sugar too and you have a base

Nucleotide
A nucleoside carrying one or more phosphate groups esterified to a sugar hydroxyl — the 5′-OH unless stated otherwise. A nucleotide is the monomer; a nucleoside cannot be polymerised.

Start with the numbering, because it is the source of most avoidable confusion. The base ring atoms are numbered 1, 2, 3…; the sugar carbons are therefore numbered 1′, 2′, 3′, 4′, 5′ with a prime so the two systems never collide. Every direction statement in molecular biology — 5′→3′, 3′-OH, 5′ cap — refers to the sugar, never the base. Use the prime character ′, not an apostrophe, and you will never misread a question.

Which are which

PURe As Gold → PURines are A and G. CUT the PY → C, U, T are PYrimidines. And note the irony that makes it stick: pyrimidine is the longer word for the smaller molecule.

Nucleoside or nucleotide — the letter T decides it

nucleoTide = phosphaTe · only a T can be tied into a chain

The one letter that separates the two words is the T of phosphaTe. So a nucleoside is base + sugar and nothing else, and a nucleotide is that same molecule with a phosphate on it. The second half of the hook carries the consequence: that phosphate is the joint to the next unit along, so only a tide can be tied into a chain — "the nucleotide is the monomer" then needs no separate memorising either. (In Chinese the pair is 核苷 and 核苷酸.) This is spelling rather than a circulating hook, which is exactly why it never decays — you cannot forget it without forgetting the word. It fixes the pair and nothing else: it does not tell you how many phosphates (NMP, NDP, NTP) or which hydroxyl carries them (the 5′-OH unless the question says otherwise).

Why ATP's phosphates are "high energy" and the backbone's are not

The sugar–phosphate link in a nucleic acid is a phosphoester: phosphate to carbon through oxygen, ΔG°′ of hydrolysis about −14 kJ·mol⁻¹. The bonds between the α, β and γ phosphates of are phosphoanhydrides: phosphate to phosphate, ΔG°′ about −30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹. Three things account for the difference, and none of them is "the bond stores energy" — the phrase high-energy bond is a misnomer for a large negative free energy of reaction:

  1. Electrostatic strain. At pH 7 the triphosphate carries three to four negative charges crowded together. Hydrolysis lets them separate.
  2. Resonance stabilisation of the products. Free Pi delocalises its charge over four equivalent oxygens; the same phosphorus inside an anhydride cannot.
  3. Solvation. Two separate products are hydrated better than one joined molecule.

This matters immediately for polymerisation. Every polymerase reaction releases pyrophosphate, and a separate enzyme, inorganic pyrophosphatase, hydrolyses PPi to 2 Pi. That second, strongly exergonic step is what makes chain extension effectively irreversible — the same trick used in aminoacyl-tRNA synthesis and fatty-acid activation.

α β γ on a phosphate chain

Counted outward from the sugar: α is the phosphate esterified to the 5′ carbon, then β, then γ at the far end. This is the sense used throughout this page — the α phosphate is the one the growing chain's 3′-OH attacks in § 02, and the one whose displacement releases PPi. α β γ on a carbon chain

The α carbon is the one next to the carboxyl, then β, then γ, and on into δ and ε. This page uses that sense too: the pyrimidine catabolites β-alanine and β-aminoisobutyrate above, and the lysine ε-amino group that histone acetyltransferases modify in § 05. Same letters, a different thing being counted.

α β on a sugar ring

In a sugar the letters mark the anomeric configuration — which face of the ring the C1 hydroxyl sits on, as in α- and β-D-glucose. That sense does not appear on this page, and it is worth keeping separate from the other two. Keep separate as well the letters that are simply names here and mark no position at all: DNA polymerases α, β, γ, δ and ε in § 07, α-amanitin and the bacterial α₂ββ′ω core in § 08, β-globin in § 08.

Nucleotides that never see a nucleic acid

Examiners like this because it tests whether you know a nucleotide is a chemical class, not a genetic one. ATP and GTP — energy currency and G-protein switches; cAMP and cGMP — second messengers made by cyclase enzymes and destroyed by phosphodiesterases (which is what sildenafil and caffeine inhibit); NAD⁺, NADP⁺, FAD, FMN — redox cofactors, each of which contains an AMP moiety; coenzyme A — carries a 3′-phospho-ADP; S-adenosylmethionine — the universal methyl donor; UDP-glucose and CDP-diacylglycerol — activated carriers for glycogen and phospholipid synthesis. Adenine turns up everywhere in metabolism, which is one of the tidier arguments for an RNA-first origin of life.

§ 02

The backbone, and why chains have polarity

The ideaJoin the units into a chain and something surprising falls out: the two ends of the finished chain are not the same as each other. One end still has a spare phosphate hanging off it, the other a spare hook on the sugar. So a chain has a front and a back, the way a written sentence does — read it the other way round and you have a different sentence, not the same one backwards. That single lopsidedness is why the machinery that builds these chains can only work in one direction, and it quietly explains a long list of facts that otherwise look like things you just have to memorise.

Why synthesis can only run 5′→3′

The mechanism answers it in one line: the 3′-OH of the growing chain attacks the of the incoming nucleoside triphosphate. The energy for the new bond therefore arrives with the monomer, not with the chain. The chain end contributes only a hydroxyl.

Now imagine the alternative. If chains grew 3′→5′, the activating triphosphate would have to sit on the growing chain's 5′ terminus, and each incoming nucleotide would supply only a 3′-OH. That arrangement is chemically possible — but it is fatally incompatible with proofreading. A polymerase that removes a mis-paired terminal nucleotide would, in the 3′→5′ scheme, chop off the chain's activating triphosphate, leaving a dead end that could never be extended again. In the real 5′→3′ scheme, excising a wrong nucleotide simply exposes a fresh 3′-OH and synthesis resumes. The direction of synthesis is the price of being able to correct mistakes, and it is the reason the lagging strand exists at all.

Three questions this one fact answers

Why is there a lagging strand? Because both new strands must grow 5′→3′ while the two templates run in opposite directions. Why does every DNA polymerase need a primer, while RNA polymerase and primase do not? Because a DNA polymerase can only extend an existing 3′-OH — it cannot create the first one. Why do dideoxy nucleotides terminate a Sanger sequencing reaction? Because ddNTPs have no 3′-OH, so nothing can be added after them.

There is no mnemonic for this section and you should not want one. Every fact people try to store separately here — synthesis runs 5′→3′, the template is read 3′→5′, DNA polymerases need a primer and RNA polymerase does not, ddNTPs terminate a chain, proofreading is possible at all, the lagging strand has to exist — is the same clause in different clothes: the 3′-OH of the growing chain attacks the α-phosphate of the incoming triphosphate. Learn that one line to the point where you can say it without thinking, and derive the other six in the exam hall. A mnemonic here would be a second thing to remember that can quietly drift out of step with the chemistry it stands for.

Consequences of being a polyanion

The remaining OH on each backbone phosphate has a pKa near 1, so it is fully ionised at every pH a cell or a gel will ever see. One nucleotide, one negative charge — exactly, with no sequence dependence. Five things follow directly:

  • Electrophoresis works and is honest. Charge is strictly proportional to length, so the charge-to-mass ratio is constant and mobility depends only on size (and on how the gel matrix impedes it). DNA always migrates towards the anode (+). Proteins need SDS to fake this property; DNA gets it for free.
  • Histones are basic. Packaging a polyanion requires a polycation, so the histones are loaded with lysine and arginine. So are protamines in sperm, and the polyamines spermine and spermidine.
  • Mg²⁺ is mandatory. Divalent cations screen the repulsion between backbone phosphates and sit in the active site of every polymerase and nuclease (the two-metal-ion mechanism). This is why EDTA — which chelates Mg²⁺ — is in every DNA storage buffer: no free Mg²⁺, no nuclease activity.
  • DNA is water-soluble despite a hydrophobic interior, because the charged surface faces the solvent. Add ethanol and salt and it precipitates: the Na⁺ neutralises the charge, the ethanol lowers the dielectric constant, and the polymer collapses. That is the whole basis of ethanol precipitation.
  • Silica columns. In high chaotropic salt the hydration shell is stripped and DNA binds silica; in low-salt buffer it releases. That is every miniprep kit you will ever use.

A sequence without a direction is meaningless

"GAATTC" is not a sequence; 5′-GAATTC-3′ is. If an exam question gives you a bare string, the convention is that it is written 5′→3′ — but if it explicitly says 3′-…-5′, that changes the answer completely. Read the primes before you read the letters. This single habit is worth more marks on this topic than any amount of memorising.

Exam technique · any question that hands you a sequence

  1. Primes before letters — before reading a single base, find the 5′ and 3′ labels and mark which end is which. If the string carries no primes at all, it is 5′→3′ by convention. Ten seconds spent here decides the whole question; every remaining step assumes you did it.
  2. Name the strand you were handed — coding (sense), template (antisense), or mRNA. The stem always says, in one word, and that word is the question. Underline it.
  3. Run the matching rule, not a general one — coding strand, 5′→3′ → the mRNA is that same string with U for T: no complementing, no reversing. Template written 3′→5′ → complement in place. Template written 5′→3′ → complement and reverse. Three rules, and picking the wrong one produces a confident, plausible, completely wrong peptide.
  4. Take the free self-check — whatever route you took, the mRNA you end up with must equal the coding strand with U for T. If your two lines disagree, you have caught your own error before you spent a minute translating it.
  5. Kill the manufactured options first — in a five-option sequence question the distractors are built from three standard slips: the given string simply reversed, the given string complemented when it should not have been, and the correct reading with the stop codon counted as a residue. Learn the shape of those three and you can eliminate two or three options before translating anything — and with −0.25 in Part I and −0.5 in Part II, a single elimination already makes answering strictly better than leaving it blank.
§ 03

Base pairing, stacking and Tm

The ideaTwo chains lie face to face because the rings sticking out of one fit the rings sticking out of the other in only one way, and always with a big ring opposite a small one — so every rung of the ladder is the same width and the whole helix keeps a constant thickness whatever the message says. What actually holds the two chains shut, though, is less that fitting-together than the fact that the flat rings pile up on one another like a stack of coins, tucked out of the way of the surrounding water. Heat the solution enough and the stack comes apart — at a temperature you can predict from what the chain is made of and what it is sitting in.

Definition & key facts

Complementarity
Adenine pairs with thymine (uracil in RNA) through two hydrogen bonds; guanine pairs with cytosine through three. A purine always faces a pyrimidine, so every rung of the ladder is the same width.

Watson–Crick geometry is constrained, not chosen. A purine paired with a purine would be too wide; two pyrimidines too narrow. Only purine·pyrimidine keeps the distance between the two C1′ atoms constant at about 10.8 Å, which is what allows a helix of uniform diameter to accommodate any sequence at all. That uniformity is the structural precondition for a general-purpose information store: the shape of the molecule must not depend on the message it carries.

The two strands run antiparallel — one 5′→3′ downwards, the other 5′→3′ upwards. This too is forced: the hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors on the base edges only line up when the glycosidic bonds point in opposite senses. And because the bases are joined edge-to-edge inside the helix while the backbones run outside, the molecule ends up with its information on the inside and its charge on the outside.

The pairing rules need no hook, because the notation is already the mnemonic. A = T — two bars, two hydrogen bonds. G ≡ C — three bars, three hydrogen bonds. Write the symbol and you have written the count, which is why the shorthand is worth using in your own working. Which letters are purines is the one genuinely arbitrary fact in this section, and § 01's PURe As Gold has already taken care of it. Everything else — antiparallel strands, constant helix width, purine facing pyrimidine — is forced geometry, and a question that asks "why" wants the geometry, not the list.

Stacking, not hydrogen bonding, is what holds the duplex together

This is the single most frequently mis-stated fact in the topic, and it follows directly from note 01. Ask the right question: when two single strands come together, what is actually gained?

Not hydrogen bonds. Before pairing, every donor and acceptor on every base edge was already hydrogen-bonded — to water. Forming the duplex swaps base–water hydrogen bonds for base–base hydrogen bonds, and the exchange is close to enthalpically neutral. What the hydrogen bonds actually supply is specificity: they are the reason A can only face T. They are the lock, not the glue.

The glue is base stacking — the flat aromatic faces piling on top of one another at 3.4 Å, which delivers exactly the two contributions note 01 put on the noncovalent energy ruler: the hydrophobic effect, because burying those apolar faces releases the ordered water that had been caging them (an entropic gain), and London dispersion between large, highly polarisable π systems in van der Waals contact. Measured stacking free energies run about 4–15 kJ·mol⁻¹ per dinucleotide step, and there are as many steps as base pairs. Stack them along a 20-mer and the duplex is unambiguously stacking-stabilised.

The trap: "G≡C has three hydrogen bonds, so GC-rich DNA is more stable"

The conclusion is right and the reason given is only half of it — which is exactly the kind of answer that gets marked down. GC-rich DNA melts higher for two reasons: the extra hydrogen bond, and the fact that G and C are the larger, more polarisable ring systems and therefore stack better with their neighbours. Nearest-neighbour thermodynamics makes this explicit — Tm depends on which dinucleotide steps are present, not merely on how many G·C pairs there are. A 5′-GC-3′ step and a 5′-CG-3′ step contain the same bases and have measurably different stabilities; hydrogen-bond counting cannot explain that, stacking can. If you write only "three hydrogen bonds", cross-reference note 01 and add the stacking sentence.

Melting, hyperchromicity and renaturation

Heat a DNA solution and the strands separate over a narrow temperature range — the transition is cooperative, because once a few pairs open, their neighbours lose their stacking partners and open more easily. The readout is optical: stacked bases partly quench one another's absorbance, so unstacking raises A₂₆₀ by roughly 40 %. This is the hyperchromic effect, and its midpoint is Tm.

Four variables move Tm, and you should be able to predict the sign of each:

  • GC content ↑ → Tm ↑ (about +0.41 °C per 1 % GC) — extra hydrogen bond plus better stacking.
  • Salt ↑ → Tm ↑ — cations screen the repulsion between the two polyanionic backbones, which is destabilising in itself.
  • pH far from neutral → Tm ↓ — extreme pH ionises the ring nitrogens and destroys the hydrogen-bond pattern. Alkali is the classic way to denature DNA without heat.
  • Formamide, urea, DMSO → Tm ↓ — they hydrogen-bond to the bases and weaken stacking, which is why hybridisation buffers contain formamide (it lets you work at a lower, less damaging temperature).

Cooling slowly lets the strands find each other again — renaturation, the physical basis of every hybridisation method on this page: Southern and Northern blots, PCR priming, FISH and microarrays all depend on it. Renaturation is second-order, so it is faster for sequences present in many copies; that observation (C₀t analysis) is how repetitive DNA was discovered.

Worked example 1 — a Chargaff deduction

One strand of a double-stranded DNA molecule contains 32 % A, 28 % T and 18 % G. What is the guanine content of the duplex, and its overall GC content?

  1. Complete the strand first. The four bases must total 100 %, so C in that strand = 100 − 32 − 28 − 18 = 22 %.
  2. Write the complementary strand by pairing rules. Every A in strand 1 faces a T in strand 2, and so on: strand 2 is 32 % T, 28 % A, 18 % C, 22 % G.
  3. The duplex composition is the average of the two strands. A = (32 + 28)/2 = 30 %, and therefore T = 30 %. G = (18 + 22)/2 = 20 %, and therefore C = 20 %.
  4. Check: 30 + 30 + 20 + 20 = 100 ✓, and Chargaff's equalities now hold, which they did not for the single strand.
  5. GC content = 20 + 20 = 40 %. Purine/pyrimidine in the duplex = (30+20)/(20+30) = 1.00 — always 1.00 for any duplex, which is why that ratio is never a useful discriminator.
duplex G = 20 % · GC = 40 % · (A+T)/(G+C) = 1.50

Exam technique · Tm, Chargaff, and the two-formula trap

  1. Read the length before you read the numbers — a duplex quoted in bp and longer than ≈50 takes 81.5 + 16.6 log[Na⁺] + 0.41(%GC) − 600/N. A primer or oligo of ≈14–25 nt takes the Wallace rule, 2(A+T) + 4(G+C). Using the long formula on a 20-mer is out by ≈10 °C, and it is a reading error, not a chemistry error.
  2. If only one variable moves, only one term moves — when the stem changes GC content and nothing else, do not rebuild the formula: ΔTm = 0.41 × Δ(%GC). Twenty percentage points is +8.2 °C, not +20 °C. That exact distractor is on this page's own quiz, and it is the commonest slip on the formula anywhere.
  3. Bound the answer before you look at the options — at 0.05 M Na⁺ the first two terms are a fixed 59.9 °C, so a duplex a few hundred bp long lands roughly between 65 and 93 °C across the whole plausible GC range. A Wallace-rule 20-mer must land between 40 °C (all A·T) and 80 °C (all G·C). Options outside those windows die with no arithmetic at all.
  4. Sign-check the four modifiers — GC ↑ and salt ↑ raise Tm; formamide, urea, DMSO and pH far from neutral lower it. An option with a sign backwards is free to eliminate whatever number it quotes, and "higher GC destabilises" appears as a distractor more often than it deserves to.
  5. Chargaff: ask "one strand, or the duplex?" — %A = %T holds for a duplex and never for a single strand, so if the stem gives you one strand's composition the single-strand values will be sitting in the option list as bait. The procedure is fixed: complete the strand to 100 %, write the complement by pairing, average the two.
  6. Where the time goes — a one-term Tm shift or a Chargaff average is a 20-second question; do it on sight and bank the time. A full Tm from scratch, with the log term, is worth the full 66 seconds only if step 3 failed to eliminate down to two.
§ 04

A, B, Z and DNA topology

The ideaA long twisted ladder does not simply sit there. It can be wound tighter or looser than it would like to be, and if its ends are joined into a loop, or pinned down, that surplus winding has nowhere to escape to — so the whole molecule writhes and coils around itself, exactly like an over-twisted telephone cord. Cells deliberately keep their DNA a little under-wound, which leaves it primed to spring open wherever something needs to read it. Anything travelling along the helix makes the winding worse in front of itself, so there is a family of enzymes whose entire job is to cut the strands, let the strain out, and seal them up again.

Supercoiling: why it exists and why it is negative

Consider the arithmetic problem a replication or transcription bubble creates. Unwinding the duplex locally removes twist, but in a closed circle — or in a long chromosome anchored to a scaffold, which is topologically equivalent — Lk cannot change. So the twist you removed reappears as writhe: positive supercoils pile up ahead of the moving polymerase and negative supercoils behind it. Left unresolved, the torsional strain stalls the machine within a few thousand base pairs. Topoisomerases exist to solve exactly this.

Resting DNA is held negatively supercoiled (σ ≈ −0.06), which means underwound, which means the duplex is already primed to open. That is a stored free-energy reservoir favouring every process that needs strand separation — origin firing, promoter melting. It is not a defect; it is a setting.

Why these drugs are selective, and why they are dangerous

Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) are genuinely selective: DNA gyrase has no human counterpart, and bacterial topo IV differs enough from human topo II to be targeted. Etoposide and camptothecin are not selective at all — they hit the human enzymes, and their utility comes only from the fact that tumour cells replicate more. Note the shared mechanism: all of these are not simple catalytic inhibitors. They stabilise the covalent enzyme–DNA "cleavable complex", trapping the enzyme mid-reaction with the DNA cut. The lethal lesion is therefore a protein-linked double-strand break, which explains both the cytotoxicity and the therapy-related secondary leukaemias seen after etoposide.

Worked example 3 — the linking number of a plasmid

A closed circular plasmid is 5250 bp and is isolated at a superhelical density σ = −0.06. Find Lk₀, ΔLk and Lk. How many catalytic events would topoisomerase I need to relax it, and how many would topoisomerase II need?

  1. Relaxed linking number: Lk₀ = N / 10.5 = 5250 / 10.5 = 500.
  2. By definition σ = ΔLk / Lk₀, so ΔLk = σ × Lk₀ = −0.06 × 500 = −30.
  3. Lk = Lk₀ + ΔLk = 500 − 30 = 470. The molecule is underwound by 30 turns.
  4. If all the strain appeared as writhe with the twist unchanged, that is 30 negative supercoils (Wr = −30). In reality it partitions between Tw and Wr, but Lk = 470 either way — that is what "topological invariant" means.
  5. Topoisomerase I changes Lk by 1 per event → 30 events. Topoisomerase II changes it by 2 → 15 events, each costing ATP.
Lk₀ = 500 · ΔLk = −30 · Lk = 470 · topo I 30 events, topo II 15 events

Exam technique · a supercoiling calculation, in four lines

  1. Lk₀ first, always — Lk₀ = N / 10.5. Examiners pick N so this divides cleanly: 4200 → 400, 5250 → 500, 10 500 → 1000. If your division is not whole, you have misread N or the question wants something else — check before going on rather than after.
  2. ΔLk from σ, then add — ΔLk = σ × Lk₀, then Lk = Lk₀ + ΔLk. There is no third step; nothing else is ever needed.
  3. Sign-check for free — cellular DNA is negatively supercoiled (σ ≈ −0.06), so Lk must come out below Lk₀. Any option above Lk₀ is dead before you calculate, and any option offering a positive σ for resting DNA is dead on sight.
  4. If it asks for catalytic events — topo I changes Lk by 1 per event, topo II by 2, so the two answers are |ΔLk| and |ΔLk|/2. When the option list contains a number and its exact double, those two are the intended pair and the others are padding.
  5. Recognition rule — "closed circular", "plasmid", "relaxed" or "nicked" in a stem means this is a topology question, not a chemistry one. And "nicked" or "linearised" means Lk is undefined, not zero: the constraint has been removed, which is the point of the word.

Euchromatin, heterochromatin, and the two chemical switches

Euchromatin is loosely packed, nuclease-accessible and transcriptionally competent. Heterochromatin is condensed and silent: constitutive at centromeres and telomeres in every cell, facultative where a cell has decided to shut something down — the inactive X chromosome (the Barr body) being the canonical example, driven by the lncRNA XIST.

  • Histone acetylation → transcription ON. A histone acetyltransferase transfers an acetyl group onto a lysine ε-amino group. That removes the positive charge, weakens the electrostatic grip on the backbone, and opens the fibre. Acetylation is therefore always activating — the one histone mark with an unambiguous direction. HDACs reverse it and silence; HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat, romidepsin, and valproate as a side effect) are used in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.
  • DNA methylation → transcription OFF. DNMTs place a methyl group on cytosine C5, almost always in a CpG dinucleotide. Methylated CpG islands in promoters recruit methyl-CpG-binding proteins and HDACs, and the gene is silenced. This is the mechanism of genomic imprinting and X-inactivation, and hypermethylation of tumour-suppressor promoters (e.g. MLH1, BRCA1) is a common route to cancer without any change in sequence. Azacitidine and decitabine are DNMT inhibitors used in myelodysplastic syndrome.
  • Histone methylation is ambiguous — do not lump it with the other two. H3K4me3 marks active promoters; H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 mark silent heterochromatin. The mark's meaning depends on which residue carries it.

Which chemical mark opens chromatin and which closes it

Acetylation Activates · Methylation Mutes

Acetylating a lysine ε-amino group cancels its positive charge, weakens the grip on the phosphate backbone and opens the fibre — so HATs activate, HDACs silence, and HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat, romidepsin) re-activate. DNA methylation at CpG recruits methyl-CpG-binding proteins and HDACs and closes it — hence imprinting, X-inactivation, and silenced tumour-suppressor promoters. The alliterative English pair is the standard one and it is worth having, but it carries a genuine trap: the "methylation" half is true of DNA methylation only. Histone methylation is ambiguous — H3K4me3 marks active promoters, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 mark silent heterochromatin — so the moment a stem says "histone methylation", drop the hook and read which residue is named. Acetylation is the only mark on this page with an unambiguous direction.

Two easy slips

H1 is not in the octamer. The core is (H2A·H2B·H3·H4)₂ — eight proteins, four kinds. H1 sits outside, on the linker. And 147 bp is the core particle, not the repeat. The repeat including linker is about 200 bp; quoting 200 as the wrapped length, or 147 as the repeat, is a marked error. Also note that 5-methylcytosine deaminates to thymine — a normal DNA base — which makes methylated CpG sites the single biggest mutational hotspot in the human genome, and explains why CpG is under-represented everywhere except in CpG islands.

§ 06

RNA, and the price of the 2′-OH

The ideaRNA is the same kind of chain with one extra oxygen atom on every sugar, and one of the four letters swapped for a near-identical cousin. That sounds like a detail and is not. The extra oxygen makes the chain chemically restless, so RNA falls apart in hours where DNA lasts for years — but the same restlessness is what lets RNA fold into working shapes and even behave like an enzyme. It reads as a deliberate trade: keep the permanent archive in the stable material, and make the disposable working copies out of the reactive one.

Difference 1: the 2′-OH — catalysis, instability, and A-form

The 2′-hydroxyl sits one bond away from the phosphodiester linkage of its own backbone. Raise the pH and it deprotonates to a 2′-alkoxide, which is perfectly placed to make an in-line nucleophilic attack on the adjacent phosphorus. The result is a 2′,3′-cyclic phosphate and a cut chain. RNA is destroyed by dilute alkali; DNA, having a hydrogen at 2′, is not. That single test — treat with NaOH and see what survives — distinguishes them, and it is also why alkaline hydrolysis is used to remove RNA from DNA preparations.

The same reactive geometry is what lets RNA be a catalyst: ribozymes such as the hammerhead and hairpin use precisely this in-line attack, as does the branch-point adenosine in splicing (RNase P is the counter-example: it hydrolyses with a metal-activated water instead), and RNase A accelerates it about 10¹² fold. So the 2′-OH buys catalysis and pays with a half-life measured in minutes to hours. For a molecule whose job is to be a disposable working copy, that is the correct trade. For the archive, it is not — which is exactly why the archive is deoxy.

And, from § 04: the 2′-OH forbids the C2′-endo sugar pucker, so double-helical RNA cannot adopt B-form. Every RNA duplex and every RNA–DNA hybrid is A-form.

Difference 2: U instead of T — an error-detection argument

Thymine is simply 5-methyluracil. Making it costs a methylation step (thymidylate synthase, the target of 5-fluorouracil, using a methyl group from methylene- — which is why methotrexate starves the same reaction). Why would DNA pay for a methyl group that contributes nothing to base pairing?

Because cytosine deaminates spontaneously, and the product of cytosine deamination is uracil. If uracil were a legitimate DNA base, a repair enzyme finding a U·G mismatch would have no way to tell which base was the intruder — it could equally be a correct U that had lost its partner. By excluding U from DNA entirely, the cell makes the rule trivial: any uracil in DNA is damage, by definition, and uracil-DNA glycosylase excises it without ambiguity. The methyl group is not chemistry; it is a checksum. RNA, being disposable and not copied forward, does not need one.

§ 07

The ideaCopying works by pulling the two chains apart and building a fresh partner against each one, so every copy ends up half old and half new. The awkward part is that the builder can only work in one direction, while the two chains it is copying point opposite ways. Fidelity, in three layers

Base pairing alone is worth about one error in 10⁴–10⁵ — hydrogen-bond geometry plus the polymerase's own induced-fit selection. Proofreading by the 3′→5′ exonuclease catches most of the rest and buys a further hundred-fold, to about 10⁻⁷. Post-replicative mismatch repair (§ 10) adds another hundred- to thousand-fold, landing at roughly one uncorrected error per 10⁹–10¹⁰ base pairs replicated. Multiply by 6.4 × 10⁹ bp per diploid cell division and you get a handful of new mutations per division — small enough to be survivable, large enough to be the raw material of evolution and of cancer.

The end-replication problem

The lagging strand's final Okazaki fragment needs a primer, and that primer sits at the extreme 5′ end of the new strand. When it is removed there is no upstream 3′-OH from which to fill the gap, so every round of replication shortens the chromosome by 50–200 bp. Somatic cells therefore have a replicative limit (the Hayflick limit) and telomeres act as an expendable buffer of TTAGGG repeats. Telomerase carries its own RNA template and extends the 3′ overhang, allowing the lagging strand to be completed; it is active in germline, stem and most cancer cells (≈90 % of tumours) and largely silent in somatic tissue. Loss-of-function mutations cause dyskeratosis congenita and some aplastic anaemias and pulmonary fibroses — tissues with high turnover fail first, which is the prediction the model makes.

Worked example 4 — how long does a bacterium take to copy itself?

The E. coli chromosome is 4.6 × 10⁶ bp, circular, with a single origin that fires bidirectionally. Each fork moves at about 1000 bp·s⁻¹. How long does one round of replication take, and how does that square with a 20-minute doubling time?

  1. Two forks leave oriC in opposite directions, so each has to copy half the genome: 4.6 × 10⁶ / 2 = 2.3 × 10⁶ bp.
  2. t = 2.3 × 10⁶ / 1000 = 2300 s ≈ 38 min.
  3. That is longer than the 20-minute doubling time — the apparent paradox. The resolution is that E. coli starts a new round of replication before the previous one has finished, so a rapidly growing cell contains multiple nested forks and more than one genome-equivalent of DNA.
  4. For contrast, a human diploid genome is 6.4 × 10⁹ bp and forks move at only ≈50 bp·s⁻¹. A single bidirectional origin would need 6.4 × 10⁹ / (2 × 50) = 6.4 × 10⁷ s ≈ 2 years. Eukaryotes therefore fire tens of thousands of origins, which is precisely why they need them.

The ideaTo use a gene, the cell does not read the archive directly — it makes a short working copy of just that stretch. The single most expensive trap on this page: template versus coding

Three rules, in this order, and you will not lose the mark. (1) Check the primes on the sequence you were given before doing anything else. (2) If you are given the coding strand 5′→3′, the mRNA is that string with U for T — no reversing, no complementing. (3) If you are given the template strand, complement it; and if that template was written 5′→3′, you must also reverse it, because the mRNA is antiparallel to the template. Skipping the reversal in rule 3 is the classic error: it silently produces a plausible-looking peptide from the wrong reading, usually with a premature stop, and the answer looks confident and is completely wrong.

Machine, message, then the parts: the three words are also the order in which protein synthesis needs the three products, which is why the numbering is not arbitrary and why the hook is hard to invert. The α-amanitin ladder then runs the same way round: II is the sensitive one, III needs a high dose, I is untouched — which is why a death-cap mushroom kills by shutting down mRNA synthesis in the liver. Mine rather than a textbook hook, although the argument underneath it — the polymerases are numbered in the order protein synthesis uses their products — is the standard one. It leaves out the two items in the table that are favourite distractors precisely because they break the pattern: 5S rRNA is made by Pol III, not Pol I, and U6 snRNA is Pol III while the other snRNAs are Pol II. It also says nothing about bacteria, which have exactly one RNA polymerase (core α₂ββ′ω + σ) — and rifampicin, conveniently, starts with the same letter as the enzyme it blocks.

Processing: three modifications, all eukaryote-only

  1. 5′ cap. A 7-methylguanosine joined to the first transcribed nucleotide by an unusual 5′→5′ triphosphate linkage — one of the very few linkages in nucleic acids that is not 3′→5′; the other you meet on this page is the 2′→5′ bond at a splicing lariat's branch point. Added co-transcriptionally after ≈25 nucleotides. It protects against 5′ exonucleases, is required for nuclear export, and is the binding site for eIF4E during translation initiation.
  2. 3′ poly(A) tail. The polymerase transcribes past the gene; cleavage occurs 10–30 nucleotides after the signal AAUAAA, and poly(A) polymerase adds ≈200 adenosines without a template. The tail sets mRNA stability — its progressive shortening is the clock that eventually triggers degradation. Histone mRNAs are the exception: no poly(A) tail, a stem-loop instead.
  3. Splicing. Introns are removed by the spliceosome (U1, U2, U4, U5, U6 snRNPs). U1 binds the 5′ GU donor site, U2 binds the branch-point adenosine; the branch A's 2′-OH attacks the 5′ splice site to form a lariat, then the freed exon 3′-OH attacks the AG acceptor and the exons are joined. Two transesterifications, no ATP consumed in the chemistry itself. Remember the GU–AG rule and note that the branch-point attack is the same 2′-OH chemistry from § 06.

The splicing numbers do not need a hook either — they are already in order. GU … A … AG is written in the direction the transcript is read, 5′→3′: donor, branch point, acceptor. The snRNP numbering runs the same way: U1 binds the first site (the 5′ GU donor) and U2 binds the second (the branch-point A). Two numbers in their natural order is not a memorisation problem — but the chemistry is worth one sentence, because it is what gets asked: the branch A's 2′-OH attacks first and makes the lariat, then the freed exon attacks the AG. Attack order, not site order.

Alternative splicing, and where it goes wrong

Roughly 95 % of human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced, which is how ≈20 000 protein-coding genes support a far larger proteome. It is also a large mutational target, because a splice site is only a few nucleotides long and every one of them matters:

  • β-thalassaemia is the classic teaching case. HBB IVS-I-1 G>A destroys the invariant GU donor, abolishing normal splicing (β⁰). IVS-I-110 G>A does something subtler — it creates a new 3′ acceptor site inside intron 1, so most transcripts retain 19 extra nucleotides and are frameshifted, while a minority splice correctly; the result is a β⁺ phenotype with reduced but not absent β-globin. The severity follows directly from how completely the site is destroyed.
  • Spinal muscular atrophy depends on splicing of SMN2 exon 7; nusinersen is an antisense oligonucleotide that redirects that splicing. This is the therapeutic proof that splice choice is druggable.

Why bacteria can couple transcription and translation and we cannot

A bacterium has no nuclear envelope, no introns in its protein-coding genes and no capping or polyadenylation, so ribosomes load onto the 5′ end of a message that is still being transcribed — you can see polysomes trailing an RNA polymerase in electron micrographs. A eukaryote physically cannot: transcription and processing happen in the nucleus, translation in the cytoplasm, and the nuclear envelope enforces the separation. That compartmentalisation is what makes splicing possible in the first place (an unspliced transcript would otherwise be translated), and it gives eukaryotes an entire extra regulatory layer — export control, nonsense-mediated decay, cytoplasmic storage — that bacteria do not have.

§ 09

The genetic code and translation

The ideaThe message is read three letters at a time, and each group of three names one building block of a protein. There are far more possible groups than there are blocks to name, so most blocks answer to several names — and that spare capacity works as a safety margin, because it means a great many single-letter typos change nothing at all. Where the reading starts is decided once, at the beginning, and never checked again, so anything that shifts the starting point along by one or two letters turns everything after it into nonsense.

Five properties, and each one is a claim you should be able to defend. The three stop codons

Why synonymous codons cluster: wobble

Look at what the colouring shows. In 8 of the 16 blocks — the boxes with a fixed first and second base — all four codons encode the same amino acid: UC* (Ser), CU* (Leu), CC* (Pro), CG* (Arg), AC* (Thr), GU* (Val), GC* (Ala) and GG* (Gly). That is 32 of the 64 codons in which the third base carries no information at all. In most of the remaining blocks the split is by chemical class of the third base: pyrimidine (U or C) means one amino acid, purine (A or G) means another. Only two blocks break that two-and-two split, and both are worth knowing by name: AU* is Ile/Ile/Ile/Met (three and one), and UG* is Cys/Cys/Stop/Trp (two, then one each). Keep this question separate from the colour trap noted on the table: how many amino acids a block encodes and how many colours it shows are different things, because two different amino acids in the same block can share a side-chain class and therefore a colour.

The mechanism is Crick's wobble hypothesis. The tRNA anticodon pairs antiparallel with the codon, so the first anticodon base pairs with the third codon base, and it does so at the loose end of a short helix where the geometric constraints are weakest. Do not memorise which position wobbles, and do not memorise anticodons at all. Both fall out of one fact you already have from § 03: the anticodon pairs antiparallel with the codon. So the anticodon's 5′ end lies opposite the codon's 3′ end — the first anticodon base therefore meets the third codon base, at the loose end of a three-base helix where non-standard pairs are tolerated. The same sentence gives you the anticodon itself: it is the reverse complement, so codon 5′-AUG-3′ is read by anticodon 5′-CAU-3′, not 5′-UAC-3′. One principle, two facts, and 5′-UAC-3′ is waiting in the option list for anyone who reversed nothing.

Exam technique · translation questions, and the eliminations that are free

  1. Count before you translate — peptide length is the number of codons before the stop. Divide the letters by three, drop one if the last triplet is a stop, and every option with the wrong residue count dies untouched. Two of this note's own quiz questions are decided by exactly that move, because both offer a correct reading that counts the stop as a residue.
  2. Third base changed → reach for "silent" first — 8 of the 16 blocks are four-fold degenerate and ≈69 % of third-position substitutions are synonymous, so "silent" is the base-rate answer. Then check the three places it fails: AUG (Met) and UGG (Trp) are single-codon amino acids, and the UG box holds a stop between Cys and Trp.
  3. Second base changed → expect non-conservative missense — the second position is the least tolerant, and a change there usually crosses side-chain classes. The colour key on the table above is the fastest conservative-or-not judgement available to you; use the colours, not your memory of the amino acid.
  4. Ribosome options die on arithmetic — prokaryotic subunits are the odd numbers 30S, 50S, 70S; eukaryotic are the even 40S, 60S, 80S. Any option pairing 50S with 80S, or adding 50 + 30 to get 80, is wrong before you read the second half of it, because S is a sedimentation coefficient and does not add. That single check clears a whole option shape in ten seconds.
  5. Familiar-sounding is not the same as answering the stem — "peptidyl transferase is an enzyme of the large subunit" reads exactly like a sentence from a textbook and is wrong here: the activity is 23S rRNA, and the ribosome is a ribozyme. Distractors on this topic are often lifted verbatim from prose, so test each option against the question actually asked rather than against your sense of having read it somewhere.

Mutation and repair

The ideaDamage to the message is constant and unavoidable, so the interesting question is never whether errors happen but which ones survive. Against this the cell keeps several repair crews, each trained on a different kind of damage — and the inherited illnesses in this section are, one for one, the story of what goes wrong when a particular crew is missing.

Classifying a point mutation

  • Silent (synonymous). Codon changes, amino acid does not — almost always a third-position change, because of wobble. Not always harmless: silent changes can create or destroy splice sites or alter translation speed and hence folding.
  • Missense. A different amino acid. Conservative if the replacement is in the same side-chain class (Asp→Glu, both acidic) and usually tolerated; non-conservative if it crosses classes and much more likely to be pathogenic. The colouring in the § 09 table lets you judge this at a glance.
  • Nonsense. A sense codon becomes a stop. The product is truncated, and in eukaryotes the transcript is usually destroyed by nonsense-mediated decay before it is even translated — so the phenotype is typically loss of function rather than a toxic short protein.
  • Frameshift. Every codon downstream is re-read, so the protein sequence is unrelated beyond the insertion point, and a stop codon appears at random within roughly 20 codons on average (there are 3 stops among 64 triplets). A frameshift is therefore usually worse than a nonsense mutation at the same position: nonsense truncates but leaves the earlier residues correct; frameshift garbles them and truncates as well.

  1. UUG → UUA (codon 4, third base G→A). Both encode Leu. This is a silent mutation, and a transition (purine→purine). Exactly the outcome wobble predicts for a third-position change.
  2. CCU → CGU (codon 2, second base C→G). Pro → Arg. Missense, and non-conservative: nonpolar to basic. A second-position change almost always changes the amino acid, and usually changes its class — that is why the second position is the least tolerant. C→G is a transversion.

Which lesion? One wrong but normal base, no distortion → BER. A bulky distortion → NER. A correctly shaped but mispaired base left behind by the polymerase → MMR. It covers the four commonest stems and deliberately omits the two pathways with no single organ: BER (MUTYH-associated polyposis; its exam handle is really the PARP-inhibitor synthetic lethality) and direct reversal (MGMT silencing predicting temozolomide response). And it is the second tool, not the first: when a stem describes the lesion rather than a patient, use the lesion rule in the box above instead — it is faster and it never has exceptions.

Two things that sound right and are not

"There are twice as many transversions possible, so they are more common." The premise is true — each base has two possible transversions but only one transition — and the conclusion is still false. Transitions dominate in real genomes, driven largely by deamination of 5-methylcytosine at CpG sites into thymine, which is invisible to uracil glycosylase because thymine is a legitimate DNA base. "A silent mutation is harmless." Often, but not reliably: synonymous changes can destroy an exonic splicing enhancer, create a cryptic splice site, or swap a common codon for a rare one and slow the ribosome enough to change co-translational folding.

Exam technique · classifying a change in three questions

  1. Is it an indel? Divide by three — remainder 0 means residues are added or lost and the frame survives; any other remainder is a frameshift, and a frameshift is the most damaging option available at that position. Settle this before you look up a single codon, because it decides the answer on its own in most stems.
  2. If it is a substitution, three lookups and no thinking — same amino acid → silent; different amino acid → missense, then judge conservative or not from the side-chain colour in § 09; a stop → nonsense. Do not reason about the chemistry until the table has told you which of the three it is.
  3. Transition or transversion, at a glance — A and G are the two-ring purines, C, T and U the one-ring pyrimidines. Stay inside a class → transition (only A↔G and C↔T qualify); cross between classes → transversion. Two letters to check, never more.
  4. Disease named → organ rule. Lesion named → lesion rule — sun → NER, bowel with MSI → MMR, breast/ovary with PARP sensitivity → HR, radiosensitive immunodeficiency → NHEJ. If instead the stem describes the damage: bulky distortion → NER, correctly shaped mispair left by the polymerase → MMR, one wrong-but-normal base → BER, broken backbone → HR (accurate, needs a sister chromatid, S/G2) or NHEJ (fast, sloppy, any time).
  5. Two option shapes that are nearly always the distractor — "transversions are commoner, because twice as many are possible" (true premise, false conclusion: 5-methyl-C → T makes transitions dominate) and "a silent mutation is by definition harmless" (it can destroy a splice enhancer, create a cryptic site, or swap in a rare codon). Both read like textbook sentences, which is exactly why they are there. Recognising either one is a free elimination and, under negative marking, a free reason to answer.

Reading nucleic acids in the laboratory

The ideaNothing in this section is new chemistry. Every method here is one of the facts you have already met, turned into a tool. Because the two chains come apart when heated and find each other again when cooled, you can make a chosen stretch copy itself over and over until there is enough of it to see. Because a chain can only grow from one particular end, blocking that end at random points tells you the order of the letters. Read each method as an application rather than a recipe and there is very little left to memorise.

PCR

Everything about the method follows from the fact that the reaction is heated to ≈95 °C once per cycle. A mesophilic polymerase would be denatured and would have to be replaced by hand every cycle, which is literally how PCR was done before 1988. Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, survives it — which is what made automation, and therefore the whole technique, practical. Taq has no 3′→5′ proofreading exonuclease, so its error rate is around 1 in 10⁴; for cloning or sequencing you use a proofreading enzyme such as Pfu (Pyrococcus furiosus) instead.

The two primers define the product because they flank the target and point towards each other; each one is extended 5′→3′ across the region between them. Note what this implies: the primer that anneals to the template's top strand must have the sequence of the bottom strand, and vice versa. Getting a primer's orientation backwards is the commonest design error, and it produces no product at all.

  • RT-PCR starts from RNA: reverse transcriptase makes cDNA first. Because cDNA is made from mature mRNA it contains no introns, which is both how you detect expression and how you distinguish an amplified transcript from contaminating genomic DNA (design primers spanning an exon–exon junction).
  • qPCR (real-time PCR) measures product as it accumulates, with SYBR Green (binds any dsDNA — cheap, but reports primer-dimers too) or a TaqMan hydrolysis probe (sequence-specific). The readout is Ct, and because amplification is exponential, Ct is logarithmically related to starting quantity: a lower Ct means more template to begin with.
Worked example 7 — PCR yield, and the qPCR conversion

(a) Starting from a single template molecule, how many copies exist after 30 cycles at perfect efficiency, and how many at 90 % efficiency? (b) In a qPCR experiment a treated sample has Ct = 22.4 and the control Ct = 25.7. By what factor does the transcript differ?

  1. (a) N = 1 × 2³⁰ = 1.07 × 10⁹ copies. Each cycle doubles; 30 doublings is a billion-fold.
  2. At E = 0.9: N = 1.9³⁰ = 2.3 × 10⁸. A 10 % shortfall per cycle costs a factor of about 4.7 over 30 cycles — which is exactly why efficiency has to be validated before any quantitative comparison.
  3. In practice yield plateaus long before this (reagents deplete, product re-anneals faster than primers can bind), so the exponential formula is only valid in the early cycles — which is precisely the region qPCR measures.
  4. (b) ΔCt = 25.7 − 22.4 = 3.3 cycles.
  5. Each cycle is a doubling, so the factor is 2³·³ = 9.8 ≈ 10-fold. The treated sample reaches threshold earlier, so it started with more transcript: ≈10× upregulated. Remember the direction — a lower Ct means more starting material.
(a) 1.07 × 10⁹ ideal · 2.3 × 10⁸ at E = 0.9 · (b) ≈10-fold higher

Exam technique · PCR and qPCR arithmetic in twenty seconds

  1. Only three powers of two ever appear — 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³, 2²⁰ ≈ 10⁶, 2³⁰ ≈ 10⁹. Read the cycle number, land on the nearest of the three, adjust by doublings. Thirty cycles from one molecule is a billion copies, and you never need a calculator for it.
  2. Fix the Ct direction before the magnitude — a lower Ct means more starting template, because an abundant target crosses the threshold sooner. Deciding the direction first usually halves the option list, and it costs nothing.
  3. ΔCt → fold change — the factor is 2ΔCt. There is no mnemonic to learn and you do not want one: 1 cycle is 2×, 3.3 cycles is 10× because log₂10 = 3.32, and 6.6 cycles is 100×. Everything else is one doubling at a time from the nearest of those.
  4. Cheap sanity check on the cycling conditions — denaturation 94–98 °C, annealing ≈5 °C below the primer Tm from § 03, extension 72 °C because that is where Taq works. An option with an annealing temperature above the extension temperature, or a denaturation step below 90 °C, is wrong on its face without any thought about the biology.
  5. Recognition rules for the failure stems — "no product at all" points at primer orientation (the two primers must point towards each other) or a primer that cannot anneal. "Product of the wrong size" points at mispriming or genomic DNA when cDNA was intended, which is why primers are designed across an exon–exon junction. "Signal with no template" points at primer-dimers — exactly what SYBR Green cannot distinguish and a TaqMan probe can.

Sequencing

Sanger works because a ddNTP has no 3′-OH: once one is incorporated, § 02's mechanism has nowhere to attack and that chain stops. Run the reaction with a small proportion of fluorescently labelled ddNTPs and you get a population of fragments terminating at every position, one colour per base; separate them by capillary electrophoresis, smallest first, and read the colours in order. Read length is roughly 800–1000 bp, accuracy is excellent, and it remains the reference method for confirming a single variant.

Next-generation sequencing trades read length for parallelism: millions of short reads (100–300 bp) generated simultaneously by sequencing-by-synthesis on a flow cell, then aligned to a reference. The consequences are practical — high depth makes it good at detecting low-frequency variants (tumour subclones, cell-free fetal DNA), while short reads make it poor at repeats, large structural variants and phasing. Long-read platforms (nanopore, SMRT) invert that trade-off.

Electrophoresis and blotting

From § 02: DNA carries one negative charge per nucleotide at any usable pH, so charge is proportional to length, the charge-to-mass ratio is constant, and DNA always runs towards the anode ("run to red"). Separation therefore depends only on how the gel matrix retards molecules by size — small fragments migrate fastest. Agarose resolves roughly 0.1–25 kb; polyacrylamide resolves single-nucleotide differences and is what sequencing gels used. Supercoiled plasmid runs faster than the same plasmid nicked open or linearised, because it is more compact — a common source of confusion when a plasmid prep shows three bands.

Restriction enzymes and palindromes

Restriction endonucleases are a bacterial immune system: they destroy incoming phage DNA, while the host genome is protected by a methylase that marks the same sites. Their recognition sequences are usually palindromic in the double-stranded sense — the sequence read 5′→3′ on one strand equals the sequence read 5′→3′ on the other, which is not the same as reading the same backwards. EcoRI recognises 5′-GAATTC-3′; its complement read 5′→3′ is also GAATTC, so the site is a true palindrome. It cuts between G and A on both strands, leaving 4-base 5′ overhangs — sticky ends that can re-anneal with any fragment cut by the same enzyme, which is the entire basis of cloning. SmaI cuts CCC^GGG down the middle and leaves blunt ends: ligatable to anything, but with no specificity and much lower efficiency.

CRISPR-Cas9 in one paragraph

Another bacterial defence system, repurposed. A ≈20-nucleotide guide RNA base-pairs with a matching genomic sequence, and Cas9 cuts both strands about 3 bp upstream of a PAM (5′-NGG-3′ for Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9) — the PAM is what stops the enzyme from cutting the bacterium's own CRISPR array, and it is the constraint that determines where you are allowed to edit. What happens next is the cell's choice, from § 10: NHEJ repairs the break sloppily, introducing small indels that frameshift the gene — the standard way to make a knockout — while supplying a homologous donor template lets HR install a defined edit, but only in cycling cells in S/G2. Base editors and prime editors avoid the double-strand break entirely, which reduces indel by-products. The first approved CRISPR therapy, exagamglogene autotemcel for sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia, works by knocking out a BCL11A enhancer to re-express fetal haemoglobin — a neat illustration that editing a regulatory element can be easier than fixing the mutated gene itself.
