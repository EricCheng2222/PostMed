Biochemistry · Note 04

Carbohydrate Metabolism: Glycolysis, Gluconeogenesis, Glycogen and the PPP

One principle organises the whole of this note: a pathway is controlled where it is irreversible. Ten glycolytic reactions run, seven of them so close to equilibrium that they slide either way depending on which side is being drained. Three do not. Those three are the only steps a cell can usefully regulate, they are exactly the steps that carry hormones and allosteric signals, and they are exactly the steps that a reverse pathway must replace with different enzymes. Once you see that, gluconeogenesis stops being a list of four odd reactions and becomes an inevitability; reciprocal control through fructose 2,6-bisphosphate stops being a fact and becomes an engineering solution; and glycogen synthesis and breakdown stop being mirror images and become two separately switchable machines. The second thread running through the note is carbon bookkeeping — knowing which atom of glucose ends up where, because the paper asks it directly.

§ 01

Getting glucose in, and the crossroads at G6P

The ideaSugar cannot simply be left lying around inside a cell, because the same doorway it came in through would let it straight back out. So the first thing every cell does to glucose is stick a phosphate on it. That one move does three jobs at once: it puts a charge on the molecule so it can no longer cross the membrane, it keeps the concentration inside the cell low so more sugar keeps flowing in, and it produces the single compound from which four different pathways depart. Which of those four a cell chooses is what the rest of this page is about. And the enzyme that adds the phosphate is not the same enzyme everywhere — the liver uses a deliberately clumsy version, and the reason it is clumsy is the whole point.

Definition & key facts

Metabolic trapping
Phosphorylation converts a neutral, transportable sugar into a charged phosphate ester that no facilitative transporter will carry. The cell keeps what it has taken up, and free intracellular glucose stays low, so the inward concentration gradient never collapses.

The transporters, and why their Km values are the interesting part

All GLUTs move glucose downhill. What distinguishes them is affinity, and affinity is how the body decides who gets served first when supply is short. A transporter with a Km well below the blood concentration is already saturated and delivers a constant flow whatever happens; one with a Km above it tracks the blood level in proportion. Neurons take the first arrangement, the liver takes the second, and that is exactly the physiology you would design.

Insulin-independent tissues

Only muscle and adipose tissue need insulin for glucose uptake, because only they rely on GLUT4. Brain, liver, erythrocyte, kidney, intestine, cornea and the pancreatic β-cell take glucose up regardless. Two clinical consequences follow immediately: in untreated diabetes those insulin-independent tissues are flooded with glucose (which is where the polyol-pathway damage of § 06 comes from), and exercise lowers blood glucose even in a patient with almost no circulating insulin, because contraction recruits GLUT4 by a separate route.

Two enzymes, one reaction, opposite jobs

Hexokinases I–III are found in essentially every cell. Their Km for glucose is about 0.1 mM, roughly fiftyfold below resting blood glucose, so they run flat out all the time and they are switched off by their own product, glucose 6-phosphate. That is precisely what a cell wants: take what you need, stop when you have enough, and do not compete with your neighbours.

Glucokinase — hexokinase IV — sits in the liver parenchyma and the pancreatic β-cell and is built to do the opposite. Its half-saturating concentration is about 10 mM, it is not inhibited by glucose 6-phosphate, and its velocity curve is sigmoidal (Hill coefficient ≈ 1.7) even though the enzyme is a monomer with a single active site — the cooperativity is kinetic, arising from a slow conformational change rather than from subunit interactions. The result is an enzyme whose rate is nearly proportional to how much glucose has just arrived. The liver therefore stores the surplus after a meal and leaves the rest of the body alone between meals, which is exactly the behaviour a buffer organ requires.

Worked example 1 — why a Km of 10 mM is a design feature

Fasting portal glucose is 4.5 mM; after a carbohydrate meal it reaches 12 mM. Compare the fractional saturation of an erythrocyte hexokinase (Km = 0.1 mM, hyperbolic) with hepatic glucokinase ([S]0.5 = 10 mM, h = 1.7).

  1. Hexokinase at 4.5 mM: v/Vmax = 4.5/(0.1 + 4.5) = 0.978.
  2. Hexokinase at 12 mM: 12/(0.1 + 12) = 0.992. A 2.7-fold rise in substrate changes the rate by 1.4 %.
  3. Glucokinase at 4.5 mM: 4.51.7 = 12.9 and 101.7 = 50.1, so v/Vmax = 12.9/(50.1 + 12.9) = 0.205.
  4. Glucokinase at 12 mM: 121.7 = 68.3, so v/Vmax = 68.3/(50.1 + 68.3) = 0.577.
  5. Ratio: the hepatic enzyme speeds up 0.577/0.205 = 2.8× over exactly the same excursion that leaves the red cell's enzyme unmoved.

The lesson generalises: an enzyme with a Km far below its substrate concentration is a constant supplier; an enzyme with a Km near or above it is a sensor. Any exam question that gives you a Km and a physiological concentration is asking you to make that judgement.

Exam technique · a Km and a concentration in the same stem

  1. Recognise the question type in five seconds — if a stem quotes a Km (or [S]0.5) and a physiological concentration, it is almost never asking you to compute. It is asking supplier or sensor? Km far below [S] → the enzyme is already saturated and its rate barely moves. Km near or above [S] → its rate tracks [S].
  2. Kill the reversed pair before anything else — this material is nearly always distractored by writing "glucokinase has the lower Km" and "glucokinase is the high-affinity enzyme" as two separate options. They are one error in two wordings, so both die together and one recognition removes two of five.
  3. Only then compute, and only if the options are numeric — v/Vmax = [S]/(Km + [S]) for a hyperbolic enzyme. Use the Hill form only when the stem hands you h; if no h is given, the examiner does not want it.
  4. Sanity check in two seconds — every fractional saturation must lie strictly between 0 and 1, and a higher [S] must give a higher fraction. A value above 1, or one that falls as [S] rises, means you inverted the fraction. A quick reference point: at [S] = Km the answer is exactly 0.5.
  5. Where the time goes — 15 seconds read as supplier-versus-sensor, 90 seconds started from the algebra. Read first, always; then compute only to separate two surviving options.

The β-cell version explains insulin secretion in a single chain. Glucose enters (the transport step is fast and not rate-limiting), glucokinase phosphorylates it at a rate proportional to blood glucose, glycolysis and oxidation raise the ATP/ADP ratio, the ATP-sensitive K⁺ channel closes, the membrane depolarises, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open, and insulin granules fuse. Glucokinase is therefore the glucose sensor of the β-cell — which is why a mutation that shifts its [S]0.5 shifts the entire set point at which insulin is released, and why sulfonylureas, which close the same K⁺ channel directly, bypass the sensor altogether.

The four exits from glucose 6-phosphate

G6P is the busiest junction in carbohydrate metabolism, and almost every question in this note is really a question about which exit is open. (1) Glycolysis, via phosphohexose isomerase, for ATP. (2) Glycogen, via phosphoglucomutase, for storage. (3) The pentose phosphate pathway, via glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase, for NADPH and ribose. (4) Free glucose, via glucose 6-phosphatase — but only in liver, kidney cortex and intestinal epithelium. Note the consequence of exit 4 being missing in muscle: muscle glycogen can never raise blood glucose, no matter how much of it there is. Note also that because G6P has four fates, the hexokinase reaction is not the committed step of glycolysis.

§ 02

Glycolysis, step by step

The ideaGlycolysis is a business that spends before it earns. The first half of the pathway uses up two units of the cell's energy currency in order to put a handle on each end of the sugar; only then is the six-carbon molecule cut cleanly in half, and each half is marched through the second stage, which pays back twice what was spent. Because the cut is in the middle and both halves are made identical, everything after the split happens twice — which is the single commonest source of arithmetic mistakes on this topic. It is also worth noticing what glycolysis does not need: no oxygen, no mitochondrion, no membrane. It is the oldest pathway we have, and every cell in the body still runs it.

Definition & key facts

Glycolysis
The cytosolic conversion of one glucose to two pyruvate in ten enzymatic steps, conserving part of the released free energy as two ATP and two NADH. It ends at pyruvate: the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is not part of glycolysis, and neither is lactate formation.
glucose + 2 NAD⁺ + 2 ADP + 2 Pi → 2 pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 2 ATP + 2 H₂O the whole pathway on one line; everything downstream of the aldolase step happens twice

What each phase is really for

Step 2 looks like a pointless shuffle — an aldose to a ketose, ΔG°′ of just +1.7 kJ·mol⁻¹. It is not. Glucose 6-phosphate has its carbonyl at C1; cleaving it in half would give two different three-carbon fragments, and the cell would need two separate sets of enzymes downstream. Isomerising to fructose 6-phosphate moves the carbonyl to C2, and now a cut between C3 and C4 yields two fragments that can be made identical. The isomerase also frees the C1 hydroxyl so that PFK-1 has somewhere to put the second phosphate. One small reaction, two structural preconditions for everything that follows.

Step 4, the aldolase reaction, has a standard free-energy change of +23.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ — strongly endergonic. It still runs, briskly, in the forward direction, because ΔG°′ describes a hypothetical state with every species at 1 mol·L⁻¹ and the cell is nowhere near it: the triose phosphates are consumed as fast as they appear, so the mass-action ratio holds the actual ΔG close to zero. This is the cleanest illustration in all of metabolism that ΔG°′ tells you nothing about direction in a cell; only ΔG does. Examiners like it precisely because students confuse the two.

The GAPDH step, which is the whole trick

Making ATP out of a sugar requires getting a phosphate onto something whose phosphoryl-transfer potential exceeds ATP's. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase does it by coupling an oxidation to a phosphorylation in one active site. The aldehyde attacks an active-site cysteine thiol to form a thiohemiacetal; NAD⁺ oxidises that to a thioester, which is a high-energy linkage; inorganic phosphate then attacks the thioester and the acyl group is displaced as an acyl phosphate, 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, ΔG°′ of hydrolysis −49.3 kJ·mol⁻¹. The energy of the oxidation has been trapped in a chemical bond instead of being lost as heat. Phosphoglycerate kinase then hands that phosphate to ADP.

Two classical inhibitors fall straight out of the mechanism. Iodoacetate alkylates the active-site cysteine and stops the enzyme dead. Arsenate is more interesting: it substitutes for phosphate, giving 1-arseno-3-phosphoglycerate, which hydrolyses spontaneously before any kinase can reach it. Glycolysis continues, NADH is still made — but the ATP is silently lost. Arsenate uncouples substrate-level phosphorylation, and the distinction between an inhibitor and an uncoupler is a favourite single-best-answer discrimination.

Step 9 deserves the same attention. Enolase does not add energy to 2-phosphoglycerate; it removes a water molecule. The resulting enol phosphate has a hydrolysis ΔG°′ of −61.9 kJ·mol⁻¹, twice that of ATP, not because the phosphate bond is unusual but because the product of hydrolysis — enolpyruvate — tautomerises immediately and irreversibly to the far more stable keto form. The driving force lives in the tautomerisation, downstream of the bond being broken. That is also why the pyruvate kinase reaction is irreversible: you cannot run a tautomerisation backwards by handing the phosphate back.

Three inhibitors that are constantly confused

Arsenate (AsO43−) mimics phosphate and uncouples the GAPDH step — glycolysis runs, no ATP. Arsenite (AsO33−) is a different ion: it chelates the vicinal thiols of lipoamide and therefore poisons pyruvate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase — a mitochondrial, not a glycolytic, lesion. Fluoride inhibits enolase, which is why the grey-topped blood tube used for glucose measurement contains sodium fluoride: it stops the red cells in the sample eating the analyte. One letter, one anion and one tube-top separate three different answers.

Three poisons, three different lesions

Arsenate apes phosphate · arsenite bites lipoamide · fluoride freezes enolase

Three clauses, and each one carries its own spelling check. Arsenate apes phosphate — the shared -ate ending is the hook: arsenate takes the place of Pi at GAPDH, the acyl arsenate hydrolyses on its own before any kinase reaches it, so glycolysis runs and NADH is still made but the ATP silently disappears — an uncoupler, not an inhibitor. Arsenite bites lipoamide — the other ion, one letter apart, and it bites down on the vicinal thiols of lipoamide, so it takes out pyruvate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase: a mitochondrial lesion, not a glycolytic one. Fluoride freezes enolase — F for fluoride, F for freeze: it blocks enolase and so freezes glycolysis in the sample, which is exactly the job of the grey-topped sodium-fluoride tube. My own line, and it deliberately covers only three of the four agents in the box above. Iodoacetate is left out because it needs no hook: GAPDH works through an active-site cysteine thiol and iodoacetate is an alkylating agent, so the mechanism the note has just given you places it. The discrimination the paper is usually after is uncoupler versus inhibitor — arsenate against iodoacetate — so hold that pair as a pair.

Where the ATP happens

The two costs are both kinases acting on a hexose (hexokinase, phosphofructokinase). The two gains are both kinases named after their product (phosphoglycerate kinase, pyruvate kinase) — and because both sit after the split, each pays twice. Note the naming convention that trips people up: a "kinase" is named for the molecule that receives the phosphate, so pyruvate kinase in the direction written actually takes a phosphate off PEP.

§ 03

The three irreversible steps, and their control

The ideaIf a reaction runs easily in both directions, a cell cannot use it as a switch — push on one side and it simply slides back. Control has to be placed where a reaction runs strongly downhill, because only there does changing the enzyme change the flow through the whole line. Glycolysis has exactly three such places, and all three of them listen to the same kind of message: is this cell short of energy, or does it have plenty? A fourth, separate message reaches the liver only — the body is short of sugar, whatever your own cell happens to need — and that is why liver control looks different from muscle control.

Definition & key facts

Committed step（承諾步驟）
The first irreversible reaction after which the intermediate has no other fate. For glycolysis that is phosphofructokinase-1: fructose 1,6-bisphosphate can go nowhere but on down the pathway. Hexokinase is irreversible but not committed, because glucose 6-phosphate has four exits.
PFK-1: F6P + ATP → F1,6BP + ADP ⊕ AMP, ADP, F2,6BP ⊖ ATP, citrate, H⁺ the rate-limiting step of glycolysis and the single most examined regulatory point in metabolism

Why AMP is the alarm signal and ATP is not

It would seem natural for a cell to monitor ATP directly. It does not, and the reason is arithmetic. ATP is abundant — several millimolar — so even a serious energy crisis moves it by only a few per cent, and a few per cent is not a signal you can build a switch on. AMP is scarce, tens of micromolar, and it is coupled to the others by adenylate kinase, 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP. Convert a little ATP to ADP and the equilibrium dumps the surplus into the small AMP pool, where it registers as a large fractional change. This is signal amplification by choosing a low-abundance reporter, and it is exactly why AMP — not ATP — activates PFK-1 and why AMP-activated protein kinase, not "ATP-inhibited protein kinase", is the cell's master fuel sensor.

Worked example 2 — hydrolysing 10 % of the ATP is a 3.5-fold rise in AMP

Take a resting muscle cell with [ATP] = 5.00 mM and [ADP] = 0.50 mM, and let adenylate kinase be at equilibrium with K = [ATP][AMP]/[ADP]² = 0.44. Hydrolyse 10 % of the ATP to ADP, then let adenylate kinase re-equilibrate. What happens to AMP?

  1. Resting AMP: [AMP] = K[ADP]²/[ATP] = 0.44 × 0.25 / 5.00 = 0.0220 mM. So the pool is about 91 % ATP, 9 % ADP, 0.4 % AMP.
  2. Hydrolyse 0.50 mM of ATP: immediately [ATP] = 4.50, [ADP] = 1.00, [AMP] = 0.0220. The total pool (5.522 mM) is unchanged — adenylate kinase moves nucleotides between forms, it does not create them.
  3. Re-equilibrate. Let x mM of the reaction 2 ADP → ATP + AMP occur: solve (4.50 + x)(0.0220 + x)/(1.00 − 2x)² = 0.44, giving x = 0.0546.
  4. Final: [ATP] = 4.555, [ADP] = 0.891, [AMP] = 0.0766 mM.
  5. Compare with the start. ATP has fallen by 8.9 %. ADP has risen 1.8-fold. AMP has risen 3.5-fold.

Nothing about the chemistry changed; only the choice of reporter did. A sensor reading ATP would see a 9 % wobble. A sensor reading AMP sees the alarm go off. That is the design principle behind every AMP-responsive enzyme in metabolism.

The three control points, one at a time

Hexokinase is inhibited by its own product. If glucose 6-phosphate is piling up — because PFK-1 downstream has been shut, or because glycogen synthesis is saturated — there is no point in phosphorylating more glucose, so the enzyme stops. This is local housekeeping, not a policy decision. Glucokinase, having no G6P site, ignores the argument entirely: the liver keeps taking glucose up even when its own G6P is high, because its job is to clear the blood, not to serve itself.

PFK-1 is where the real decision is made. ATP is both a substrate and an inhibitor, binding at a second, low-affinity allosteric site that converts the F6P saturation curve from hyperbolic to sigmoidal — that is, high ATP does not lower Vmax, it raises the apparent Km for fructose 6-phosphate. AMP and ADP compete that inhibition away. Citrate reinforces ATP inhibition and is the message from the mitochondrion that carbon, not just energy, is already in surplus. H⁺ inhibits too, which is a protective circuit: in an anoxic muscle, falling pH throttles glycolysis before lactic acidosis becomes dangerous. And fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, the hormonal signal of § 08, overrides all of it in the liver.

Pyruvate kinase is activated by fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, the product of PFK-1 — a feed-forward loop that ensures the exit gate opens as soon as the entry gate does, so intermediates do not pile up in the middle of the pathway. It is inhibited by ATP, by acetyl-CoA and by alanine (which is one transamination away from pyruvate, and therefore a proxy for "there is plenty of pyruvate already"). In liver only, the L isoenzyme is phosphorylated and inactivated by PKA in response to glucagon; the M1 isoenzyme of muscle has no such site, because muscle has no business shutting down glycolysis when blood sugar is low.

Read the direction of the signal, not the name of the molecule

Almost every distractor on this topic is a real regulator pointed the wrong way. ATP inhibits PFK-1 (it is also the substrate — both facts are true and the question usually hinges on which one it is asking about). Citrate inhibits, it does not activate; it is the "carbon is abundant" signal. Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate activates PFK-1; fructose 1,6-bisphosphate activates pyruvate kinase — swap the numbers and both statements become false. AMP activates; ATP inhibits. And alanine inhibits pyruvate kinase, which surprises people until they notice that alanine is pyruvate wearing an amino group.

Exam technique · sign the regulator instead of memorising the list

There is no 口訣 for these lists and you do not need one. Almost every allosteric regulator in this section answers a single question — is this cell short of energy? — so you can give each molecule a sign on sight and reconstruct both tables. A mnemonic here would be a second thing to remember on top of a rule you already have.

  1. Sign the molecule — AMP and ADP mean "short of energy" → activate. ATP means "plenty" → inhibit. Citrate means "carbon is already in surplus downstream" → inhibit.   2. Swapping those two numbers is the commonest distractor on this topic, and it is the one place worth rote.
  3. Alanine and acetyl-CoA are pyruvate in disguise — alanine is one transamination from pyruvate, acetyl-CoA is one decarboxylation past it. Both therefore sign as "there is plenty of pyruvate already" and both inhibit pyruvate kinase. You do not have to remember them; you have to notice them.
  4. When two options about ATP are both true statements — ATP is a substrate of PFK-1 and its allosteric inhibitor, and a distractor here is often lifted verbatim from textbook prose. Go deeper · the three time-scales of control, and why they do not compete

Metabolic regulation runs on three clocks at once, and a question that seems to have two right answers is usually asking about a different clock than you assumed.

  • Allosteric, in seconds. A ligand binds, the enzyme's conformation shifts, flux changes immediately and reverses the moment the ligand leaves. AMP on PFK-1, G6P on hexokinase, F1,6BP on pyruvate kinase. No new protein, no signalling cascade.
  • Covalent and hormonal, in minutes. A kinase or phosphatase modifies the enzyme, and the modification persists until it is reversed. Glucagon → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylated liver pyruvate kinase and phosphorylated PFK-2/FBPase-2. This layer exists so that whole-body priorities can override a single cell's local reading of its own energy charge.
  • Transcriptional, in hours to days. The amount of enzyme changes. Insulin induces glucokinase, PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase; glucagon and cortisol induce PEPCK, glucose 6-phosphatase and fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase. PEPCK is the pure case — it has essentially no allosteric regulation at all and is controlled almost entirely at this level, which is why steroid excess produces hyperglycaemia over days rather than minutes.

The layers do not conflict because they answer different questions. Allosteric control asks "does this cell need ATP right now?"; hormonal control asks "does the organism need blood glucose right now?"; transcriptional control asks "what kind of metabolic life is this cell going to be living next week?"

§ 04

The ATP ledger, and the fates of pyruvate

The ideaGlycolysis produces two things: a small amount of usable energy, and a used-up carrier that must be handed back before the line can run again. The energy is almost trivial — the great majority of what a sugar is worth is still locked in the pyruvate at the end. But the carrier problem is urgent. There is only a tiny stock of it in the cell, and if it is not restored within seconds the whole pathway stops. Everything a cell does with pyruvate — burn it, turn it into lactate, turn it into alcohol — is at bottom a solution to that one problem, and which solution it picks decides how much energy it eventually gets.

Doing the ledger honestly

Three habits keep the arithmetic right. First, remember that the payoff phase runs twice. Second, state which shuttle you are using, because the answer differs by two. Worked example 3 — the complete ATP ledger, three ways

  3. (a) Liver uses the malate–aspartate shuttle, so each cytosolic NADH is delivered as matrix NADH: 2 + 2(2.5) + 25 = 2 + 5 + 25 = 32 ATP.
  4. (b) Skeletal muscle (and brain) uses the glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle, which hands electrons to FAD: 2 + 2(1.5) + 25 = 2 + 3 + 25 = 30 ATP.
  5. (c) From glycogen, phosphorolysis delivers glucose 1-phosphate directly, so the hexokinase ATP is never spent. Add one: 30 + 1 = 31 ATP in muscle (33 with the malate–aspartate shuttle).

Exam technique · producing an ATP yield in under a minute

  1. Commit the fixed part once — the mitochondrial half is identical in every tissue: 2(2.5) + 6(2.5) + 2(1.5) + 2 = 25, that is PDH twice, two turns of the cycle and the two GTP. Memorise 25 and you never rebuild it under time pressure.
  2. Add glycolysis, choosing the shuttle from the tissue named in the stem — skeletal muscle, brain, brown adipose use glycerol 3-phosphate: 2 + 2(1.5) + 25 = 30. Liver, kidney, heart use malate–aspartate: 2 + 2(2.5) + 25 = 32.
  3. Then two adjustments, applied independently — a glucosyl residue taken from glycogen skips the hexokinase ATP, so add 1 (31 in muscle, 33 in liver). Anaerobic means only the substrate-level phosphorylations count: 2 from free glucose, 3 from a glycogen residue.
  4. Read the option set backwards — when the choices are 30 / 31 / 32 / 33 the examiner is testing two binary decisions and no arithmetic at all: which tissue and glucose or glycogen. Settle those two first and the number is forced. The distractors are the three other corners of that 2 × 2.
  5. Sanity-check the convention — if 36 or 38 appears among the options, that paper is using the old integers (NADH = 3, FADH₂ = 2). Match the family to the P/O values printed in the stem and never mix them: a stem that says 2.5 cannot have 38 as its answer.
  6. Cheap check on anything gluconeogenic — the Cori cycle is +2 in muscle and −6 in liver, net −4. If your net comes out at −2 you have counted the two GTP as free; if it comes out positive you have the direction backwards.

What can happen to pyruvate

Pyruvate is a junction, like glucose 6-phosphate, and the question "which fate?" always resolves to "is there oxygen, and does this cell have mitochondria?"

  • Oxidative decarboxylation. The mitochondrial pyruvate carrier brings pyruvate into the matrix, and the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex converts it to acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH. Five cofactors, four of them vitamins: thiamine pyrophosphate (B1), lipoamide, coenzyme A (B5), FAD (B2), NAD⁺ (B3). The reaction is irreversible, and that single fact is the reason fatty acids cannot be turned into glucose.
  • Reduction to lactate. Lactate dehydrogenase reoxidises NADH and hands the electrons to pyruvate. Obligatory in erythrocytes, which have no mitochondria at all; obligatory in any cell during hypoxia; and used routinely by fast-twitch muscle whose glycolytic rate exceeds its mitochondrial capacity even in full oxygen.
  • Carboxylation to oxaloacetate. Pyruvate carboxylase, in the matrix — the gateway to gluconeogenesis and the main anaplerotic top-up of the citric acid cycle (§ 07).
  • Transamination to alanine. Alanine aminotransferase, reversible. This is how muscle exports nitrogen and carbon together, in the glucose–alanine cycle.
  • Decarboxylation to ethanol. Pyruvate decarboxylase (a TPP enzyme) then alcohol dehydrogenase. Yeast and some bacteria only — humans have no pyruvate decarboxylase, and any option claiming otherwise is wrong.

Lactate is not a waste product, and it is not the acid

Two corrections that examiners increasingly test. First, lactate is a fuel: it leaves the fast-twitch fibre on a monocarboxylate transporter and is oxidised by heart, slow-twitch muscle and brain, or reconverted to glucose by liver. During hard exercise the heart prefers it to glucose. Second, the protons of "lactic acidosis" do not come from lactate dehydrogenase, which actually consumes a proton. Written with its coupled ATP synthesis, glycolysis to lactate is proton-neutral; the H⁺ appears when that ATP is hydrolysed faster than oxidative phosphorylation can resynthesise it. The acidosis reports ATP turnover outrunning oxygen supply, and lactate is simply the most convenient thing to measure.

The Cori cycle, and what it actually buys

An erythrocyte or an anoxic muscle fibre exports lactate. The liver takes it up, oxidises it back to pyruvate (regenerating the NADH it will need), and rebuilds glucose. Count the cost: the muscle gained 2 ATP per glucose; the liver spends 6 nucleoside triphosphates per glucose remade (§ 07). Every turn of the cycle therefore costs the body a net 4 ATP.

That looks like a bad deal until you see what it is for. The cycle does not create energy — it moves the bill. Muscle gets ATP now, at the moment it cannot wait for oxygen, and the liver pays later, aerobically, out of fat oxidation. It also prevents lactic acidosis and recycles carbon that would otherwise be lost. The same architecture runs in the glucose–alanine cycle, which carries amino nitrogen to the liver along with the carbon skeleton and therefore costs an additional 4 ATP per urea molecule made. In both cases the liver is acting as the body's metabolic bank.

Clinical hooks worth having ready

Thiamine deficiency blocks pyruvate dehydrogenase, so pyruvate and lactate accumulate — wet beriberi, and Wernicke encephalopathy in chronic alcohol use. Give thiamine before glucose in a malnourished patient, or the glucose load precipitates the deficit. Pyruvate kinase deficiency is the second commonest enzyme cause of hereditary non-spherocytic haemolytic anaemia after G6PD deficiency: the erythrocyte has no other ATP source, so its membrane pumps fail — but the block also raises 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate upstream, which right-shifts the oxygen dissociation curve and makes the anaemia far better tolerated than the haemoglobin figure suggests. The Warburg effect — tumours running high glycolysis and lactate production even in oxygen, partly through the M2 pyruvate kinase isoenzyme — is the physiological basis of the FDG-PET scan, since 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose is phosphorylated by hexokinase and then cannot be isomerised, so it is trapped in proportion to glycolytic flux.

§ 05

Carbon bookkeeping: which carbon ends up where

The ideaIf you mark one atom of a sugar and then let the cell take the sugar apart, you can follow the mark and find out which route the molecule actually took. Experimenters have done this for a century, and exam papers ask you to predict the result. It sounds forbidding and it is not: the whole thing rests on one geometric fact. The six-carbon molecule is cut exactly down the middle, and then the two halves are made identical — but to make them identical, one of them has to be turned round. Work out which way round, once, and every labelling question in this topic becomes bookkeeping you can do on the back of the question paper.

Worked example 4 — three tracer experiments

(a) [1-14C]glucose is given to a muscle working anaerobically. Which carbon of lactate is labelled? (b) [3,4-14C]glucose is given to a well-oxygenated cell. Where does the label first appear? Exam technique · any 14C-labelling question, in four pen strokes

This is the most mechanical question type in the note, and the reason the box above is a principle rather than a mnemonic: you do not recall the answer, you run the procedure on the question paper.

  1. Write 1 2 3 | 4 5 6 in the margin. The bar is where aldolase cuts.
  2. Reverse the left half only, giving 3 2 1 | 4 5 6. The right half never moves. This single step is the question; everything after it is reading.
  3. Read down the columns — (3,4) → pyruvate C1, the carboxyl; (2,5) → C2, the carbonyl; (1,6) → C3, the methyl. Confirm each pair sums to 7 before going on; if one does not, you mis-copied.
  4. Apply the fate the stem names — PDH removes pyruvate C1, so glucose C3 and C4 are the first carbons out as CO₂. LDH reduces only C2, so a lactate label sits exactly where the pyruvate label sat. The pentose phosphate pathway decarboxylates glucose C1 and nothing else.

And on the first turn of the citric acid cycle both CO₂ come from carbons that were already in oxaloacetate, so an option that has acetyl carbon leaving on turn one is always wrong.
  6. Know the trap you are being set — answering "carboxyl" for a C1 label is exactly what you get if you skip step 2. If your answer for C1 is not the methyl carbon, you did not reverse. Budget 30 seconds; this type should never take the full 66.
§ 06

Fructose, galactose and the inborn errors

The ideaGlucose is not the only sugar arriving from a meal, and the other two common ones do not simply queue up behind it. Each has its own short private entrance into the main pathway, and each entrance joins the main road at a different place. Fructose joins below the checkpoint that decides how fast sugar is burned — so it walks straight past the traffic control, which is why a large fructose load behaves so differently from the same amount of glucose. Galactose joins right at the top. The diseases in this section are all the same story: one enzyme in a short private entrance is missing, and the queue backs up behind it. What the backed-up compound happens to be decides whether the child is perfectly healthy or critically ill.

Fructose: fast, unregulated, and lipogenic for a reason

In the liver, fructokinase phosphorylates fructose at C1. Fructokinase has a low Km, a high Vmax and no allosteric regulation whatever — so essentially all fructose reaching the liver is phosphorylated on first pass. Aldolase B then splits fructose 1-phosphate into dihydroxyacetone phosphate and free glyceraldehyde, and triose kinase phosphorylates the glyceraldehyde to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. Both products are glycolytic intermediates below PFK-1.

That is the whole story of why fructose is metabolically different. PFK-1 is the step that says "stop, there is enough ATP and enough citrate". Fructose walks past it. The trioses arrive whether or not the cell wants them, pyruvate and then acetyl-CoA accumulate, and the surplus acetyl-CoA is exported as citrate for fatty-acid synthesis and packaged into VLDL. A large fructose load therefore raises triglycerides in a way an equivalent glucose load does not — and, because fructokinase consumes ATP faster than the cell can replace it, the transient AMP surge is degraded to urate, which is why fructose raises serum uric acid.

Muscle and adipose tissue take a different route: their hexokinase will phosphorylate fructose to fructose 6-phosphate, but with a Km far above physiological fructose concentrations, so it is a minor pathway that is easily outcompeted by glucose. Fructose metabolism is essentially a hepatic story.

The pattern behind all four

Compare them in pairs and the principle jumps out. In each pair, blocking the first enzyme is mild and blocking the second is severe — because the first block leaves an innocuous sugar that the kidney can excrete, while the second block leaves a phosphorylated intermediate that is charged, cannot leave the cell, and holds a phosphate hostage. Fructose 1-phosphate and galactose 1-phosphate are the toxic species; fructose and galactose themselves are not. Note also that cataract appears in both galactose disorders, because both accumulate galactose upstream and aldose reductase turns it into galactitol; but only GALT deficiency has the liver, kidney, brain and sepsis picture, because only GALT deficiency accumulates the phosphate.

There is no 口訣 for that table and you should not build one. Four disorders, two pathways, one rule: a block before the phosphorylation step leaves an innocuous sugar that the kidney throws away, and a block after it leaves a phosphate ester that is charged, cannot leave the cell, and holds a Pi hostage. Read the enzyme order straight off the two equations in the box above — fructokinase then aldolase B, galactokinase then GALT — and the severity ranking, the hypoglycaemia, the hyperuricaemia and the cataracts all fall out of it. A four-item hook here would be a second thing to remember sitting on top of a rule you already have, and under time pressure that is exactly the kind of hook that decays into noise.

Why hereditary fructose intolerance causes hypoglycaemia

This is worth deriving rather than remembering, because it is a favourite vignette. Fructokinase is unregulated, so it keeps converting fructose to fructose 1-phosphate. Aldolase B cannot clear it, so fructose 1-phosphate accumulates to millimolar concentrations, and every molecule of it is holding one inorganic phosphate out of circulation. Cytosolic Pi falls. Now three things fail at once. ADP cannot be rephosphorylated to ATP, so the energy charge collapses and AMP is degraded to urate. Glycogen phosphorylase requires Pi as its co-substrate (§ 09), so glycogenolysis stops. And accumulated fructose 1-phosphate directly inhibits the aldolase and phosphorylase steps. The liver therefore cannot release glucose by either route — which is why an injection of glucagon during an attack produces no rise in blood glucose at all, a finding that neatly separates this from a simple fasting hypoglycaemia.

Fructose 1-phosphate has a second, non-pathological job

In a healthy liver, fructose 1-phosphate is the ligand that releases glucokinase from its nuclear regulatory protein, while fructose 6-phosphate keeps it sequestered. A small amount of dietary fructose therefore primes the liver to take up glucose — which is the physiological reason that fruit sugar and starch arriving together are handled better than either alone, and a nice example of one metabolite doing signalling work in one context and damage in another.

The polyol pathway, and why diabetes damages the tissues it does

Aldose reductase reduces glucose to sorbitol, consuming NADPH; sorbitol dehydrogenase then oxidises sorbitol to fructose. In most tissues both enzymes are present and nothing accumulates. But lens, retina, kidney, erythrocyte and Schwann cell have plenty of the reductase and little or none of the dehydrogenase. Sorbitol is a polyol: it does not cross membranes and it is osmotically active. In chronic hyperglycaemia — and remember from § 01 that all of these are insulin-independent tissues, so their intracellular glucose tracks the blood level directly — sorbitol accumulates, water follows, and the cell swells. Add the depletion of NADPH, which weakens glutathione-based antioxidant defence (§ 11), and you have a mechanistic account of diabetic cataract, retinopathy and peripheral neuropathy. Galactitol does the same thing in galactose disorders, only faster, because no dehydrogenase acts on it at all. The same chemistry is put to legitimate use in the seminal vesicle, where the polyol pathway makes the fructose that sperm use as fuel.

§ 07

Gluconeogenesis: four bypasses, not a reversal

The ideaMost of the reactions that break sugar down will happily run the other way if you push on them, because they barely release any energy in the first place. Three will not: they gave up so much energy going forwards that pushing them backwards is like pushing water uphill. So the cell does not push. It builds three detours — four enzymes in all, because one detour needs two steps — each of which spends extra energy to get around the obstacle. This is not wasteful design, it is the only design that works: if the same enzyme ran both ways, the cell could never make one direction go while the other stopped, and the two pathways would simply spin against each other burning fuel for nothing.

The four bypass enzymes, in the order the carbon travels

Pathway Produces Fresh Glucose

Pyruvate carboxylase → PEP carboxykinase → Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase → Glucose 6-phosphatase. This is the version that circulates in board-review material, so prefer it to anything homemade. Read it upwards against the glycolytic map and the pairing is automatic: the first two together replace pyruvate kinase, the third replaces PFK-1, the fourth replaces hexokinase — three obstacles, four enzymes, because the first obstacle is too large for one reaction. It carries no costs and does not need to: 1 ATP at pyruvate carboxylase and 1 GTP at PEP carboxykinase, and nothing at the two phosphatases, because they hydrolyse rather than transfer. The third high-energy phosphate per pyruvate is spent outside the bypasses, at the reverse of phosphoglycerate kinase — which is how 3 per pyruvate becomes 6 per glucose. Nor does it carry the compartments: pyruvate carboxylase is the only one of the four in the mitochondrial matrix, and glucose 6-phosphatase is the only one inside the ER lumen.

Why the carboxylate-then-decarboxylate detour

Pyruvate kinase released 31 kJ·mol⁻¹ going forward, and one GTP is not enough to push it back. So the cell raises pyruvate to a higher-energy intermediate first. Pyruvate carboxylase, a biotin enzyme, spends one ATP to attach CO₂ and make oxaloacetate. PEP carboxykinase then spends a GTP and, in the same step, removes the CO₂ again — and it is the decarboxylation, thermodynamically very favourable, that supplies the extra push needed to create the enol phosphate. Adding a group in order to remove it is a recurring trick in biochemistry: the same device appears in fatty-acid synthesis, where acetyl-CoA is carboxylated to malonyl-CoA only so that the same carboxyl can be lost again as CO₂ to drive the condensation.

Two details that get examined. First, pyruvate carboxylase is switched on by acetyl-CoA, and essentially dead without it. That is a beautiful piece of logic: acetyl-CoA piles up when fat is being oxidised, which is exactly the situation in which the liver needs to make glucose. Fat cannot supply the carbon, but it supplies the signal and the ATP. Second, oxaloacetate cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. It is reduced to malate, exported, and reoxidised in the cytosol — which conveniently also delivers one cytosolic NADH, exactly what the reverse GAPDH step will need.

Worked example 5 — the price of gluconeogenesis, and the Cori cycle balance sheet

(a) Count the high-energy phosphates needed to make one glucose from two pyruvate. (b) Compare with what a simple reversal of glycolysis would have cost. (c) Close the Cori cycle: what is the net whole-body ATP balance per turn?

  1. (a) Per pyruvate: pyruvate carboxylase 1 ATP, PEP carboxykinase 1 GTP, and the reverse of phosphoglycerate kinase 1 ATP. That is 3 nucleoside triphosphates. For two pyruvate: 2 × 3 = 6 — conventionally written as 4 ATP + 2 GTP, plus 2 NADH for the reverse GAPDH step. Note that the two phosphatase bypasses cost nothing extra: they hydrolyse rather than transfer, so the phosphate energy is simply lost as heat.
  2. (b) Glycolysis made 2 ATP going forward, so a naive reversal would need to put 2 back. The real cost is 6. The extra 6 − 2 = 4 high-energy phosphates are the thermodynamic surcharge for making the pathway exergonic in both directions — which is the price of being able to control it. A pathway that is reversible cannot be regulated.
  3. (c) Muscle: glucose → 2 lactate yields +2 ATP. Liver: 2 lactate → glucose costs −6. Net per turn: 2 − 6 = −4 ATP, paid by the liver, aerobically, out of fatty-acid oxidation.
  4. Where do the 2 NADH come from when the substrate is lactate? From lactate dehydrogenase running in reverse: lactate + NAD⁺ → pyruvate + NADH + H⁺. Lactate arrives pre-loaded with its own reducing equivalents. Starting from alanine instead, no NADH is generated, so the malate shuttle described above has to supply it — which is why alanine and lactate are not interchangeable substrates at the level of detail an exam may probe.

What can and cannot become glucose

  • Lactate — via LDH to pyruvate. The Cori cycle.
  • Glucogenic amino acids — all of them except leucine and lysine. Alanine and glutamine are quantitatively the most important; alanine enters as pyruvate, most others enter the citric acid cycle and leave it as oxaloacetate.
  • Glycerol — from triacylglycerol lipolysis. Glycerol kinase (a liver enzyme; adipose tissue has almost none, which is why adipocytes cannot recycle their own glycerol) makes glycerol 3-phosphate, and glycerol 3-phosphate dehydrogenase makes DHAP. This is the only part of a fat molecule that yields glucose.
  • Propionate — from odd-chain fatty acids and from the branched-chain amino acids: propionyl-CoA → (biotin) → methylmalonyl-CoA → (vitamin B12) → succinyl-CoA → oxaloacetate. Numerically minor in humans, conceptually important because it is the exception that proves the rule about fatty acids.
  • Not acetyl-CoA. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible, so acetyl-CoA cannot go back to pyruvate; and entering the citric acid cycle does not help, because the two carbons that enter as acetyl are matched by two carbons leaving as CO₂. Plants and bacteria escape this with the glyoxylate cycle (isocitrate lyase and malate synthase); animals have neither enzyme. Hence: no net conversion of even-chain fatty acid or ketone-body carbon into glucose, ever.

Three clinical stories that are really this section in disguise

Alcoholic hypoglycaemia. Ethanol is oxidised by alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase, both of which reduce NAD⁺. The cytosolic NADH/NAD⁺ ratio rises steeply, pulling lactate away from pyruvate and malate away from oxaloacetate — removing both gluconeogenic substrates at once. A fasting, glycogen-depleted drinker therefore becomes hypoglycaemic and lactic-acidotic, and the same surplus NADH pushes DHAP towards glycerol 3-phosphate, favouring triglyceride synthesis: hepatic steatosis in the same mechanism. Von Gierke disease (GSD I) is loss of glucose 6-phosphatase — so neither gluconeogenesis nor glycogenolysis can deliver free glucose, and G6P backs up into glycolysis (lactic acidosis), into the pentose phosphate pathway (NADPH → lipogenesis → hyperlipidaemia, and increased purine turnover → hyperuricaemia, compounded by lactate competing with urate for renal excretion). Glucagon produces no glycaemic response. Metformin suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis, in part by mildly inhibiting mitochondrial respiration and thus lowering hepatic energy charge and activating AMPK — which is also why its rare serious toxicity is lactic acidosis, and why it is withheld in renal failure.

Go deeper · biotin, compartments, and the two routes out of the mitochondrion

Biotin is the CO₂ carrier for every carboxylase in human metabolism, attached through an amide bond to a lysine residue (the conjugate is called biocytin). There are four: pyruvate carboxylase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase (fatty-acid synthesis), propionyl-CoA carboxylase and methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase. Biotinidase deficiency, which prevents biotin being recycled, therefore presents as a multiple carboxylase deficiency with lactic acidosis, alopecia and a seborrhoeic rash — and responds dramatically to oral biotin.

The compartment problem. Pyruvate carboxylase is in the matrix; the cytosolic isoform of PEP carboxykinase is not. Oxaloacetate cannot cross the inner membrane, so it must be disguised. Which disguise the liver uses depends on the substrate, and this is the sort of detail Lehninger draws as a figure:

  • From lactate: lactate dehydrogenase has already made cytosolic NADH, so the cell does not need any more. Oxaloacetate is transaminated to aspartate and exported on the glutamate–aspartate carrier, then transaminated back. No redox equivalents are moved.
  • From pyruvate or alanine: no cytosolic NADH has been generated, and the reverse GAPDH step needs it. Oxaloacetate is reduced to malate by matrix malate dehydrogenase, exported, and reoxidised in the cytosol — exporting one NADH per oxaloacetate as a side effect. The malate–aspartate shuttle of § 04, run backwards.

Notice how this dovetails with § 04: the tissues that do gluconeogenesis are exactly the tissues that use the malate–aspartate shuttle, because they need it to run in both directions.

§ 08

Reciprocal control and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate

The ideaTwo opposing pathways run through the same cell at the same time. If both were switched on at once, the cell would build sugar and take it apart simultaneously, achieving nothing except turning food into heat. So the cell needs a switch that cannot possibly leave both on — one signal that says "go" to one pathway and, with the same breath, "stop" to the other. The liver's solution is a small molecule that is not a building block at all. It is manufactured purely to be a message, it speeds up the breakdown enzyme and slows the building enzyme, and its concentration is set by a single unusual protein that is a maker and a destroyer in one, with a switch on top of it that hormones can flip.

Glucagon shuts hepatic glycolysis at two points, not one

The same PKA that phosphorylates the bifunctional enzyme also phosphorylates the liver isoenzyme of pyruvate kinase, inactivating it. So a single hormone closes the pathway at PFK-1 (indirectly, by lowering F2,6BP) and again at pyruvate kinase (directly). Meanwhile PEP, no longer being consumed, is available for gluconeogenesis, and transcription of PEPCK and glucose 6-phosphatase is being induced in the background. Four mechanisms, one message. Muscle receives none of this: skeletal muscle has no glucagon receptor, and its pyruvate kinase (M1) has no PKA site, because a muscle has no reason to stop using glucose merely because the blood level has fallen.

The heart does the opposite, deliberately

Adrenaline acting on cardiac muscle raises cAMP just as glucagon does in liver — but the cardiac isoenzyme of the bifunctional protein carries its regulatory serine in a different place, and phosphorylating it activates the kinase domain. F2,6BP therefore rises in a stimulated heart, and glycolysis accelerates. This is not an exception to the rule; it is the rule applied correctly. The rule is "serve the organism's need", and the organism's need from a heart under adrenaline is more ATP, not more exported glucose. Any question that asks about adrenaline and F2,6BP is asking whether you know which tissue you are in.

Exam technique · direction questions (hormone → which way?)

  1. Ask which organ before you ask which molecule — the identical cascade has opposite endpoints in liver and heart, and skeletal muscle has no glucagon receptor at all. Any option that applies glucagon to muscle is dead on sight, whatever else it says, and that alone is often one of the five gone.
  2. Derive the endpoint from the organism's need, then check the chain against it — glucagon means "the blood needs glucose", so hepatic glycolysis must finish off. Adrenaline on a working heart means "this muscle needs ATP", so cardiac glycolysis must finish on. Fix the endpoint first and you cannot invert the four arrows in between; work forwards through the arrows and one slip flips the answer.
  3. One permanent kill — F2,6BP lies on no pathway and nothing consumes it as a substrate. Any option calling it a glycolytic intermediate, or having aldolase or PFK-1 act on it, is wrong regardless of direction. This distractor appears often because it sounds like the textbook sentence about F1,6BP.
  4. Use the mirror as a filter — one ligand, two enzymes, opposite signs: F2,6BP activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1. An option that has it doing the same thing to both has broken the definition of reciprocal control and can go without any recall of the cascade.
  5. When two options are the same mechanism pointed opposite ways, one of them is the answer. That puts you at worst 50/50 on a five-option question, which under −0.5 marking is strongly positive expected value — so answer it even if you cannot recall the direction, and never leave that one blank.

Substrate cycles are not simply waste

PFK-1 and FBPase-1 can in principle run at the same time, hydrolysing one ATP per cycle and producing nothing but heat. Reciprocal regulation exists to stop that. But a small amount of cycling is deliberately tolerated, and it buys sensitivity: if forward and reverse fluxes are both large and nearly equal, a small percentage change in either produces a large percentage change in the net rate. The cell trades some ATP for a more responsive control system, exactly as an engineer trades power for gain. Bumblebees push the trade to its limit — they run the cycle openly in flight muscle to warm the thorax before take-off, and a bee lacking flight-muscle FBPase-1 cannot warm up at all.

The reciprocal-regulation checklist

Three opposing enzyme pairs on this page follow the identical template, and knowing the template is worth more than knowing any one of them: PFK-1 / FBPase-1 at fructose 6-phosphate; pyruvate kinase / (PC + PEPCK) at pyruvate; and, in § 10, glycogen phosphorylase / glycogen synthase. In every case: (1) the two directions use different enzymes; (2) a single second messenger reaches both; (3) the modification that activates one inactivates the other; (4) the tissue distribution of the isoenzymes decides who obeys which hormone. If a question describes a novel regulator, apply the template and you can usually derive the answer without having met the molecule before.

§ 09

Glycogen: structure, synthesis, breakdown

The ideaA cell that stored its sugar as loose sugar would burst: thousands of small molecules exert an osmotic pull that would drag water in. Stringing them into one enormous molecule solves that at a stroke — one particle instead of fifty thousand. But a plain string has only one loose end, and one loose end means one place to add or remove a unit, which is far too slow for a muscle that needs fuel in a second. So the string is made bushy. Every dozen units or so a new side branch starts, and after twelve rounds of that there are thousands of loose ends, all of them available at once. Almost every fact in this section — including the two extra enzymes nobody remembers — exists to create or dismantle those branches.

Synthesis: four steps, and a protein that starts itself

  1. Phosphoglucomutase moves the phosphate: glucose 6-phosphate ⇌ glucose 1-phosphate, through a glucose 1,6-bisphosphate intermediate. Freely reversible, and used in both directions.
  2. UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase activates it: glucose 1-phosphate + UTP → UDP-glucose + pyrophosphate. The reaction itself is near equilibrium; inorganic pyrophosphatase then hydrolyses the pyrophosphate, and that strongly exergonic step is what commits the glucose. The same device drives DNA synthesis, RNA synthesis and fatty-acid activation.
  3. Glycogen synthase transfers the glucosyl group from UDP-glucose to the C4 hydroxyl of a non-reducing end, making α(1→4). This is the rate-limiting and regulated step. It cannot start a chain from nothing and it cannot make a branch.
  4. Branching enzyme (amylo-(1,4→1,6)-transglycosylase) takes a block of 6 or 7 residues from the end of a chain that is at least 11 residues long and reattaches it through an α(1→6) bond to a C6 hydroxyl further in, at least four residues from any existing branch.

The primer problem is solved by glycogenin, a small protein that glucosylates itself on an active-site tyrosine using UDP-glucose, then extends the chain to about eight residues by its own glucosyltransferase activity. Only then does glycogen synthase take over. Glycogenin remains buried at the centre of the finished particle, which is why every glycogen molecule has exactly one reducing end — it is spoken for.

Breakdown: phosphorolysis, and the two jobs of the debranching enzyme

Glycogen phosphorylase attacks α(1→4) bonds at non-reducing ends using inorganic phosphate rather than water. The distinction matters twice over. The product, glucose 1-phosphate, is already phosphorylated — so the cell skips the hexokinase step and its ATP, which is exactly why a glycogen residue is worth 3 anaerobic ATP rather than 2. And being charged, it cannot leak out of the cell. The enzyme's cofactor is pyridoxal phosphate, used in a way seen nowhere else in metabolism: not as a Schiff-base partner for an amino group, but for its own phosphate group, which acts as a general acid–base catalyst. Phosphorylase holds about 80 % of the body's vitamin B6.

Phosphorylase cannot pass a branch. It stops four residues short of an α(1→6) point, leaving a structure called a limit dextrin. Clearing it takes one polypeptide with two different catalytic activities:

  • Oligo-α(1,4)→α(1,4) glucantransferase lifts the outer three residues off as a block and reattaches them, still α(1→4), to the non-reducing end of a nearby chain.
  • Amylo-α(1,6)-glucosidase then hydrolyses the single remaining α(1→6)-linked residue, releasing free glucose.

Hence the standard figure: glycogenolysis yields roughly 90 % glucose 1-phosphate and about 8–10 % free glucose, one free glucose per branch point. Phosphoglucomutase converts the glucose 1-phosphate to glucose 6-phosphate, and there the two tissues part company — the liver hydrolyses it to glucose and exports it, while muscle sends it into glycolysis.

The four numbers of branching and debranching

Eleven, then six or seven; four is three plus one

Eleven rhymes with seven, which locks the two building numbers together, and the second half is an equation that audits itself. Building: branching enzyme waits until a chain is at least 11 residues long, then lifts a block of 6 or 7 off its non-reducing end and reattaches it through an α(1→6) bond. Dismantling: phosphorylase stops 4 residues short of the branch point, and those four are cleared as 3 + 1 — the debranching transferase moves 3 as an α(1→4) block onto a nearby chain, the α(1→6)-glucosidase hydrolyses the last 1 and releases it as free glucose. If your 3 + 1 does not come to the 4 left standing, you have misremembered one of them. My own line. It leaves out two other numbers in this section, and you should hold them separately: branch points occur every 8–12 residues along a chain, and a new branch must be placed at least 4 residues away from an existing one — do not confuse that 4 with the 4 that phosphorylase leaves. The ≈ 90 % glucose 1-phosphate to ≈ 10 % free glucose ratio needs no hook: it follows from exactly one free glucose per branch point.

Worked example 6 — how long does liver glycogen last?

An adult liver holds about 100 g of glycogen. The brain alone consumes roughly 120 g of glucose per day. Ignoring gluconeogenesis, how long could hepatic glycogen support the brain — and how does that compare with the glucose actually circulating?

  1. A glucosyl residue in a polymer has Mr = 162.14, not 180.16: a molecule of water is lost at each linkage. So 100 g of glycogen is 100 / 162.14 = 0.617 mol = 617 mmol of residues.
  2. Released as free glucose, that is 0.617 × 180.16 = 111 g of glucose — the mass increases on hydrolysis, which is a small but genuine trap in stoichiometry questions.
  3. Duration: 111 / 120 = 0.93 day ≈ 22 h.
  4. Compare the circulating pool: 5 mmol·L⁻¹ across ~5 L of blood is 25 mmol = 4.5 g; over the whole extracellular volume of ~15 L, about 13.5 g. That is under three hours' supply for the brain alone, and the blood pool by itself is barely one hour.
  5. So blood glucose is a buffer measured in minutes, hepatic glycogen a reserve measured in hours, and anything longer must come from gluconeogenesis. This is exactly the clinical timeline: hepatic glycogen is substantially depleted by 24 h of fasting, and gluconeogenesis is already supplying about half of hepatic glucose output by 16 h.
  6. Muscle holds four times as much glycogen — about 400 g, roughly 444 g of glucose equivalents — and none of it can reach the blood directly, for want of glucose 6-phosphatase. It reaches the blood only the long way round, as lactate through the Cori cycle.

Phosphorolysis is not hydrolysis, and the two glucosidases are not the same

Phosphorylase uses Pi and produces glucose 1-phosphate; the debranching glucosidase uses water and produces free glucose. A third enzyme entirely, lysosomal acid α-glucosidase (acid maltase), hydrolyses glycogen that has been swallowed by autophagy — only a few per cent of the total, and in a completely separate compartment. That is why its deficiency, Pompe disease, produces massive lysosomal glycogen accumulation and cardiomyopathy while leaving blood glucose entirely normal. If a stem mentions normal blood sugar with a severe glycogen phenotype, the answer is lysosomal.

§ 10

The phosphorylase cascade and the storage diseases

The ideaA hormone arriving at a liver cell is present in vanishingly small amounts — a few thousand molecules against a cell containing billions of sugar units. It cannot possibly act on the store directly. What it does instead is start a relay in which each runner activates many of the next, so that a whisper at the surface becomes a shout inside. And because the same relay both switches breakdown on and switches storage off, the cell can never end up doing both at once. The muscle version of the relay has an extra input that the liver version lacks: the same calcium signal that makes the muscle contract also, without any hormone at all, tells it to start burning its own store — so the fuel arrives at the same instant as the demand.

One signal, both switches

Follow protein kinase A once activated and you find it doing four things, all pointing the same way. It phosphorylates phosphorylase kinase, which activates it, which converts phosphorylase b to phosphorylase a — breakdown on. It phosphorylates glycogen synthase directly, which inactivates it — storage off. It phosphorylates inhibitor-1, which shuts down PP1, so neither of the first two modifications can be undone while the signal lasts. And in the liver it phosphorylates pyruvate kinase and the PFK-2/FBPase-2 enzyme of § 08, so the glucose being liberated is not immediately burnt. Reciprocal regulation is not a special property of glycogen metabolism; it is what any well-designed control system looks like.

The a / b trap

a is Active · Phosphorylase Prefers Phosphate

The first clause is the easy half and it holds for both enzymes: the letter a is the more active form of whichever one you are holding, so phosphorylase a and glycogen synthase a are each the working form of their own enzyme. The second clause fixes the half people invert, and it runs on three P's — Phosphorylase Prefers Phosphate — so phosphorylase a is the phosphorylated one. Synthase a is then forced to be the dephosphorylated one, because a single PKA signal has to switch breakdown on and storage off in the same moment. Store that one direction rather than two separate states and you cannot get them the wrong way round. My own phrasing of a standard trap. The second clause is scoped to glycogen — it happens to survive into § 08, since PKA-phosphorylated liver pyruvate kinase and PFK-2 also point away from storing glucose, but do not stretch it further than the note takes it. Two allosteric overrides sit outside the rule entirely and are examined precisely because they do: AMP activates muscle phosphorylase b with no phosphate involved at all, and glucose inactivates liver phosphorylase a.

The liver's glucose sensor is phosphorylase a itself

This is the most elegant mechanism in the section and it is frequently examined. Free glucose binds to liver phosphorylase a and shifts it towards the inactive T conformation. That conformational change exposes the phosphorylated Ser14 to PP1, which had been bound to the enzyme and held inert. PP1 removes the phosphate, converting a back to b, and glycogenolysis stops. But now PP1 is released — and the first thing it does is dephosphorylate glycogen synthase, switching storage on. So the arrival of glucose in the hepatocyte automatically stops breakdown and starts synthesis, in that order, with a built-in delay between them and no hormone required. Phosphorylase a is functioning as a glucose receptor.

Why muscle glycogen cannot treat hypoglycaemia

This single fact generates a family of questions. Muscle lacks glucose 6-phosphatase, so its glucose 6-phosphate has exactly one exit: glycolysis. A 400 g muscle store is therefore invisible to the blood sugar. It contributes indirectly — as lactate through the Cori cycle, and as alanine through the glucose–alanine cycle — but never directly. The corollary is diagnostic: a patient whose glycogen disorder affects muscle only (McArdle, Tarui) has normal fasting glucose and normal glucagon responses, while a patient whose disorder affects the liver (von Gierke, Hers, phosphorylase kinase deficiency) presents with fasting hypoglycaemia and hepatomegaly and no myopathy. Reasoning your way through the table

Ask three questions in order. Is blood glucose affected? If no, the lesion is muscle-only (V, VII) or lysosomal (II). Is lactate high or low? High points to type I, where G6P cannot leave and floods glycolysis; normal or low points to III, VI and IX, where gluconeogenesis still works and the child compensates with ketones. Is the glycogen structurally abnormal? Short outer branches = debranching (III); long unbranched chains = branching (IV). Almost every vignette resolves on those three.

Exam technique · triaging a glycogen storage disease vignette

  1. Organ first, in ten seconds — hepatomegaly with fasting hypoglycaemia → a liver form (0, I, III, VI, IX). Exercise cramps and myoglobinuria with a normal fasting glucose → a muscle form (V, VII). Hypotonia, macroglossia and cardiomegaly with normal blood glucose → lysosomal (II, Pompe). That one reading usually removes three of the five options before you have named a single enzyme.
  2. Then the two laboratory values — lactate and urate both high → type I, because glucose 6-phosphate cannot leave and floods glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway. Both normal → III, VI or IX, where gluconeogenesis is intact and the child compensates with ketones.
  3. Then split the two muscle forms — a second wind means McArdle (V). Worse after a carbohydrate meal, with a compensated haemolytic anaemia and no second wind, means Tarui (VII).
  4. Structural wording is a giveaway — "short outer branches" or "limit dextrin" → debranching enzyme (III). "Long, poorly branched, amylopectin-like", with cirrhosis → branching enzyme (IV).
  5. Check the confirmatory test was interpretable before you trust it — in ischaemic forearm exercise a flat venous lactate only counts if the ammonia rose normally. A flat ammonia means the patient did not work hard enough and the test proves nothing. Any option that reads the lactate without the ammonia has ignored the control.
  6. Where the time goes — this family is worth the full 66 seconds, because the vignette hands you every discriminator. Spend that time on the organ and the two laboratory values, never on trying to recall the enzyme list.
Go deeper · why a cascade, and how much amplification it really buys

A cascade multiplies because every tier is catalytic. One occupied receptor activates many G proteins over its lifetime; one adenylyl cyclase makes many cAMP; one PKA catalytic subunit phosphorylates many phosphorylase kinase molecules; one phosphorylase kinase activates many phosphorylase molecules; and one phosphorylase a releases glucose 1-phosphate for as long as it stays phosphorylated. Multiply three or four modest gains of a few hundred each and the product runs into the tens of millions — which is the figure textbooks quote, and it is a product of estimates rather than a measured constant, so treat the order of magnitude as the teaching point rather than the digits.

Amplification is not the only thing the architecture buys, and the other benefits are the ones worth being able to state:

  • Multiple inputs at one node. Phosphorylase kinase integrates PKA phosphorylation (hormonal) and Ca²⁺ binding (electrical). Neither alone is maximal; together they are synergistic. A frightened, sprinting animal gets both.
  • Sensitivity and threshold. Because PKA must bind four cAMP molecules to release its catalytic subunits, the response to cAMP is sigmoidal, not linear — small fluctuations are ignored and a real signal produces a near-complete switch.
  • A fast off-switch. Phosphodiesterase destroys cAMP within seconds, and PP1 reverses every phosphorylation. Caffeine and theophylline inhibit the phosphodiesterase, which is why they prolong adrenergic effects.
  • Coordination. One kinase reaching many substrates guarantees that opposing pathways cannot both be active — a property no amount of independent allosteric regulation could deliver.
§ 11

The pentose phosphate pathway and G6PD deficiency

The ideaA cell needs two completely different things that both look like "hydrogen carriers". One kind collects electrons from fuel being burnt and takes them to be turned into ATP. The other kind hands electrons out — to build fat, to build cholesterol, and above all to keep the cell's own repair chemistry stocked so that oxygen does not slowly destroy it from the inside. The two carriers are chemically almost identical; they differ by a single phosphate that acts purely as a label, so that enzymes can tell them apart and the cell can keep one pool empty and the other full. This pathway is the factory for the second kind. It makes no ATP and it costs none; it exists to supply reducing power and, as a side product, the five-carbon sugar that nucleotides are built on.

Definition & key facts

Pentose phosphate pathway（磷酸戊糖途徑）
Also called the hexose monophosphate shunt. Entirely cytosolic. An irreversible oxidative arm that makes 2 NADPH and loses one carbon as CO₂, followed by a fully reversible non-oxidative arm that reshuffles five-carbon sugars back into glycolytic intermediates. No ATP is made or consumed anywhere in it.

Full definition, symbols and units
The oxidative arm

The non-oxidative arm
Ribulose 5-phosphate is isomerised to ribose 5-phosphate or epimerised to xylulose 5-phosphate; then transketolase (thiamine pyrophosphate, moves 2 carbons) and transaldolase (a lysine Schiff base, moves 3 carbons) shuffle the skeletons into fructose 6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. Almost entirely by the availability of NADP⁺. The cytosolic NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio is about 100:1, so G6PD is normally starved of its cosubstrate and accelerates the instant NADPH is consumed. Demand-driven control, with no hormone and no allosteric effector needed.

NADPH and NADH: one chemistry, two pools, opposite jobs

NADPH differs from NADH by a phosphate on the 2′-hydroxyl of the adenosine ribose — a position nowhere near the nicotinamide ring that actually does the redox chemistry. The phosphate is therefore not chemistry, it is a label. Catabolic dehydrogenases have binding sites that recognise NAD⁺; the reductases of biosynthesis and antioxidant defence recognise NADP⁺. That lets the cell hold the two pools in opposite states: the NAD pool is kept largely oxidised (NADH/NAD⁺ ≈ 10−3), poised to accept electrons from fuel; the NADP pool is kept largely reduced (≈ 100:1), poised to donate them. Two thermodynamic reservoirs, chemically interchangeable, kept apart by a recognition tag. It is one of the most elegant ideas in metabolism and it is regularly examined as a straight comparison.

G6PD deficiency: why the red cell in particular

Reactive oxygen species are made continuously, and the cell's front line against them is glutathione: glutathione peroxidase uses two GSH to destroy a peroxide, producing GSSG, and glutathione reductase regenerates GSH using NADPH. Cut the NADPH supply and that cycle stops within minutes of an oxidant challenge.

An erythrocyte is uniquely exposed. It has no mitochondria, so it has no alternative NADPH-generating route. It does carry cytosolic catalase — plenty of it — but catalase holds bound NADPH to keep itself from being inactivated, so even that second line of defence leans on the same supply. It has no nucleus and no ribosomes, so it cannot replace an enzyme as that enzyme ages over the cell's 120-day life. And it spends its entire existence carrying oxygen, the largest oxidant load in the body. G6PD is effectively its only source of NADPH — which is why a deficiency that other tissues shrug off destroys red cells.

The failure sequence is worth being able to recite: NADPH falls → GSH cannot be regenerated → haemoglobin is oxidised and denatures → it precipitates as Heinz bodies (visible only with a supravital stain such as crystal violet, not on a routine film) → splenic macrophages pluck the inclusions out of passing cells → bite cells and blister cells appear → acute haemolysis, jaundice and haemoglobinuria.

G6PD deficiency, the details that decide the question

Inheritance is X-linked, so affected patients are overwhelmingly male; heterozygous females are mosaics by lyonisation and carry two red-cell populations. It is the commonest enzymopathy in the world, and its geography — Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, Southeast Asia — matches endemic Plasmodium falciparum, because the deficiency is protective, the same evolutionary bargain as sickle trait and thalassaemia. The A− variant makes a normal enzyme with a short half-life, so only old red cells are deficient and haemolysis is self-limiting; the Mediterranean variant has low activity in cells of every age, is more severe, and is the one in which favism occurs. Triggers: infection (the commonest of all), primaquine and other 8-aminoquinolines, sulfonamides, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, rasburicase, naphthalene mothballs, and fava beans. Methylene blue is contraindicated for methaemoglobinaemia here, because its own action requires the NADPH the patient cannot make. And the diagnostic trap: an enzyme assay taken during an acute crisis can be falsely normal, because the most deficient cells have already lysed and the surviving reticulocyte-rich population has the highest residual activity — repeat it two to three months later.

Go deeper · the four modes, and the thiamine assay

Because the non-oxidative arm is fully reversible, the pathway is not one route but four, and the cell selects by demand alone:

  1. Much ribose, little NADPH — a rapidly dividing cell needs nucleotides but not reducing power. It runs the non-oxidative arm backwards: fructose 6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate from glycolysis are converted into ribose 5-phosphate without touching G6PD at all. Worth remembering because it disproves the common belief that ribose can only come from the oxidative arm.
  2. Balanced needs — the oxidative arm alone: G6P → R5P + 2 NADPH + CO₂.
  3. Much NADPH, little ribose — adipose tissue and lactating mammary gland. Full recycling mode, as in worked example 7: up to 12 NADPH per G6P completely oxidised.
  4. NADPH and ATP both wanted — the pentoses are converted to fructose 6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and carried on down glycolysis to pyruvate.

Transketolase and thiamine. Transketolase is one of only four thiamine pyrophosphate enzymes in human metabolism, alongside pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase. Because it is easy to sample, erythrocyte transketolase activity measured with and without added TPP is the classical functional assay for thiamine deficiency: a large activation by added cofactor means the enzyme in the patient's cells was sitting there empty.

A link back to § 08. Xylulose 5-phosphate from this pathway activates protein phosphatase 2A, which dephosphorylates the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 enzyme and therefore raises fructose 2,6-bisphosphate. So flux through the pentose phosphate pathway itself signals "carbohydrate is abundant" and pushes the liver towards glycolysis and lipogenesis — the two pathways are not as separate as the maps suggest.
