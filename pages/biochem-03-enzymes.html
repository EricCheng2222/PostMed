Enzymes: Kinetics, Inhibition and Regulation

One idea organises the whole note: an enzyme does nothing to the destination of a reaction and everything to the route. It cannot make an unfavourable reaction favourable, it cannot change an equilibrium constant, and it comes out of the reaction unchanged. What it does is build a lower pass over the energy barrier, by being shaped to fit not the substrate but the strained, half-reacted arrangement the substrate has to pass through. Everything else here — why Km is not a dissociation constant, why a competitive inhibitor leaves Vmax alone, why an uncompetitive inhibitor is the one you cannot out-compete with more substrate, why cooperative enzymes exist at all — falls out of that single sentence plus some careful algebra.

§ 01

Catalysis: the barrier, not the equilibrium

The ideaA reaction that "does not happen" in a test tube is usually one that is perfectly happy to happen — the products are already more stable than the starting material. What stops it is that on the way across, the molecule has to pass through an awkward, half-broken arrangement that costs a lot of energy, and at room temperature almost none of the molecules ever have that much. A catalyst does not push the reaction downhill. It builds a lower pass over the same hill. Because it is the same hill, with the same two ends, the catalyst helps the reverse journey by exactly as much as the forward one — which is why an enzyme can never change where a reaction ends up.

Definition & formula

Catalyst
A substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing a pathway with a lower activation free energy ΔG‡, is regenerated unchanged at the end, and does not alter ΔG°′ or the equilibrium constant K′eq.
k = kBTh · e−ΔG‡/RT Eyring: the rate constant depends exponentially on the barrier, so small changes in ΔG‡ are enormous changes in rate
Full definition, symbols and units
Transition state (‡)
The highest-energy arrangement on the reaction coordinate — bonds part-formed and part-broken. It is not a molecule: it sits at a maximum, not in a well, and survives for about one bond vibration, ~10−13 s. It cannot be isolated.
Intermediate
A genuine chemical species that sits in a local minimum (a well) between two transition states. It has a finite lifetime and can sometimes be trapped and identified — the acyl-enzyme of § 03 is one.
Rate enhancement
kcat/kuncat, the factor by which the enzyme accelerates its reaction. Ranges from ~105 to ~1017.

Why the exponential matters

Because k ∝ e−ΔG‡/RT, rate depends on the barrier exponentially. Take the log and the arithmetic becomes friendly: at 25 °C, every factor of ten in rate costs 2.303 RT = 5.71 kJ·mol⁻¹ of barrier. So an enzyme that accelerates its reaction 106-fold — a modest enzyme — has lowered ΔG‡ by 6 × 5.71 = 34.2 kJ·mol⁻¹. That is about the energy of two or three hydrogen bonds. The lesson is that catalysis does not require exotic chemistry; a handful of well-placed weak interactions, applied to the right structure at the right moment, is enough.

Four things an enzyme does not do

It does not change ΔG°′, K′eq, or the position of equilibrium. It does not make an endergonic reaction spontaneous — cells solve that by coupling, not by catalysis. It does not change the ratio of forward to reverse rate constants, only both together. And it is not consumed, so it never appears in the balanced equation. The classic exam sentence is "an enzyme accelerates the attainment of equilibrium without altering it"; every distractor built on this topic is a violation of one of those four.

Exam technique · killing options with the thermodynamic filter

  1. Scan for the forbidden verbs first — any option saying an enzyme shifts the equilibrium, changes K′eq, changes ΔG°′, changes the free energy of the reaction, or makes an endergonic reaction spontaneous is wrong without further thought. That is one or two options gone in three seconds — and with five options and −0.5 marking, a single elimination already makes answering strictly better than a blank.
  2. Then the symmetry filter — "lowers the activation energy of the forward reaction" is wrong as written. The barrier is the same hill from both ends, so forward and reverse rate constants rise by exactly the same factor. Any option that speeds one direction preferentially is out.
  3. Then the stoichiometry filter — the enzyme is regenerated, so it never appears in the balanced equation and is neither reactant nor product. An option that writes it into the stoichiometry is out.
  4. Check which quantity was actually asked — ΔG‡ (what happens in between) or ΔG°′ / K′eq (the difference between the two ends)? Distractors on this topic are usually true sentences aimed at the wrong quantity, so an option can sound exactly like the textbook and still be wrong for this stem.
  5. Two absolutes worth having ready — "all enzymes are proteins" is false (ribozymes: RNase P, self-splicing introns, the spliceosome, the ribosomal peptidyl transferase centre); and a transition state is a maximum, an intermediate is a well, so an option calling ES or the acyl-enzyme a "transition state" is wrong on definition alone.
  6. Spend ten seconds here, not sixty — this question type is pure recognition. Bank the time for §§05–08, where the marks need arithmetic.
Go deeper · Arrhenius, Eyring, and where 5.71 kJ·mol⁻¹ comes from

Arrhenius wrote k = A e−Ea/RT empirically: a pre-exponential collision/orientation factor A times the Boltzmann fraction of molecules with at least Ea. Transition-state theory (Eyring) replaces A with something derivable: assume the transition state is in quasi-equilibrium with the reactants, and it decomposes at the universal frequency kBT/h ≈ 6 × 1012 s⁻¹ at 25 °C. Then k = (kBT/h) e−ΔG‡/RT, and because ΔG‡ = ΔH‡ − TΔS‡ you can separate an enthalpic contribution (bond strain, electrostatics) from an entropic one (holding two reactants still — the "proximity and orientation" strategy of § 02).

The number every calculation reduces to: take the ratio of two rates, k₂/k₁ = e−(ΔG‡₂ − ΔG‡₁)/RT. Rearranged, ΔΔG‡ = RT ln(k₂/k₁). With R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ and T = 298 K, RT = 2.48 kJ·mol⁻¹ and RT ln 10 = 5.71 kJ·mol⁻¹. Orotidine-5′-monophosphate decarboxylase, the current record holder, achieves about 1017-fold — that is 17 × 5.71 = 97 kJ·mol⁻¹ of barrier removed by a protein that uses no metal and no coenzyme at all.

The naming minefield examiners reuse

Synthase vs synthetase. A synthase joins things without nucleotide hydrolysis; a synthetase (ligase, class 6) requires ATP or another NTP. Citrate synthase needs no ATP; glutamine synthetase does. One consonant, two different requirements. Note what the rule does not say: only the synthetase half maps onto a single EC class. Synthases are spread across several — citrate synthase itself was moved out of the lyases (EC 4.1.3.7) and into the transferases (EC 2.3.3.1) in 2002, though plenty of textbooks still print the old number. Answer on the nucleotide, not on the class.

Kinase vs phosphorylase vs phosphatase. A kinase transfers phosphoryl from (transferase). A phosphorylase cleaves a bond using inorganic phosphate instead of water — glycogen phosphorylase is a glycosyltransferase, class 2, and notably does not use ATP. A phosphatase removes phosphate using water, so it is a hydrolase, class 3. Answering "kinase" for a phosphorolysis question is a standard trap.

Not all enzymes are proteins. Ribozymes catalyse real chemistry: RNase P (tRNA 5′ processing), self-splicing group I and II introns, the spliceosome, and — the important one — the peptidyl transferase centre of the ribosome, which is built entirely of 23S/28S rRNA. A question that says "all enzymes are proteins" is false as written.

Synthase or synthetase — which one burns a nucleotide

synthe-T-ase needs a Triphosphate

The extra T in synthetase is the T of NTP. A synthetase therefore couples joining to ATP (or another NTP) hydrolysis and is a ligase, class 6 — glutamine synthetase, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, pyruvate carboxylase. A synthase has no extra T and needs no nucleotide — citrate synthase. One consonant, two different requirements. This one is standard and you will meet it in 補習班 material and textbooks alike. Two cautions it does not cover: the ending is a naming convention, not a law — the note's own table puts ATP synthase in class 7 (translocases), and fatty-acid synthase is a multi-enzyme complex. Nor does "synthase" pin down an EC class: citrate synthase was moved out of the lyases (EC 4.1.3.7) into the transferases (EC 2.3.3.1) in 2002, and many textbooks still print the old number — so answer on whether a nucleotide is spent, never on the class. When a stem gives you the reaction rather than the name, always classify from the chemistry instead: water in → hydrolase; NTP consumed → ligase; electrons moved → oxidoreductase; same molecular formula out → isomerase.

§ 02

Binding energy and the catalytic strategies

The ideaWhere does an enzyme get the energy to lower a barrier? From sticking to its substrate. Every hydrogen bond, salt bridge and buried hydrophobic contact between enzyme and substrate releases a little free energy, and there are dozens of them. But here is the twist that makes the whole subject make sense: if the enzyme were shaped to grip the substrate perfectly, it would just hold it in a comfortable pit and nothing would happen. The site is instead shaped to grip the strained, half-reacted form. So the substrate is only partly comfortable, and it can become fully comfortable by distorting towards the product. The enzyme pulls the reaction over the hill by rewarding the climb.

Definition & principle

Binding energy (ΔGB)
The free energy released when the many weak, non-covalent interactions between enzyme and substrate form. It is the major source of catalytic free energy — the fund the enzyme spends on lowering ΔG‡.
the active site is complementary to the transition state, not to S the single most-tested statement in the whole of enzymology
Full definition, symbols and units
Active site
A small pocket or cleft, usually a few percent of the protein's volume, lined by residues that are distant in sequence but adjacent in space. Mostly excluded from bulk water, which raises the effective strength of electrostatic interactions.
Lock-and-key vs induced fit
Fischer's lock-and-key (1894) explains specificity but not catalysis. Koshland's induced fit (1958) has the enzyme change shape on binding — hexokinase closing over glucose is the textbook picture, and the closure is what excludes water so that ATP phosphorylates glucose rather than being hydrolysed.
Transition-state analogue
A stable molecule that mimics the geometry and charge of the transition state. Because the enzyme is built to bind that shape, such analogues bind 102–106 times more tightly than the substrate — the founding principle of rational drug design.

"A good enzyme binds its substrate tightly" — no

Very tight substrate binding deepens the ES well, which increases the climb from ES to the transition state and makes the enzyme slower. The quantity that must be maximised is the difference in binding between the transition state and the substrate, not the binding of the substrate. This is exactly why transition-state analogues are such potent inhibitors — they exploit the affinity the enzyme reserves for a species that normally exists for 10−13 s — and why Km values cluster in the range of physiological substrate concentrations rather than being as low as chemistry could make them.

Transition-state logic in the clinic

Statins resemble the mevaldyl-CoA intermediate/transition state of HMG-CoA reductase and bind with nanomolar Ki against a micromolar Km substrate. Oseltamivir and zanamivir mimic the planar sialosyl oxocarbenium transition state of influenza neuraminidase — the sugar in the drug is deliberately drawn flat. Saquinavir and the other HIV protease inhibitors carry a hydroxyethylene isostere in place of the scissile peptide bond: it looks like the tetrahedral intermediate but cannot be cleaved. Allopurinol is converted by its own target, xanthine oxidase, into oxypurinol, which then sits on the reduced molybdenum centre — catalysis that produces the inhibitor is called mechanism-based, and § 08 returns to it. The general moral: if you want a tight-binding inhibitor, do not copy the substrate; copy the transition state.

§ 03

Chymotrypsin and the catalytic triad

The ideaA peptide bond in neutral water survives for hundreds of years. To cut one in milliseconds you have two problems. First, you need something far more eager to attack than water is — and the only nucleophile conveniently to hand in a protein, a serine hydroxyl, is feeble. Second, the attack produces a short-lived, negatively charged, four-cornered arrangement at a carbon that would much rather be flat, and unless something grips that negative charge it will simply collapse back. The serine proteases solve both with two devices: a three-residue relay that strips the proton off the serine at the instant it is needed, and a pair of backbone N–H bonds pointing into exactly the place the new negative charge appears.

Definition & mechanism

Catalytic triad
Ser195 – His57 – Asp102 (chymotrypsin numbering, used for the whole family). His57 acts as a general base to deprotonate Ser195; Asp102 orients the imidazole and stabilises the resulting imidazolium by electrostatics. The three residues are far apart in sequence and adjacent in space.
Asp102 ⋯ His57 ⋯ HO–Ser195 → Ser195–O⁻ attacks the carbonyl C a proton relay that turns an ordinary alcohol into a genuine nucleophile
Full definition, symbols and units
Oxyanion hole
A small pocket formed by the backbone amide N–H groups of Gly193 and Ser195. It hydrogen-bonds the negatively charged oxygen of the tetrahedral intermediate. The substrate's flat, uncharged carbonyl cannot reach both N–H groups; the tetrahedral oxyanion can — textbook transition-state complementarity.
Acyl-enzyme intermediate
A covalent ester between the substrate's carbonyl carbon and Ser195 Oγ, formed after the amine leaving group departs. A true intermediate, not a transition state — it can be trapped and its accumulation is what produces burst kinetics.
Specificity pocket (S1)
The pocket that accepts the side chain immediately before the scissile bond. Its shape and charge, not the catalytic machinery, decide what each family member cuts.

The four numbers in the chymotrypsin active site, and the direction they run

102 holds 57 · 57 robs 195 · 193 + 195 catch the O⁻

Asp102 holds His57 — it orients the imidazole and stabilises the imidazolium, and it never takes a proton from His57. His57 robs the proton off Ser195, and the resulting alkoxide attacks the carbonyl carbon. The oxyanion hole is the backbone N–H of Gly193 and of Ser195 itself, and it grips the O⁻ that only the tetrahedral intermediate carries — the flat neutral carbonyl of the substrate cannot reach both. My own hook, not a circulating one. It fixes the residue numbers, the direction of the relay, and the fact that the oxyanion hole is main chain — the three things actually examined. It does not cover the S1 pocket (that is specificity, not catalysis), nor which half-reaction is rate-limiting (deacylation, for ester substrates). Note the numbers also give you the sequence order for free: 57 < 102 < 193 < 195 is N-terminus to C-terminus, while the chemistry runs 102 → 57 → 195, i.e. backwards.

The evidence: burst kinetics

The acyl-enzyme is not a story; it was measured. Give chymotrypsin the artificial substrate p-nitrophenyl acetate and watch the yellow p-nitrophenolate appear. You see a rapid burst — one mole of product per mole of enzyme, released within milliseconds — followed by a much slower steady rate. The interpretation is forced: acylation is fast and releases the first product (the alcohol/amine), leaving every active site loaded as the acyl-enzyme; the slow phase is deacylation, which is rate-limiting for this substrate. The size of the burst is a direct titration of active sites, which is one of the few ways to measure [E]t without assuming purity — and § 06 needs [E]t to get kcat.

Same machinery, different pocket

Trypsin, chymotrypsin and elastase are built on the same fold with the same triad in the same relative positions. They cut different bonds solely because the S1 pocket differs. This is the cleanest illustration in biochemistry of specificity and catalysis being separable properties.

Aspartate proteases: the same problem, no covalent intermediate

Not every protease goes through a covalent intermediate. Aspartate proteases carry two aspartate carboxylates facing each other across the site, one ionised and one protonated at the working pH. The ionised one acts as a general base and activates a water molecule directly; the protonated one protonates the carbonyl oxygen. Water attacks the carbonyl carbon in one step, giving a non-covalent gem-diol tetrahedral intermediate. There is no acyl-enzyme, and the mechanism is therefore called general acid–base rather than covalent catalysis.

The family is medically enormous. Pepsin works at pH 1.5–2, which is what a pair of carboxylates with widely split pKa values makes possible. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen and is the target of aliskiren. BACE1 makes the amyloid β peptide. And HIV-1 protease is a symmetric homodimer contributing one Asp25 each — a structural fact that is directly exam-relevant, because the drug that fills a two-fold symmetric site can be designed symmetric too, and because a homodimer cannot tolerate a mutation on one chain only.

Irreversible inhibitors of the serine machinery

DIPF and PMSF phosphorylate or sulfonylate Ser195 itself; the 1 : 1 stoichiometry was the original proof of the mechanism. Nerve agents and organophosphate insecticides do exactly the same to the equivalent serine of acetylcholinesterase, producing a cholinergic crisis (the SLUDGE / DUMBBELS picture, plus fasciculations and respiratory failure). Pralidoxime pulls the phosphoryl group off the serine — but only before "aging", the spontaneous loss of one alkyl group from the bound phosphate, after which the adduct is permanent. That is the pharmacological reason pralidoxime is time-critical, and it is a clean illustration of what "irreversible" really means: not that the chemistry is magic, but that the off-rate is effectively zero. Carbamates such as neostigmine and physostigmine carbamylate the same serine but hydrolyse off in minutes to hours — "pseudo-irreversible".

§ 04

Coenzymes: chemistry the protein cannot do

The ideaTwenty amino acid side chains are excellent at two things: pushing protons around, and holding molecules still in a particular orientation. They are hopeless at almost everything else. Nothing in a protein can carry an electron one at a time, carry a one-carbon fragment, stabilise a carbanion, or activate carbon dioxide. So enzymes hire specialists — small organic molecules, most of them things we must eat because we cannot make them. The division of labour is exact: the coenzyme supplies the chemistry, the protein supplies the speed and the choice of substrate. That is why the same coenzyme turns up in dozens of unrelated enzymes, and why a single vitamin deficiency knocks out a whole set of reactions at once.

How this is actually asked

Not as "what is the coenzyme for X", but as a chemistry question: which cofactor is required by an enzyme that removes CO₂ from an α-keto acid? (TPP). Which is needed to add CO₂ at the cost of ATP? NADH and NADPH are not interchangeable

They differ by one phosphate on the 2′-OH of the adenosine ribose — no difference in redox chemistry whatsoever. The difference is entirely one of recognition: enzymes read the phosphate and so the cell can hold two separate pools at two separate redox poises. The NAD pool is kept mostly oxidised (high NAD⁺/NADH) to drive catabolic dehydrogenations and feed the respiratory chain; the NADP pool is kept mostly reduced (high /NADP⁺) to drive reductive biosynthesis — fatty acids, cholesterol, deoxyribonucleotides — and to reduce glutathione. It is the same molecule doing two jobs that must not be allowed to short-circuit, and the phosphate is the tag that keeps them apart. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency is the clinical demonstration: no NADPH, no reduced glutathione, haemolysis under oxidant stress.

§ 05

Michaelis–Menten, derived properly

The ideaFix the amount of enzyme and keep adding substrate while you measure how fast product appears. At first the rate rises in step with what you add — the enzyme spends most of its time waiting for a substrate molecule to arrive. Eventually adding more does nothing at all, because every active site is already occupied the instant it becomes free and the only thing left limiting the rate is how long one cycle of chemistry takes. The curve that connects those two regimes is a rectangular hyperbola, and the two numbers that describe it are the substrate concentration at which the enzyme is working at half speed, and the speed it approaches but never reaches. Everything else in this note is about reading those two numbers off real data.

Definition & formula

The rate measured at the very start of the reaction — conventionally before ~5–10 % of the substrate is consumed. This matters: it keeps [S] effectively constant, keeps [P] ≈ 0 so the reverse reaction is negligible, and avoids product inhibition. Every constant below is defined for v0.

The derivation, in five lines

Do this once with a pen. It takes two minutes and it immunises you against every Km misconception in § 08.

  1. Product only comes from ES, so v₀ = k₂[ES]. The whole job is finding [ES].
  2. Steady state. ES is formed at k₁[E][S] and lost at k₋₁[ES] + k₂[ES]. Setting formation equal to loss: k₁[E][S] = (k₋₁ + k₂)[ES].
  3. Conservation. Free enzyme is what is left over: [E] = [E]t − [ES]. Substituting, k₁([E]t − [ES])[S] = (k₋₁ + k₂)[ES].
  4. Divide through by k₁, collect the [ES] terms and define Km ≡ (k₋₁ + k₂)/k₁: [E]t[S] = [ES](Km + [S]), so [ES] = [E]t[S] / (Km + [S]).
  5. Put that back in line 1 and notice that at saturation every enzyme is ES, so Vmax = k₂[E]t. Result: v₀ = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]). ∎

Two checks that make the equation intuitive. Put [S] = Km and you get Vmax/2 — that is the operational definition. Let [S] → ∞ and the Km in the denominator becomes negligible, so v₀ → Vmax — asymptotically, never actually reaching it, which is the practical reason § 07 exists.

What Km is, and the three things it is not

Km is a concentration. It tells you the substrate concentration at which the enzyme is half-busy, and therefore where on the curve the enzyme is operating in a real cell. An enzyme whose Km sits far above physiological [S] responds proportionally to substrate supply; one whose Km sits far below is running flat out and is deaf to supply.

Km is not Kd. From the definitions, Km = (k₋₁ + k₂)/k₁ while Kd = k₋₁/k₁. So Km ≥ Kd, always, and they coincide only in the rapid-equilibrium limit k₂ ≪ k₋₁ — the case Michaelis and Menten originally assumed, before Briggs and Haldane generalised it. For a fast enzyme where k₂ is comparable to or larger than k₋₁, Km can exceed Kd by orders of magnitude, and quoting Km as "the affinity" is then simply wrong.

Km is not a property of the enzyme alone. It is a property of an enzyme–substrate pair under stated conditions: change the substrate and Km changes (hexokinase has Km ≈ 0.05 mM for glucose but ≈ 1.5 mM for fructose); change pH, ionic strength or temperature and it changes again. A Km quoted without its conditions is incomplete.

The three Km traps that cost marks

1. "Low Km means high affinity." True only under rapid equilibrium. The safe phrasing, and the one exam answers use, is "a lower Km means half-maximal velocity is reached at a lower substrate concentration". If a question gives you k₁, k₋₁ and k₂ and asks for affinity, it is testing precisely this.

2. "Km depends on enzyme concentration." It does not. Double [E]t and Vmax doubles while Km is unchanged — the curve gets taller, not wider. This is the reason Vmax is a useless way to compare two enzymes and kcat (§ 06) is the right one.

3. "Vmax is reached at high [S]." It is approached. Even at 99 Km you are only at 99 %. This is not pedantry: it is why Vmax cannot be read reliably off a hyperbola and why the linearisations of § 07 were invented.

Two Km values that are worth knowing as facts

Hexokinase vs glucokinase. Hexokinase (I–III, all tissues) has Km ≈ 0.05 mM for glucose and is inhibited by its product glucose-6-phosphate — it is a high-affinity scavenger, saturated at any normal blood glucose, guaranteeing that the brain and red cells get theirs. Glucokinase (hexokinase IV, liver and pancreatic β-cell) has S0.5 ≈ 10 mM, is mildly sigmoid, and is not inhibited by — it deliberately works on the steep part of its curve so that hepatic glucose uptake rises with portal glucose after a meal, and so that the β-cell can use it as a glucose sensor. MODY 2 is a glucokinase mutation, and the phenotype — a resetting of the glucose set-point — falls straight out of the kinetics. Alcohol dehydrogenase is the other one: it has a lower Km for ethanol than for methanol, which is the entire logic of treating methanol poisoning with ethanol, and of the cleaner modern alternative, the competitive inhibitor fomepizole (§ 08).

Exam technique · Michaelis–Menten numbers, and fencing the answer first

  1. Decide which of the two directions the stem runs — given [S] and asked for v, substitute into v = Vmax[S]/(Km+[S]). Given a fraction f of Vmax and asked for [S], use [S] = Km·f/(1−f). There is no third move on this topic.
  2. Fence the answer before computing anything — v < Vmax always, and v > Vmax/2 exactly when [S] > Km. Comparing [S] with Km is one glance and it typically kills two or three of the five options with no arithmetic at all — which is the entire game under negative marking.
  3. Then the checkpoints, which you derive rather than memorise — at [S] = nKm, v/Vmax = n/(n+1). So ½Km → ⅓ · Km → ½ · 2Km → ⅔ · 3Km → ¾ · 4Km → 80 % · 9Km → 90 % · 99Km → 99 %. There is no mnemonic for that list and you do not need one: n/(n+1) generates all of it, and inverting it gives f/(1−f) for the other direction.
  4. Unit filter — v carries Vmax's units; [S] and Km are concentrations. An option offering a concentration when the stem asked for a rate, or the reverse, is a free elimination.
  5. The free arithmetic check — put [S] = Km through your own working. It must give exactly Vmax/2. If it does not, the slip is in your algebra, and you have found it in five seconds instead of losing the mark.
  6. Read what was asked, not what is familiar — "half-maximal velocity" happens at Km and nowhere else. The standard distractor is Vmax/2 offered for any [S] above Km, which is a true-sounding sentence answering a question nobody asked.
Worked example 1 — substitution both ways, and the price of saturation

An enzyme has Vmax = 120 µmol·min⁻¹·mg⁻¹ and Km = 4.0 mM. (a) Find v₀ at [S] = 1.0, 4.0 and 12 mM. (b) What [S] gives 80 % of Vmax? (c) What [S] gives 90 %? (d) Comment on the ratio.

  1. (a) v₀ = 120 × 1.0/(4.0 + 1.0) = 120/5 = 24 µmol·min⁻¹·mg⁻¹.
  2. v₀ = 120 × 4.0/(4.0 + 4.0) = 60 — as it must be, since [S] = Km is by definition half-maximal. Use this as a free arithmetic check on every such question.
  3. v₀ = 120 × 12/(4.0 + 12) = 1440/16 = 90 µmol·min⁻¹·mg⁻¹, which is 75 % of Vmax at three times Km.
  4. (b) Invert the equation. If v = f·Vmax then f = [S]/(Km + [S]), so [S] = Km·f/(1 − f). With f = 0.80: [S] = 4.0 × 0.8/0.2 = 4.0 × 4 = 16 mM — i.e. 4 Km.
  5. (c) With f = 0.90: [S] = 4.0 × 0.9/0.1 = 4.0 × 9 = 36 mM — i.e. 9 Km.
  6. (d) Going from 80 % to 90 % of Vmax required more than doubling the substrate. Going from 10 % (0.111 Km) to 90 % (9 Km) requires an 81-fold rise. Remember that 81; § 10 uses it as the baseline against which cooperative enzymes are judged.

§ 06

kcat, kcat/Km and catalytic perfection

The ideaThere are two quite different questions you can ask about how good an enzyme is, and they have two different answers. When it never has to wait for substrate, how many molecules can one active site process per second? That is the turnover number. When substrate is scarce — which is the normal state of a cell — how efficiently does the enzyme find it and convert it? That is a completely separate question, and its answer combines how fast the chemistry is with how well the enzyme captures what little there is. Most people learn the first and assume it answers the second. Exam questions are built on that gap.

Definition & formula

Turnover number kcat
The number of substrate molecules converted to product per active site per unit time when the enzyme is fully saturated. Units s⁻¹ (a first-order rate constant). For the simple scheme of § 05, kcat = k₂; for multi-step mechanisms it is a composite of all the first-order steps after substrate binding.
kcat = Vmax[E]t you cannot get kcat without knowing how much enzyme is present — that is the whole difficulty
Full definition, symbols and units
Specificity constant kcat/Km
The apparent second-order rate constant for the whole reaction E + S → E + P when [S] ≪ Km. Units M⁻¹·s⁻¹. It is the correct single number for ranking substrates and for comparing enzymes.
Diffusion limit
kcat/Km can never exceed k₁, the rate at which E and S can meet — about 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹·s⁻¹ in water. Enzymes at that ceiling are said to have achieved catalytic perfection: chemistry is no longer limiting, encounter is.
Specific activity
µmol of product per minute per milligram of total protein. A purification metric, not a kinetic constant: it rises during purification as contaminating protein is removed. Convert to kcat only if you know the molecular weight and the enzyme is pure.

The derivation of the specificity constant takes one line and is worth seeing, because it explains why the ratio, and not either constant alone, is the meaningful number. Start from Michaelis–Menten and let [S] ≪ Km, so the denominator is essentially Km:

v₀ = Vmax[S]Km + [S] ≈ Vmax[S]Km = kcatKm [E]t[S] at low [S] almost all the enzyme is free, so [E] ≈ [E]t

The rate is now first order in enzyme and first order in substrate, and the constant of proportionality is kcat/Km. Give an enzyme two substrates at once and each is converted at its own such rate, so the ratio of the two rates is the ratio of their specificity constants weighted by concentration. That is the operational meaning of "this enzyme prefers A over B", and it is why an enzyme can prefer a substrate with the worse kcat.

Which constant answers which question

kcat — "how fast at saturation?" Compare two enzymes' raw chemical speed. Km — "at what concentration does it engage?" Predict whether a physiological change in [S] will change flux. kcat/Km — "which substrate wins when both are present at low concentration?" and "how close to perfect is this enzyme?" Vmax — answers nothing about the enzyme, only about this particular assay tube, because it scales with [E]t. A question that asks you to compare two enzymes and gives you Vmax values is either giving you [E]t as well, or testing whether you notice.

Exam technique · micrograms of protein → kcat, and the traps in the units

  1. Moles of enzyme first — mass ÷ Mr. Stay in moles; do not convert to a concentration, because the assay volume cancels on the next line and converting only creates a chance to slip a factor of 1000.
  2. kcat = (mol product per unit time) ÷ (mol enzyme) — that is Vmax/[E]t with the volume already divided out. Divide by the number of active sites per molecule only if the stem says there is more than one.
  3. Divide by 60 — assay rates come per minute, kcat is quoted in s⁻¹. If two of the five options differ by exactly 60, that ratio is the question, and the units printed in the option are what catch it. A third option 60 the other way is the same trap doubled.
  4. For kcat/Km, put Km into molar first — 25 µM is 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ M. The answer must land in M⁻¹·s⁻¹, and real values run about 10³–10⁹; if your exponent is outside that you have dropped a thousand somewhere.
  5. Ceiling check, worth a free elimination — nothing can exceed the diffusion limit of roughly 10⁹ M⁻¹·s⁻¹, so any option above it is impossible. Conversely, an answer landing at 10⁸–10⁹ is the examiner setting up "is this enzyme catalytically perfect?" — and the answer is yes.
  6. Check which constant the question wants — "how fast when saturated" is kcat; "which substrate wins when both are scarce" is kcat/Km; "does flux respond to a change in supply" is Km. Comparing two enzymes by Vmax is meaningless unless [E]t is given. The distractors here are usually correct arithmetic for the other constant, so they survive every check except reading the stem again.
Worked example 2 — from micrograms of protein to kcat/Km

A 1.0 mL assay contains 2.0 µg of a pure enzyme of Mr 50 000, with one active site per molecule. At saturating substrate the assay produces 6.0 µmol of product per minute. Km for this substrate is 25 µM. Find [E]t, kcat and kcat/Km, and say whether this enzyme is close to catalytic perfection.

  1. Moles of enzyme: 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ g ÷ 5.0 × 10⁴ g·mol⁻¹ = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ mol = 40 pmol.
  2. In 1.0 mL = 1.0 × 10⁻³ L, so [E]t = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ / 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ M = 40 nM. Note how little enzyme this is — nanomolar against a micromolar-to-millimolar substrate, which is exactly the condition the steady-state derivation assumed.
  3. The measured rate at saturation is Vmax: 6.0 µmol·min⁻¹ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol·min⁻¹.
  4. kcat = Vmax/[E]t. Work in moles, not concentrations, and the volume cancels: 6.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol·min⁻¹ ÷ 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ mol = 1.5 × 10⁵ min⁻¹.
  5. Convert to the conventional unit: 1.5 × 10⁵ ÷ 60 = 2.5 × 10³ s⁻¹. Each active site turns over 2500 times a second — about 2–3 molecules every millisecond.
  6. kcat/Km = 2.5 × 10³ s⁻¹ ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ M = 1.0 × 10⁸ M⁻¹·s⁻¹.
  7. That sits at the bottom of the diffusion-limited range of 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹·s⁻¹, so yes — this enzyme is essentially perfect. Improving the chemistry further would gain nothing, because substrate cannot be delivered any faster.

§ 07

Straightening the hyperbola: three plots

The ideaA hyperbola creeps towards its ceiling and never touches it, so if you try to read that ceiling off a curve by eye you will guess low — and since the other constant is defined as the concentration giving half the ceiling, a bad guess corrupts both numbers. The fix is algebra: rearrange the equation into the form y = mx + c so the data lie on a straight line and the constants become intercepts, which the eye is good at. There are three ways to do the rearrangement. They contain identical information and differ only in where they put the experimental error — and, far more usefully for exams, in the shape they make when an inhibitor is present.

Definition & formulae

Lineweaver–Burk (double-reciprocal) plot
Take reciprocals of both sides of the Michaelis–Menten equation. Plot 1/v₀ against 1/[S]. The most-drawn plot in biochemistry and the one every inhibition question uses.
1v0 = KmVmax · 1[S] + 1Vmax y-intercept 1/Vmax, x-intercept −1/Km, slope Km/Vmax
Full definition, symbols and units
Eadie–Hofstee plot
Multiply out and rearrange to v₀ = −Km(v₀/[S]) + Vmax. Plot v₀ against v₀/[S]. Deviations from Michaelis–Menten behaviour — cooperativity, two binding sites — show up as visible curvature far more clearly than on a Lineweaver–Burk plot.
Hanes–Woolf plot
Multiply the reciprocal form through by [S]: [S]/v₀ = (1/Vmax)[S] + Km/Vmax. Plot [S]/v₀ against [S]. Statistically the best of the three, because it does not take the reciprocal of the measured velocity on its own.

The intercept mix-ups, in order of how often they happen

On a Lineweaver–Burk plot the y-intercept is 1/Vmax, not Vmax; a line that intercepts higher therefore means a smaller Vmax. The x-intercept is −1/Km — negative, and reciprocal, so a point further left means a smaller Km. On an Eadie–Hofstee plot the slope is −Km, so a steeper downward slope means a larger Km and the y-intercept is Vmax itself, not its reciprocal. On Hanes–Woolf the x-intercept is −Km directly, no reciprocal at all. If you remember only one thing: reciprocal plots invert the sense of "bigger".

Write them down; it takes four seconds and it prevents the only error that matters here.
  2. Direction filter, before any arithmetic — on a reciprocal plot "bigger" is inverted. A higher LB y-intercept means a smaller Vmax; a point further left on the LB x-axis means a smaller Km.   3. Free cross-check: |slope| = y-intercept ÷ |x-intercept| — true of any straight line whatever, so it costs nothing and catches a mis-remembered intercept immediately. On the worked example below: 0.0125 ÷ 0.50 = 0.025 = Km/Vmax ✓.

  5. Where the time goes — Hanes–Woolf and Eadie–Hofstee are the ones worth rehearsing, precisely because Lineweaver–Burk is the only one most candidates have drawn. A question set on the least-drawn plot is the cheapest mark in § 07 for anyone who spent ten minutes on it.

§ 08

Inhibition: five patterns, one equation

The ideaMost drugs are enzyme inhibitors, and the question the exam asks about them is always the same one: where does the inhibitor bind? There are only three possibilities — it binds the free enzyme, it binds the enzyme–substrate complex, or it binds both — and each possibility leaves a distinct fingerprint on the two numbers you can measure. If the inhibitor competes for the same site as the substrate, then piling on more substrate crowds it out, and the enzyme can still reach full speed; it just needs more substrate to get there. If instead the inhibitor only grabs the enzyme after the substrate is already bound, adding substrate makes things worse, not better. Everything in this section is that idea, made quantitative.

Definition & general formula

Reversible inhibitor
Binds non-covalently and dissociates; activity returns on dialysis or dilution. Characterised by a dissociation constant: KI = [E][I]/[EI] for binding to free enzyme, K′I = [ES][I]/[ESI] for binding to the ES complex. A smaller K means tighter binding.

Comparing the general equation with the standard form gives Vmaxapp = Vmax/α′ and Kmapp = Km·α/α′. Every entry in the table below is these two expressions with particular values of α and α′ — you do not have to memorise the table if you can reconstruct it.

Forms a covalent bond, or binds so tightly that the off-rate is effectively zero. It does not have a KI in the usual sense; it is characterised by a rate of inactivation, and the extent of inhibition increases with time of exposure.

Work through the four reversible cases by asking only "which species does the inhibitor bind?", and let the two expressions for the apparent constants do the rest.

Competitive（競爭型抑制）— binds free E only

The inhibitor occupies the substrate site, so E and I compete. α > 1, α′ = 1. Then Vmaxapp = Vmax — unchanged — and Kmapp = αKm, raised. The reason Vmax survives is the important part: at sufficiently high [S], substrate outnumbers inhibitor at the site by any margin you like, so essentially all the enzyme is in ES and the full rate is recovered. Competitive inhibition is surmountable. Km rises because you now need more substrate to achieve any given occupancy. Note that kcat is untouched but kcat/Km falls by α — catalysis is unimpaired, capture is impaired.

Uncompetitive（反競爭型抑制）— binds ES only

The inhibitor's site only exists once substrate has bound and the enzyme has changed shape. α = 1, α′ > 1. Both apparent constants fall by the same factor: Vmax/α′ and Km/α′. Vmax falls because ESI is a dead end. Km falls — apparently improving the "affinity" — because removing ES from the equilibrium pulls more E and S together, a straightforward Le Chatelier effect. Since both fall equally, kcat/Km is unchanged, and on a Lineweaver–Burk plot the slope αKm/Vmax is unchanged too: the lines are parallel. That parallel-line signature is the single most reliable identification in the whole topic.

The consequence that matters clinically: because the inhibitor needs ES, raising the substrate concentration makes inhibition worse. An uncompetitive inhibitor cannot be out-competed. It is rare — but lithium acting on inositol monophosphatase is a genuine example, and its uncompetitive character is exactly why it acts preferentially on over-active signalling pathways where the substrate has accumulated.

Mixed and pure non-competitive（非競爭型抑制）— binds both

The inhibitor binds at a separate site available on both E and ES, but usually not with equal affinity, so α ≠ α′ and both exceed 1. Vmax always falls; Km rises if α > α′ (inhibitor prefers free enzyme) and falls if α′ > α. The special case α = α′ — equal affinity for E and ES, meaning the inhibitor's binding is entirely indifferent to whether substrate is present — is pure non-competitive inhibition: Vmax falls by α, Km is unchanged. It behaves as if the inhibitor simply deleted a fraction of the enzyme molecules from the tube, which is precisely what it does. Heavy metals acting on essential thiols are the classic example; so, functionally, is an irreversible inhibitor.

Where the two Lineweaver–Burk lines meet

Competitive keeps the Ceiling · Non-competitive: No change in Km · Uncompetitive: Untouched slope · Mixed meets in the Middle

The first three pairs each name the one quantity that type of inhibitor leaves alone — and what is left alone is precisely what the two lines then share on the plot; mixed is the case where nothing is. Competitive keeps the Ceiling — Vmax is unchanged, so both lines run through the same 1/Vmax and meet on the y-axis. Non-competitive, No change in Km — so they share −1/Km and meet on the x-axis. Uncompetitive, Untouched slope — Vmax and Km both fall by the same α′, so Km/Vmax is unaltered: the lines are parallel and never meet. Mixed meets in the Middle — nothing is shared, so the crossing point sits off both axes, in the second quadrant, above the x-axis when α > α′ and below it when α′ > α. The N clause ("non-competitive, no change in Km") is the standard English hook; the other three are my own, built to the same pattern. That pattern is deliberate, because un- versus non- is the single commonest confusion on this topic and the two prefixes look almost identical on the page: attach each one to a word that starts with its own letter — Untouched slope, No change in Km — and the prefix stops being an arbitrary label. The hook encodes only the geometry. What happens to Vmax and Km needs no mnemonic: it drops out of Vmaxapp = Vmax/α′ and Kmapp = Kmα/α′ with α = 1 or α′ = 1 or α = α′ substituted in, which is two lines of algebra and cannot decay the way a memorised table does. Also not covered: an irreversible inhibitor, which produces the pure non-competitive pattern without being one.

Three confusions the examiners rely on

"Non-competitive" does not mean "binds elsewhere and is therefore weak." It means the inhibitor binds E and ES with equal affinity, so substrate presence is irrelevant to it. Nor does "competitive" require binding at the active site — a competitive inhibitor merely has to make S-binding and I-binding mutually exclusive, which an allosteric site can also achieve.

Uncompetitive inhibition lowers Km, and that is not a mistake in the table. Pulling ES out of the equilibrium drags E + S towards ES, so half-maximal velocity is reached at a lower [S] — even though the enzyme is less active overall. If a question offers you "Km decreased and Vmax decreased" and you reject it as impossible, you have lost the mark.

An irreversible inhibitor reads out as pure non-competitive. It removes enzyme molecules permanently from the population; the survivors have a perfectly normal Km, and there are simply fewer of them, so Vmax falls. Distinguish it from a genuine reversible non-competitive inhibitor by two things a kinetic plot cannot show you: activity does not return on dialysis or dilution, and inhibition deepens with pre-incubation time. Aspirin on cyclo-oxygenase is the standard vignette.

Exam technique · the inhibition data stem, in four lines and forty seconds

  1. α′ first, from Vmax — α′ = Vmax/Vmaxapp. If Vmax is unchanged, α′ = 1 and you are already most of the way there.
  2. α second, from Km — α = (Kmapp/Km) × α′. Note the order: you cannot get α without α′, which is why Vmax is always the first line.
  3. Read the type straight off the pair — (α>1, α′=1) competitive · (α=1, α′>1) uncompetitive · (α=α′>1) pure non-competitive · (both >1, unequal) mixed. Four cases, no table.
  4. If a constant is wanted, it is one line — KI = [I]/(α − 1) and K′I = [I]/(α′ − 1). Examiners attach this as a second mark precisely because most candidates stop at naming the type.
  5. Recognition shortcuts that skip steps 1–3 entirely — "Vmax unchanged" ⇒ competitive, stop reading. "Parallel lines" or "both fell by the same factor" ⇒ uncompetitive. "Vmax falls, Km unchanged" ⇒ pure non-competitive or irreversible, and the words pre-incubation, dialysis, washed out, covalent, acetylates, time-dependent settle it as the latter every time.
  6. Three eliminations that need no solving — since Vmaxapp = Vmax/α′ with α′ ≥ 1, no reversible inhibitor can ever raise Vmax or kcat; a competitive inhibitor can never lower Vmax; and uncompetitive is the only reversible type that leaves kcat/Km unchanged, because the specificity constant is divided by α alone. Any option breaking one of those is gone, and one elimination is all the −0.5 marking needs.
  7. The one that feels wrong and is right — "Km decreased and Vmax decreased" is uncompetitive inhibition, not a misprint. Do not eliminate it on instinct; it is placed there to be rejected.

Irreversible and mechanism-based inhibitors

An irreversible inhibitor forms a covalent adduct. Two sub-types are worth separating. A group-specific reagent attacks a chemical class wherever it finds it — DIPF on any reactive serine, iodoacetamide on any cysteine — and is a laboratory tool rather than a drug. A mechanism-based or suicide inhibitor is far more elegant: it is an unreactive compound that the target enzyme itself converts into a reactive species, which then kills the enzyme that made it. Selectivity is therefore guaranteed by the catalytic step, because only the intended enzyme can do the activating chemistry.

Three examples worth knowing precisely. Penicillin mimics the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus that the bacterial transpeptidase normally cleaves; the enzyme's serine attacks the β-lactam ring, opens it, and is left as a stable penicilloyl-enzyme. 5-Fluorouracil is converted in the cell to FdUMP, which lets thymidylate synthase begin its normal chemistry and then traps it in a covalent ternary complex with N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-, because the fluorine cannot be abstracted as the hydrogen would have been. Allopurinol is oxidised by xanthine oxidase to oxypurinol, which then binds the reduced molybdenum centre and stays there.

§ 09

Two substrates, pH and temperature

The ideaAlmost no enzyme has just one substrate. Most take two, and there is a genuine mechanistic fork: either both substrates have to be sitting on the enzyme at the same time, so that a group can pass directly from one to the other, or the enzyme takes a piece off the first substrate, lets the remainder go, and then hands that piece to the second. The second route means the enzyme itself is chemically different in between — it is temporarily carrying a load — and that is visible in the kinetics. Separately, two environmental variables change the rate for reasons that look similar and are not: pH changes which groups are protonated, while temperature changes both the speed of the chemistry and the survival of the protein.

Definition & the two mechanisms

Sequential (single-displacement) mechanism
Both substrates bind before any product leaves, forming a ternary complex. Binding may be ordered (an obligatory sequence — most NAD⁺-dependent dehydrogenases bind the coenzyme first) or random (either order — creatine kinase, glycogen phosphorylase).
ping-pong ⇒ parallel lines on a Lineweaver–Burk family; sequential ⇒ lines intersect plot 1/v against 1/[S₁] at several fixed [S₂] — the shape names the mechanism
Full definition, symbols and units
Ping-pong (double-displacement) mechanism
The first substrate binds, transfers a group to the enzyme, and the first product leaves. Only then does the second substrate bind and collect the group. No ternary complex ever exists, and the enzyme passes through a substituted (modified) form, usually via a prosthetic group.
pH optimum
The pH at which activity is maximal. The curve is usually bell-shaped because two ionisable groups must be in opposite protonation states — one deprotonated to act as a base, one protonated to act as an acid. The optimum is therefore a property of the mechanism, not of protein stability, over most of the range.
Temperature "optimum"
Not a real optimum. It is the crossing point of two competing effects: the Arrhenius increase in rate with temperature (Q₁₀ ≈ 2, so roughly a doubling per 10 °C) and the onset of thermal denaturation. Shift the incubation time and the apparent optimum moves — which is the giveaway that it is not a constant.

The kinetic signature has a simple origin. In a ping-pong mechanism, changing the concentration of the second substrate changes how quickly the substituted enzyme E′ is returned to E, and in the double-reciprocal form that appears purely as a change in the intercept — the slope term has no [B] in it, because A and B never meet on the enzyme. Hence parallel lines. In a sequential mechanism both substrates occupy the same complex, the cross-term survives, and the slope changes with [B] as well: the lines converge.

This one circulates widely and you will meet it elsewhere. Two limits the note itself supplies. First, parallel lines are suggestive, not conclusive: a sequential mechanism with one very small K value fakes them, and the definitive test is isotope exchange between A and P in the total absence of B. Second — and this is the real exam trap — uncompetitive inhibition also gives parallel Lineweaver–Burk lines (§ 08). Tell the two apart by what was varied: a family of lines at several fixed concentrations of a second substrate is a mechanism question; two lines differing by the presence of an inhibitor is an inhibition question.

The aminotransferases are worth carrying as the concrete case, because they also carry a large amount of clinical weight. In the first half-reaction an amino acid donates its amino group to enzyme-bound , converting it to pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP) and releasing an α-keto acid. In the second, PMP hands the amino group to a different α-keto acid, regenerating PLP. The amino group is passed through the enzyme. That is what ping-pong means, and it explains why, in B₆ deficiency, serum AST and ALT activities read falsely low unless the assay is supplemented with PLP — the apoenzyme is there, but empty, and only added coenzyme reconstitutes the holoenzyme the assay is trying to measure.

pH: mechanism, not denaturation

The bell-shaped pH profile of chymotrypsin has its ascending limb at pKa ≈ 6.8 (His57 must be unprotonated to act as a general base) and its descending limb near pKa ≈ 9 (the Ile16–Asp194 salt bridge that holds the active conformation together must stay ionised). Neither limb is denaturation. Contrast pepsin, optimal at pH 1.5–2, where the mechanism requires one active-site aspartate protonated and one ionised, and alkaline phosphatase, optimal near pH 10. The exam version of this: an enzyme's activity falls at low pH; is the enzyme denatured? Usually not — test by returning the pH to neutral. If activity comes back, it was ionisation; if not, it was denaturation.

Go deeper · Cleland notation, and reading a bisubstrate plot properly

Cleland's shorthand draws the enzyme as a horizontal line, with substrates arriving on downward arrows and products leaving on upward arrows, and the enzyme's successive forms named underneath. An ordered bi-bi mechanism reads: A binds, B binds, chemistry, P leaves, Q leaves. A ping-pong bi-bi reads: A binds, P leaves (leaving E′), B binds, Q leaves. "Bi-bi" means two substrates and two products; you will also meet uni-bi (one in, two out — as in a lyase) and ter-bi.

To read the experiment: hold [B] at several fixed values and vary [A], then plot 1/v against 1/[A] once for each [B]. In double-reciprocal form a ping-pong mechanism gives 1/v = (KA/Vmax)(1/[A]) + (1/Vmax)(1 + KB/[B]) — [B] appears only in the constant term, so changing it slides the line up and down without tilting it. A sequential mechanism adds a term in 1/([A][B]), which lives in the slope, so the lines fan out and intersect.

One practical caution: parallel lines are also produced by a sequential mechanism in which one K value is very small, so a single experiment showing parallel lines is suggestive rather than conclusive. The definitive test is an isotope-exchange experiment — a ping-pong enzyme will exchange label between A and P in the complete absence of B, because the first half-reaction is genuinely independent. A sequential enzyme cannot.

§ 10

Allostery and cooperativity

The ideaA Michaelis–Menten enzyme makes a terrible switch. To take it from a tenth of full speed to nine tenths you have to raise the substrate concentration eighty-one fold — and no cell can swing a metabolite over that range. The way out is to build the enzyme from several subunits and let them talk to each other, so that the first substrate molecule to bind makes the next one easier to bind. The response curve then stops being a hyperbola and becomes an S-shape: sluggish at low concentrations, then suddenly steep over a narrow band, then saturated. That steep band is a switch. And once subunits can influence one another, other molecules that are not the substrate at all can bind somewhere else and shift the whole curve sideways — which is how a pathway tells its own first enzyme to slow down.

Allosteric enzyme（異位調節酵素）
An enzyme whose activity is changed by a ligand binding at a site other than the active site, acting through a conformational change. Almost always multi-subunit. Its kinetics are not Michaelis–Menten, so the constant that replaces Km must be called K0.5 or S0.5.

Heterotropic: the modulator is a different molecule, and it may activate or inhibit.

Telling the two apart in one line

MWC = "all or none" — the whole molecule flips together, so no mixed states and no negative cooperativity. KNF = "one at a time" — induced fit propagating subunit by subunit, so mixed states are allowed and negative cooperativity is possible. If a question mentions negative cooperativity, the answer cannot be the concerted model.

The three canonical systems

Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) catalyses the committed step of pyrimidine synthesis in bacteria — in mammals the regulated step is carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase II, and ATCase is one domain of the multifunctional CAD protein. It is c₆r₆: six catalytic subunits in two trimers, six regulatory subunits in three dimers. CTP, the end product, binds the regulatory subunits and stabilises T — classic feedback inhibition. ATP binds the same sites and stabilises R, which balances purine against pyrimidine supply. Treat the enzyme with a mercurial to strip off the regulatory subunits and you get an enzyme that is more active, hyperbolic rather than sigmoid, and completely insensitive to CTP — the cleanest demonstration anywhere that regulation and catalysis are separable functions of separate protein parts.

Phosphofructokinase-1 is the one to know for metabolism. ATP is both its substrate (at the catalytic site) and its allosteric inhibitor (at a separate, lower-affinity site) — a fact that looks paradoxical until you notice the two sites are different. and ADP relieve that inhibition, citrate reinforces it, and is the most powerful activator of all. This is how a cell reads its own energy charge and its own supply of biosynthetic precursors, and converts both into one flux decision.

Haemoglobin is not an enzyme but is the reference implementation of everything above: four sites, nH ≈ 2.8, T ⇌ R, with heterotropic modulation by H⁺ and CO₂ (the Bohr effect) and by 2,3-BPG. Myoglobin, a monomer, gives nH = 1 and a hyperbola — the control experiment.

Feedback inhibition

The organising principle of metabolic regulation: the end product of a pathway inhibits the enzyme that catalyses the committed step — the first step that is both irreversible under cellular conditions and unique to that pathway. Inhibiting later would waste the intermediates already made; inhibiting a reversible or shared step would disturb other pathways. Examples worth having: CTP on ATCase; isoleucine on threonine deaminase (the original 1956 observation); cholesterol on HMG-CoA reductase — by three separate mechanisms, allosteric-like inhibition, reduced transcription via SREBP, and accelerated proteolytic degradation of the enzyme; haem on ALA synthase; and purine nucleotides on PRPP amidotransferase.

Sigmoid does not mean inhibited

Two traps sit next to each other. First, an allosteric enzyme's constants are K0.5 and nH, not Km — writing Km for a sigmoid enzyme is a definitional error, because Km is derived from a scheme this enzyme does not obey, and its Lineweaver–Burk plot is curved, not straight. Second, a heterotropic inhibitor of a K-system raises K0.5 without touching Vmax, which looks exactly like competitive inhibition on a superficial reading — but the inhibitor is not competing for the active site at all, and its curve is sigmoid. The discriminator is the shape of the uninhibited curve. And note the third member of the family: the committed step is not necessarily the first step of a pathway, only the first irreversible one that is unique to it.

Exam technique · Hill questions, and four things that kill options outright

  1. One relation answers every numerical version — [S]90/[S]10 = 811/n. Given the span, take n = log 81 ÷ log(span); given n, the span is 811/n. Nothing else is examined here.
  2. Three values cover almost every paper — n = 1 → 81× · n = 2 → 9× · n = 4 → 3×. They are exact because 81 = 3⁴, which is also why examiners avoid n = 3 (it gives an ugly 4.3×). No mnemonic is warranted for these: they are one square root apart, and doubling n square-roots the span.
  3. nH ≤ the number of binding sites — an option quoting a Hill coefficient larger than the number of subunits is impossible, and haemoglobin's four sites give ≈ 2.8, never 4.0. That kills an option on most cooperativity stems without any calculation.
  4. Word triggers — "sigmoid" ⇒ the constant is K0.5/S0.5 and never Km, so any option applying Km or a straight Lineweaver–Burk line to a sigmoid enzyme is wrong on definition. "Negative cooperativity" or "nH < 1" ⇒ the concerted MWC model is out, because it cannot produce it. "Vmax unchanged, curve shifted sideways" ⇒ a K-system modulator, which mimics competitive inhibition and is the standard distractor pairing.
  5. The sanity check — at [S] = K0.5, Y = 0.5 whatever n is, because the two terms in the Hill equation are then identical. Put it through your own working; if 0.5 does not fall out, your exponent is in the wrong place.
  6. Where the time goes — the Hill arithmetic is worth the full 66 seconds because it is a guaranteed-format calculation. The MWC-versus-KNF and ATCase-versus-PFK1 detail is recall, so answer it in fifteen seconds or flag it and come back.
Worked example 5 — extracting the Hill coefficient, and what it buys

An allosteric enzyme reaches 10 % of Vmax at [S] = 1.33 mM and 90 % of Vmax at [S] = 12.0 mM. (a) Find nH. (b) Find K0.5. (c) How much substrate would a non-cooperative enzyme with the same K0.5 need to cover the same 10 %→90 % span? (d) An activator halves K0.5 without changing nH — what is v at [S] = 4.0 mM, before and after?

  1. (a) From the Hill equation, at fractional saturation Y, [S] = K0.5(Y/(1 − Y))1/n. Divide the 90 % case by the 10 % case and K0.5 cancels: [S]90/[S]10 = (9 ÷ ⅑)1/n = 811/n.
  2. Here the ratio is 12.0/1.33 = 9.0, so 811/n = 9. Since 81 = 9², we need 1/n = ½, giving nH = 2.0. (Formally, n = log 81 / log 9 = 1.908/0.954 = 2.00.)
  3. (b) Substitute back at Y = 0.9: 12.0 = K0.5 × 91/2 = 3K0.5, so K0.5 = 4.0 mM. Check at Y = 0.1: 4.0 × (1/9)1/2 = 4.0/3 = 1.33 mM ✓.
  4. (c) With n = 1 the ratio is 811/1 = 81: from 4.0/9 = 0.44 mM up to 4.0 × 9 = 36 mM. Cooperativity has compressed a 81-fold requirement into a 9-fold one. With n = 4 it would be 811/4 = 3.0-fold — and this is the quantitative reason cooperative enzymes exist.
  5. (d) Before: Y = 4.0²/(4.0² + 4.0²) = 16/32 = 0.50 — half-maximal, as expected at [S] = K0.5. After the activator halves K0.5 to 2.0 mM: Y = 16/(4 + 16) = 16/20 = 0.80. A two-fold change in one constant produced a 60 % increase in rate at unchanged substrate — allosteric amplification in a single line of arithmetic.

§ 11

Covalent modification and zymogens

The ideaAllostery is instantaneous and it is also instantly forgotten — the moment the signalling molecule floats away, the enzyme reverts. Some jobs need memory: a hormone arrives for thirty seconds and the liver should keep releasing glucose for an hour. Other jobs need the opposite of reversibility altogether — a protease that digests protein must be absolutely, unconditionally off until it is somewhere it can do no harm. So cells also regulate by changing the enzyme's covalent structure. Bolt a phosphate on, and it stays on until a different enzyme takes it off. Cut a piece out of the protein, and there is no going back at all.

Definition & the two mechanisms

Covalent modification
Reversible attachment of a chemical group to a specific residue, changing activity. Overwhelmingly the commonest is phosphorylation of Ser, Thr or Tyr — reversible, but only because two different enzymes exist to do the two directions.
E–OH + ATP →kinase E–OPO32− + ADP · E–OPO32− + H₂O →phosphatase E–OH + Pi two enzymes, two reactions — the phosphatase is not the kinase running backwards

Why phosphorylation is such a good switch
A phosphoryl group adds two negative charges where there were none, which can make or break salt bridges across a whole domain; it adds three hydrogen-bond acceptors; it is bulky; and ATP is abundant so the modification is always available. Roughly a third of human proteins are phosphorylated at some point, by ~518 kinases, and removed by a smaller set of phosphatases.
Zymogen（酵素原）/ proenzyme
An inactive precursor activated by irreversible proteolytic cleavage. Because the cell cannot undo it, the switch is one-way and control has to be exerted on the activating protease and on separate inhibitor proteins.

Each enzyme in a covalent cascade activates many copies of the next, so the signal is multiplied at every tier. One hormone molecule can lead to the release of ~107–108 glucose molecules. Neither allostery nor gene expression can amplify like that.

Two features of the glycogen cascade deserve to be understood rather than memorised. First, the same kinase, PKA, phosphorylates both glycogen phosphorylase kinase and glycogen synthase — and phosphorylation activates the first and inactivates the second. One signal, opposite effects, no possibility of a futile cycle in which the cell simultaneously builds and breaks glycogen. Second, the modification is reversible only in the sense that a second enzyme, protein phosphatase 1, can remove it; the kinase reaction itself is thermodynamically one-way because it consumes ATP. This is a general design: reversible control built from two irreversible reactions, which is what allows the cell to set the position of the switch independently of any equilibrium.

Which form is the active one, and what phosphate does to glycogen

a is active · phosphate switches on the phosphorylase, so the synthase switches off

a is active — by convention the a form of an interconvertible enzyme is the more active one and b the less active one, for glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase alike. Phosphate switches on the phosphorylase — the enzyme that shares its name with the modification is the one the modification turns on (phosphorylase b → a at Ser14). The same cascade phosphorylates glycogen synthase in the same breath, and the no-futile-cycle rule sends that one the other way: glycogen breakdown on, glycogen synthesis off, never both at once. Now put the two halves together and the sting appears: phosphorylase a is the phosphorylated form, but synthase a is the dephosphorylated one. Phosphorylation is not a synonym for activation. The "a = active" convention is standard and appears in every textbook; the phosphate clause is my own phrasing of a standard fact. Two limits. It is glycogen-specific — do not generalise "phosphorylation activates breakdown" to other pathways, where the sign goes either way. And it omits the allosteric override that sits on top: muscle phosphorylase b, the less active form, is still switched on directly by AMP, which is how contracting muscle mobilises glycogen without waiting for a hormone.

Zymogens: making destructive enzymes safe

Chymotrypsinogen is synthesised, packaged and secreted as a single inactive chain. In the duodenum, trypsin cleaves the Arg15–Ile16 bond, producing π-chymotrypsin, and autolysis then excises two dipeptides to give α-chymotrypsin: three chains held together by disulfide bonds. What actually switches the enzyme on is subtle and worth knowing — the new N-terminal Ile16 swings inward and forms a salt bridge with Asp194, and it is that rearrangement which finally shapes the substrate pocket and completes the oxyanion hole. The catalytic triad was correctly assembled all along; what was missing was the binding site.

Trypsinogen is the master switch of the whole pancreatic set, activated by enteropeptidase (enterokinase) from the duodenal mucosa, which removes a hexapeptide; trypsin then activates further trypsinogen, plus chymotrypsinogen, proelastase and the procarboxypeptidases. Pepsinogen is different — it autoactivates below about pH 5, so the stomach's acid is itself the trigger. And the same logic governs blood coagulation (a cascade of serine-protease zymogens, amplifying at each tier), apoptosis (procaspases), and connective-tissue turnover (pro-MMPs).

Three diseases that are zymogen control failing

Acute pancreatitis is premature intrapancreatic trypsinogen activation: the amplifying cascade fires inside the organ that made it. The protective layers are physical (zymogen granules), chemical (a low-Ca²⁺ environment) and molecular — SPINK1, a trypsin inhibitor secreted alongside. Hereditary pancreatitis is usually PRSS1 R122H, a mutation of cationic trypsinogen that removes the site at which trypsin normally destroys itself, so activated trypsin persists.

α₁-antitrypsin deficiency. The serpin that restrains neutrophil elastase in the lung. The PiZZ variant (Glu342Lys) polymerises inside hepatocytes, giving both a loss of function in the lung — unopposed elastase, panacinar basal emphysema — and a gain of toxic function in the liver, with PAS-positive globules and cirrhosis. Cigarette smoke oxidises the reactive-centre methionine of even normal α₁-antitrypsin, which is why smoking and the deficiency compound each other so viciously.

Hereditary angioedema is C1-inhibitor deficiency — the same principle applied to complement and to kallikrein: with the brake gone, a protease cascade runs, bradykinin accumulates, and non-pruritic non-urticarial swelling follows. Note that antihistamines do nothing, which is the diagnostic clue and follows directly from the mechanism.
