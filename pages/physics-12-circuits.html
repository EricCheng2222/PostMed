Direct-Current Circuits

A circuit is a bookkeeping problem with two ledgers, and every equation on this page is one of them. Charge is conserved, so it cannot pile up at a junction — that gives you the junction rule, series current, and parallel current addition. Energy is conserved, and the electric field is conservative, so the potential you gain going round a loop must equal the potential you lose — that gives you the loop rule, the series voltage sum, terminal voltage, and every power formula. Learn the two ledgers and the twenty formulas stop being twenty things.

§ 01

Current: charge in motion, and why the lamp lights instantly

The ideaA wire is already full of loose electric charge before you switch anything on — it is just sitting there jostling about at random. Closing a switch does not send charge from the battery to the lamp; it tells the charge that is already spread through the whole circuit, the lamp included, to start shuffling along together. That is why the lamp lights the moment you press the switch even though the charge itself creeps forward more slowly than a snail, and it is why nothing gets used up on the way round: as much comes out of one end of a lamp as went into the other.

Definition & formula

Electric current
The rate at which charge passes through a chosen cross-section of a conductor. It is a scalar with a sign, not a vector — the arrow on a circuit diagram is a bookkeeping label, not a direction in space.
I = ΔQΔt   (instantaneous: I = dq/dt) definition; always true
Full definition, symbols and units
Drift velocity
The small average velocity that the otherwise random thermal motion of the conduction electrons acquires once an electric field is applied along the wire.
I = n q vd Auniform conductor, one carrier type
J = I / A = n q vdcurrent density; a vector, along E

One ampere is one coulomb per second. In the 2019 SI the ampere is fixed by defining the elementary charge as e = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ C exactly, so the coulomb is now the derived unit: 1 C = 1 A·s. For every calculation on this page use e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C.

Conventional current points the way positive charge would move — out of the positive terminal of a battery, through the external circuit, back into the negative terminal. In a metal the actual carriers are electrons, which drift the opposite way. This is a historical accident (Franklin guessed), and it is harmless: a flow of −q to the left is electrically identical to a flow of +q to the right. Keep every circuit calculation in conventional current and only switch to electrons when the question explicitly asks about them. In electrolytes and in tissue both signs move at once — Na⁺ one way, Cl⁻ the other — and both contribute to the same conventional current.

Worked example 1 · How slowly do electrons actually move?

A copper wire of cross-sectional area A = 1.0 mm² carries I = 3.0 A. Copper contributes about one free electron per atom, giving n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³. Find the drift speed, and the time for one electron to travel 1.0 m.

  1. Convert the area: A = 1.0 mm² = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m².
  2. Rearrange I = n q vd A → vd = I / (n q A).
  3. Denominator: (8.5×10²⁸)(1.60×10⁻¹⁹) = 1.36×10¹⁰; × (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 1.36×10⁴.
  4. vd = 3.0 / 1.36×10⁴ = 2.2×10⁻⁴ m·s⁻¹ = 0.22 mm·s⁻¹.
  5. Time for 1.0 m: t = 1.0 / 2.2×10⁻⁴ = 4.5×10³ s ≈ 76 minutes.

vd ≈ 0.22 mm·s⁻¹ — about 76 min to crawl one metre

Yet the lamp lights the instant you close the switch. Three facts resolve the paradox, and an examiner will want all three:

  1. The conduction electrons are already everywhere, including inside the filament. Nothing has to travel from the battery to the lamp; the lamp's own electrons only need to be told to start moving.
  2. The instruction travels as a field, not as matter. Closing the switch redistributes surface charge along the conductors, and that redistribution propagates at a large fraction of c — typically ≈ 2×10⁸ m·s⁻¹ in ordinary cable. For a 2 m lamp cord the delay is 2 / (2×10⁸) ≈ 1×10⁻⁸ s = 10 ns.
  3. Brightness depends on I, not on vd. Power dissipated is I²R. A slow drift of an enormous number of carriers (≈10²⁹ per cubic metre) is still a large current.

Exam point

The standard question is "estimate the drift speed and comment". The comment they want: the drift speed is tiny, but the electric field that sets the whole circuit in motion propagates near the speed of light, and the carriers are already distributed throughout the circuit. A water-filled pipe is the safe analogy: open the tap and water leaves the far end at once, even though any individual molecule moves slowly.

Trap

"Current is used up as it goes round the circuit." It is not. Charge is conserved, so in a series loop the current is identical before and after the lamp. What is used up is energy per unit charge — the potential drops. Say "the potential falls across the lamp", never "the current falls across the lamp".

§ 02

Resistance, resistivity and temperature

The ideaSome materials let charge through easily and some fight it, but the same material also fights harder when it is long and thin than when it is short and fat. It pays to keep those two things apart: how obstructive the stuff itself is, and how much of it the charge has to squeeze through. Temperature then leans on the first of them — and it leans in opposite directions for a metal and for something like silicon or living tissue, which is a difference worth understanding rather than memorising.

Definition & formula

Resistance
The ratio of the potential difference across a conductor to the current through it. It is a property of this piece of material, at this temperature — geometry included.
R = ρ LA uniform cross-section, uniform temperature
Full definition, symbols and units
Resistivity
The material's intrinsic opposition to current, independent of the size and shape of the sample. Copper is a good conductor whether it is a hair or a bus-bar.

The shape of R = ρL/A is not something to memorise. Double the length and the charge must fight through twice as much material in series, so R doubles: R ∝ L. Double the cross-section and you have provided two parallel paths, so R halves: R ∝ 1/A. That is the same reasoning as blood flow through a vessel — the only difference is that Poiseuille's law gives 1/r⁴ rather than 1/r², because a fluid also has to shear against itself.

Why metals and semiconductors go opposite ways

Resistivity depends on two things: how many carriers there are (n) and how far each travels before scattering (the mean free path).

  • Metals. n is fixed — roughly one free electron per atom, whatever the temperature. Heating makes the lattice ions vibrate harder, the electrons scatter sooner, the mean free path shortens. So ρ rises, and α is positive and of order +4×10⁻³ K⁻¹.
  • Semiconductors. n is tiny at room temperature and rises roughly as e−Eg/2kBT. Heating silicon (Eg = 1.1 eV) frees carriers exponentially fast, and that swamps the extra scattering. So ρ falls steeply and α is large and negative. This is exactly the NTC thermistor used in a clinical temperature probe: a few kΩ at 25 °C, measurably less at 37 °C, and cheap enough to be disposable.
  • Superconductors. Below a critical temperature ρ is not small — it is exactly zero. Niobium–titanium at 4 K is what lets an MRI magnet hold 1.5–3 T indefinitely on a persistent current with no power supply attached.
Worked example 2 · A copper lead, cold and hot

A copper wire is 20 m long with a diameter of 1.0 mm. Take ρ₂₀ = 1.68×10⁻⁸ Ω·m and α = 3.9×10⁻³ K⁻¹. Find R at 20 °C and at 80 °C.

  1. Area: A = πd²/4 = π(1.0×10⁻³)²/4 = 7.85×10⁻⁷ m². (Use the radius 0.5 mm — the most common slip in this calculation is squaring the diameter.)
  2. R₂₀ = ρL/A = (1.68×10⁻⁸)(20) / (7.85×10⁻⁷) = 3.36×10⁻⁷ / 7.85×10⁻⁷ = 0.428 Ω.
  3. ΔT = 80 − 20 = 60 K, so the bracket is 1 + (3.9×10⁻³)(60) = 1.234.
  4. R₈₀ = 0.428 × 1.234 = 0.528 Ω.

R₂₀ ≈ 0.43 Ω → R₈₀ ≈ 0.53 Ω, a 23% rise

Push that further and it stops being a footnote. A 100 W, 110 V incandescent lamp has a hot resistance of R = V²/P = 110²/100 = 121 Ω. Its tungsten filament runs near 2800 K, some 2500 K above room temperature; with α = 4.5×10⁻³ K⁻¹ the linear model predicts a factor of 1 + 11.25 ≈ 12. So the cold filament is only about 10 Ω, and the instant you flick the switch the inrush current is roughly 110/10 ≈ 11 A against a steady-state 0.91 A. That is why filament lamps almost always fail at switch-on.

Trap · stretching a wire

"A wire is stretched to twice its length; what happens to R?" Students answer "doubles". Stretching conserves volume, so if L doubles then A halves, and R = ρL/A goes up by 2 × 2 = four times. ρ never changes — it is a property of copper, not of this piece of copper. Keep that question distinct from a different one that looks similar: choosing a wire of twice the diameter at the same length quadruples A and divides R by four, with no compensating change in L.

§ 03

Ohm's law is a property, not a law

The ideaYou can take the voltage across anything at all, divide by the flow through it, and call the answer its resistance. That is just bookkeeping, and it always works. What is special about a plain metal resistor is that the number you get stays the same however hard you push. That is a habit of certain materials, not a rule the universe has to obey — a lamp, a temperature sensor and a diode all break it, which is why a graph of what flows against what pushes bends for them instead of running straight.

Definition & formula

Ohm's law
The empirical observation that for certain materials the current is proportional to the applied potential difference, so that R does not depend on V or I. It describes a class of materials; it is not a conservation principle and it has no universal validity.
I ∝ V  ⟺  R = V / I = constant ohmic materials, constant temperature
Full definition, symbols and units
Ohmic conductor
A conductor whose I–V graph is a straight line through the origin at fixed temperature.
V = I Rdefinition of R at this operating point — always true
J = σ Emicroscopic form; the statement about the material itself
Vpotential difference (V = J·C⁻¹)
Icurrent (A)
Rresistance at that operating point (Ω)
Jcurrent density (A·m⁻²)
σconductivity (S·m⁻¹)

The distinction matters and it is examined. V = IR is a definition: given any device at any operating point, you can always divide the voltage across it by the current through it and call the answer its resistance. Ohm's law is the extra claim that this ratio stays the same as you change V. A diode obeys V = IR at every instant — its "resistance" simply changes by six orders of magnitude between 0.4 V and 0.8 V, so it is not ohmic.

Exam point

Read the axes before you read the curve. Many papers plot V on the vertical axis against I. On an I-against-V plot the gradient is 1/R (steep = low resistance). On a V-against-I plot the gradient is R. A curve bending towards the horizontal axis means opposite things on the two plots.

One line to keep

V = IR is a definition you may always use. Ohm's law is the claim that R is a constant — and that claim is a property of the material, not a law of nature.

§ 04

Electrical power and the kilowatt-hour

The ideaVoltage says how much energy each scrap of charge is carrying; current says how much charge goes past every second. Multiply the two and you have energy per second, which is exactly what power means — so the headline formula here is not something to memorise, it is the two definitions written next to each other. Everything else in this section is that same product rearranged, plus the reminder that an electricity bill charges you for energy, meaning power kept up for a length of time, and never for power on its own.

Definition & formula

Electrical power
The rate at which a circuit element converts electrical energy into some other form — heat in a resistor, light plus heat in a lamp, chemical energy in a charging cell, mechanical work in a motor.
P = I V any element; V is the pd across it, I the current through it
Full definition, symbols and units
Kilowatt-hour
A unit of energy, not power: the energy used by a 1 kW device running for 1 hour.
P = I² Rsubstituting V = IR; use when I is shared (series)
P = V² / Rsubstituting I = V/R; use when V is shared (parallel)
E = P tconstant power
1 kW·h = 1000 × 3600 = 3.6×10⁶ Junit conversion
Ppower (W = J·s⁻¹)
Icurrent through the element (A)
Vpotential difference across it (V)
Rresistance (Ω)
Eenergy converted (J, or kW·h)
ttime (s, or h for kW·h)

P = IV needs no memorising: V is joules per coulomb and I is coulombs per second, so their product is joules per second. The two derived forms are only valid where V = IR holds numerically, i.e. for a resistor. Never apply P = V²/R to a battery or a motor — for those, use P = IV and let the sign of I tell you whether energy is being delivered or absorbed.

Exam point · pick the right form

The two forms answer opposite questions and the choice is usually the whole mark. Series: the current is common, so P = I²R — the largest resistor dissipates the most. Parallel: the voltage is common, so P = V²/R — the smallest resistor dissipates the most. Students who reach for P = V²/R in a series chain get the ranking exactly backwards.

Worked example 3 · An electric kettle on Taiwanese mains

A kettle is rated 1500 W at 110 V. Find (a) the operating current, (b) the resistance of the element, (c) the time to raise 1.0 kg of water from 25 °C to 100 °C assuming all the energy reaches the water (cwater = 4186 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹), and (d) the cost of that boil at NT$3.5 per kW·h.

  1. (a) P = IV → I = P/V = 1500 / 110 = 13.6 A. (A 15 A circuit; a 10 A extension lead would be a fire.)
  2. (b) R = V²/P = 110² / 1500 = 12100 / 1500 = 8.07 Ω. Cross-check: V/I = 110/13.64 = 8.07 Ω ✓.
  3. (c) Energy needed: Q = mcΔT = (1.0)(4186)(75) = 3.14×10⁵ J. t = Q/P = 313 950 / 1500 = 209 s ≈ 3 min 29 s.
  4. (d) 3.14×10⁵ J ÷ 3.6×10⁶ J·(kW·h)⁻¹ = 0.0872 kW·h. Cost = 0.0872 × 3.5 = NT$0.31.

13.6 A · 8.07 Ω · 209 s · ≈ NT$0.31

Trap · the rating is not a property of the device

"1500 W" is what the kettle draws at its rated 110 V. The element's resistance (8.07 Ω) is the fixed quantity. Plug the same kettle into 220 V and P = V²/R = 48 400 / 8.07 = 6000 W — four times the power, because P ∝ V² at fixed R. Voltage doubles, current doubles, power quadruples. That is why a Taiwanese 110 V appliance destroys itself on a 220 V outlet in seconds.

The kilowatt-hour survives because the joule is inconveniently small: a month of household electricity is of order 10⁹ J. Keep two facts: 1 kW·h = 3.6×10⁶ J, and a kW·h is an energy, so "kilowatts per hour" is always a mistake.

§ 05

Emf, internal resistance and terminal voltage

The ideaA real source is not a perfect one. Every battery carries some obstruction inside itself, so the harder you make it work, the more of its push is spent internally and the less is left at its terminals for everything else. That one fact explains why a nearly dead battery still looks healthy on a meter and then collapses the instant you ask it for real current, why a car battery can weld a dropped spanner in place, and why there is a hard ceiling on how much power you can ever pull out of a given source.

Definition & formula

Electromotive force (emf), ε
The energy a source gives to each coulomb of charge it drives round the circuit. Despite the name it is an energy per unit charge, measured in volts — not a force.
V = ε − I r source delivering current (discharging)
Full definition, symbols and units
Internal resistance, r
The unavoidable resistance of the source's own electrolyte, electrodes and leads, permanently in series with everything else in the loop.
Terminal voltage, V
The potential difference actually available between the source's terminals — what a voltmeter across the battery reads.
I = εR + rsingle loop, one source
V = ε + I rsource being charged: current forced in at the + terminal
ε I = I²R + I²renergy audit: supplied = useful + wasted internally
PR = ε²R(R + r)²  →  max at R = rmaximum power transfer; Pmax = ε²/(4r)
εelectromotive force (V)
rinternal resistance of the source (Ω)
Rexternal (load) resistance (Ω)
Vterminal voltage (V)
Icurrent in the loop (A)
PRpower delivered to the load (W)

Every real source is an ideal emf in series with a resistor. The moment current flows, some of the energy per coulomb is spent inside the source itself, so the terminal voltage sags below the emf by exactly Ir. Two consequences you should be able to state without thinking:

  • Open circuit (I = 0): V = ε. This is the only way to measure an emf directly — with a high-resistance voltmeter drawing negligible current.
  • Short circuit (R = 0): I = ε/r. Nothing but r limits the current. A car battery with ε = 12 V and r = 0.01 Ω can deliver 1200 A into a dropped spanner, which is why the spanner welds itself in place.

This also explains the most confusing everyday observation about batteries. A flat AA cell still reads 1.5 V on a multimeter, because a digital voltmeter draws only microamperes and Ir is negligible. Its internal resistance, however, has risen from ≈0.2 Ω when new to several ohms. Ask it for 500 mA and the terminal voltage collapses. A battery ages by growing r, not by losing ε.

Worked example 4 · A 12 V battery under load, and the price of maximum power

A battery has ε = 12.0 V and r = 0.50 Ω. It supplies an external resistor R = 5.5 Ω. Find I, the terminal voltage, the power delivered to R, and the efficiency. Then find the load that would draw the greatest possible power, and the efficiency there.

  1. I = ε/(R + r) = 12.0 / (5.5 + 0.50) = 12.0/6.00 = 2.00 A.
  2. V = ε − Ir = 12.0 − (2.00)(0.50) = 11.0 V. (Cross-check: V = IR = 2.00 × 5.5 = 11.0 V ✓)
  3. PR = I²R = (2.00)²(5.5) = 22.0 W; wasted internally Pr = I²r = 4.00 × 0.50 = 2.0 W. Total = 24.0 W = εI = 12.0 × 2.00 ✓.
  4. Efficiency = 22.0/24.0 = 91.7%.
  5. Maximum power transfer requires R = r = 0.50 Ω. Then I = 12.0/1.00 = 12.0 A and PR = I²R = 144 × 0.50 = 72 W (= ε²/4r = 144/2.00 ✓).
  6. But now Pr is also 72 W, so the efficiency has fallen to 50% — half the chemical energy is cooking the battery.

2.00 A · 11.0 V · 22.0 W · 91.7% → at R = r: 72 W but only 50% efficient

Trap · "maximum power" ≠ "maximum efficiency"

R = r maximises the power reaching the load, and at that point the efficiency is exactly 50% — never more. Efficiency η = R/(R + r) rises monotonically as R grows, tending to 100% as the load draws less and less power. Power delivery and efficiency are optimised at opposite ends. Impedance matching is what you want when the object really is to pull power out of a weak source — an antenna into a receiver, a transducer into its driver; it is the last thing you want in a power distribution grid, where r is deliberately made negligible. It is equally the wrong goal whenever you are measuring a voltage: an ECG front end deliberately refuses to match, using an input impedance vastly larger than the electrode impedance precisely so that it draws almost no current and loads the source as little as possible (§8).

Exam point · measuring r from a graph

Rearranged, V = ε − Ir is the straight line y = c + mx with V on the vertical axis and I on the horizontal. The vertical intercept is ε and the gradient is −r. If a question gives you two (I, V) pairs, subtract them: r = (V₁ − V₂)/(I₂ − I₁), which needs no intercept at all.

§ 06

Series and parallel — the reasoning, not the rule

The ideaComponents can be strung end to end, so that everything passing through one must then pass through the next, or joined side by side between the same two points, so that each offers a separate route. End to end, the obstructions simply add up. Side by side, you have opened extra lanes, so the pair is always easier to get through than either one on its own. Neither result has to be learned as a rule: both fall straight out of the fact that charge does not pile up at a junction and energy does not appear from nowhere on a trip round a loop.

Definition & formula

Series connection
Elements joined end to end with no junction between them, so every charge that passes through one must pass through the next.
Rs = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + … series: same I, voltages add
Full definition, symbols and units

Elements joined between the same two nodes, so each has the same pair of endpoints and therefore the same potential difference across it.

Rp = R₁R₂R₁ + R₂shortcut, two branches only

Icurrent into the whole combination (A)

Why series resistances add. There is no junction, so charge conservation forces the same I everywhere. Energy conservation then says the total energy lost per coulomb is the sum of the losses: V = V₁ + V₂ = IR₁ + IR₂ = I(R₁ + R₂). Divide by I and you have the rule. The current cancels — which is the reason the rule contains no I.

Why parallel conductances add. Both branches connect the same two nodes, so they share the same V. Charge conservation at the node says the currents add: I = I₁ + I₂ = V/R₁ + V/R₂ = V(1/R₁ + 1/R₂). Divide by V and you get 1/Rp. Written in conductances the rule is prettier and easier to remember: Gp = G₁ + G₂ + … Adding a parallel path is adding another lane, so it can only make it easier for charge to get through: a parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest branch. If your answer is bigger, you have inverted something once too few or once too many times.

Worked example 5 · A series–parallel combination, audited

An ideal 12.0 V supply drives R₁ = 6.0 Ω in series with the parallel pair R₂ = 4.0 Ω and R₃ = 12.0 Ω. Find every current, every voltage, and check the power.

  1. Collapse the parallel pair first: R23 = (4.0 × 12.0)/(4.0 + 12.0) = 48/16 = 3.0 Ω — smaller than 4.0 Ω, as it must be.
  2. Total: R = 6.0 + 3.0 = 9.0 Ω, so I = 12.0/9.0 = 1.33 A from the supply.
  3. Across R₁: V₁ = IR₁ = 1.333 × 6.0 = 8.0 V. Across the pair: V₂₃ = 12.0 − 8.0 = 4.0 V (= 1.333 × 3.0 ✓).
  4. Branch currents: I₂ = 4.0/4.0 = 1.00 A, I₃ = 4.0/12.0 = 0.33 A. Junction check: 1.00 + 0.33 = 1.33 A ✓. Current-divider check: I₂ = I·R₃/(R₂+R₃) = 1.333 × 12/16 = 1.00 A ✓.

I = 1.33 A · V₁ = 8.0 V · I₂ = 1.00 A · I₃ = 0.33 A · Ptotal = 16.0 W

Trap · the two dividers point opposite ways

In a voltage divider the resistor's own resistance is on top: V₁ = V·R₁/(R₁+R₂) — the bigger resistor takes the bigger share of the voltage. In a current divider the other resistor is on top: I₁ = I·R₂/(R₁+R₂) — the smaller resistor takes the bigger share of the current. Reversing them is the single most common algebra error in this chapter. Sanity-check by asking "which branch should get more?" before you write the fraction.

Trap · forgetting to invert

1/Rp = 1/4 + 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3. The answer is 3 Ω, not 1/3 Ω. Half of all lost marks in this section are people who stopped one step early.

§ 07

Kirchhoff's rules and a two-loop circuit solved end to end

The ideaOnce a circuit stops being a tidy chain of end-to-end and side-by-side pieces, the shortcuts run out and you need a method that always works. Two statements are enough for any circuit anyone will ever draw for you: whatever arrives at a junction has to leave it again, and if you walk all the way round any closed path you must finish at the same electrical level you started from. Turn those into equations, guess the directions of the flows however you like, and the algebra will quietly tell you which guesses were backwards.

Definition & formula

Junction (node) rule
At any junction the total current arriving equals the total current leaving. It is conservation of charge: nothing accumulates at a point in the steady state.
Σ Iin = Σ Iout   and   Σloop ΔV = 0 any DC network, however tangled
Full definition, symbols and units
Loop rule
Around any closed loop the algebraic sum of the potential changes is zero. It is conservation of energy, together with the fact that electric potential is single-valued: you must come back to the potential you started at.
resistor, traversed with the assumed current:  ΔV = − I Ryou are walking downhill
resistor, traversed against it:  ΔV = + I Rwalking uphill

A sign procedure that never fails

  1. Label every branch with an assumed current direction and stick to it. Guess freely; the algebra will correct you.
  2. Write the junction rule at (number of junctions − 1) junctions. The last one is always a combination of the others and gives you nothing new.
  3. Choose enough independent loops so that the number of equations equals the number of unknown currents. A loop is independent if it includes at least one branch no previous loop used.
  4. Traverse every loop clockwise. Not because clockwise is special, but because using one fixed convention removes an entire class of error.
  5. Apply the four sign rules above, in the order you meet the elements, and set the sum to zero. Note that the sign for a battery depends only on which plate you enter first — never on the current.
  6. Solve. A negative current means your guess was backwards; the magnitude is still correct. Do not go back and redraw — just report "1.0 A in the direction opposite to the arrow".

Two phrases carry the whole sign convention

"Walk with the current and the potential falls: −IR." · "Walk into the plus plate and the potential rises: +ε."

Worked example 6 · The two-loop circuit above, solved completely

ε₁ = 12 V with R₁ = 2 Ω; ε₂ = 4 V with R₂ = 2 Ω; the shared branch has R₃ = 3 Ω. Both batteries have their positive plate at the top. Find all three currents and audit the power.

  1. Junction rule at a. I₁ and I₂ arrive, I₃ leaves: I₁ + I₂ = I₃  …(3)
  2. Loop 1, clockwise from b: up the left branch, then down the middle. Going up we meet R₁ travelling with I₁ (−I₁R₁), then enter the battery at − and leave at + (+ε₁). Coming down the middle we travel with I₃ (−I₃R₃):
    12 − 2I₁ − 3I₃ = 0  …(1)
  3. Loop 2, clockwise from b: up the middle branch, then down the right. Going up the middle we travel against I₃ (+I₃R₃). Coming down the right branch we enter ε₂ at + and leave at − (−ε₂), and cross R₂ against I₂ (+I₂R₂):
    3I₃ − 4 + 2I₂ = 0  …(2)
  4. Solve. From (3), I₂ = I₃ − I₁. Put that in (2): 3I₃ + 2(I₃ − I₁) = 4 → 5I₃ − 2I₁ = 4. From (1), 2I₁ = 12 − 3I₃, so I₁ = 6 − 1.5I₃. Substituting: 5I₃ − 2(6 − 1.5I₃) = 4 → 5I₃ − 12 + 3I₃ = 4 → 8I₃ = 16.
  5. I₃ = 2.0 A, hence I₁ = 6 − 3.0 = 3.0 A and I₂ = 2.0 − 3.0 = −1.0 A.
  6. Read the negative sign. The 4 V cell carries 1.0 A downward, opposite the arrow: the 12 V battery is driving current backwards through it. The weaker cell is being charged.
  7. Independent check — compute Va − Vb three ways. Via R₃: I₃R₃ = 2.0 × 3 = 6.0 V. Via branch 1: ε₁ − I₁R₁ = 12 − 6 = 6.0 V. Via branch 2: ε₂ − I₂R₂ = 4 − (−1.0)(2) = 6.0 V. All agree ✓
  8. Power audit. Supplied: ε₁I₁ = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W. Absorbed: ε₂ × 1.0 = 4 W stored chemically in the 4 V cell, plus I₁²R₁ = 18 W, I₂²R₂ = 2 W and I₃²R₃ = 12 W as heat. Total 4 + 18 + 2 + 12 = 36 W ✓

I₁ = 3.0 A · I₂ = −1.0 A (i.e. 1.0 A the other way) · I₃ = 2.0 A · Vab = 6.0 V

Exam point · the free checks

Two audits cost thirty seconds and catch almost every sign error. (i) Compute the potential difference between the same two nodes along different branches — the answers must agree. (ii) Check that total power supplied equals total power absorbed. If a marker sees a correct audit they will usually believe the rest.

Trap · the battery sign does not depend on the current

A battery's contribution to the loop equation is set purely by which plate you walk into first: enter at −, exit at +, write +ε. The current may be flowing backwards through it (as I₂ is here) and the term is unchanged. Only resistor terms depend on the current direction. Conversely, do not "fix" a negative current by flipping the arrow halfway through — that changes the sign in one equation and not the others.

§ 08

Ammeters, voltmeters and the cost of measuring

The ideaAny instrument you attach to a circuit becomes part of that circuit, and so changes the very thing it is reporting. A meter that has to sit in the path of the flow should get in the way as little as possible; a meter that clips across two points should steal as little of the flow as possible. Those two demands are opposites, which is why the two instruments are built differently and why connecting one where the other belongs ruins the reading or the meter. It is a practical worry rather than a fussy one: a meter that is not good enough for the circuit it is placed on returns a wrong number rather than no number.

Worked example 7 · How much does the voltmeter lie?

Two 100 kΩ resistors are in series across an ideal 10.0 V supply, so each truly carries 5.00 V. What does a voltmeter read across one of them if (a) RV = 100 kΩ (a cheap analogue meter) and (b) RV = 10.0 MΩ (a digital multimeter)?

  1. (a) The meter is now in parallel with the resistor it is measuring: Rp = (100 × 100)/(100 + 100) = 50 kΩ.
  2. The circuit is now 100 kΩ in series with 50 kΩ, total 150 kΩ. Voltage divider: V = 10.0 × 50/150 = 3.33 V — an error of −33%.
  3. (b) Rp = (10⁵ × 10⁷)/(10⁵ + 10⁷) = 10¹²/1.01×10⁷ = 99.0 kΩ.
  4. Total = 199.0 kΩ, so V = 10.0 × 99.0/199.0 = 4.975 V — an error of −0.5%.

3.33 V with a 100 kΩ meter · 4.98 V with a 10 MΩ meter

Exam point

Loading error is worst when the meter's resistance is comparable to the circuit resistance it is bridging. The rule of thumb: keep RV at least 100× the resistance you are measuring across and the error stays under about 1%. This is not a laboratory curiosity — it is why an ECG amplifier needs an input impedance of >10 MΩ against skin–electrode impedances of tens of kΩ, and why a badly prepared electrode site attenuates the trace.

Trap · ohmmeters and live circuits

An ohmmeter contains its own source. Applying it to a powered circuit gives a meaningless reading and may destroy the meter. Always de-energise first, and remember that a component still connected in parallel with others is measured together with them, not on its own.

§ 09

RC circuits and the time constant

The ideaPut a store of charge behind an obstruction and it can no longer fill or empty in an instant. It has to be fed through the restriction, and the fuller it gets the less eagerly it accepts more, so the filling slows down as it goes. The result is the same unhurried approach you see in a bath filling through a half-closed tap, or a drug clearing out of the blood: brisk at first, dawdling later, never quite finished. One number sets the whole timetable — the time it takes to close roughly two thirds of whatever gap is left — and everything else in this section is counted in multiples of it.

Definition & formula

RC circuit
A resistor and a capacitor in series. Because the resistor limits the current and current is the rate of charge transfer, the capacitor's charge — and therefore its voltage — cannot change instantaneously.
τ = R C   [Ω × F = (V/A)(C/V) = C/A = s] series RC; the unit check is worth memorising

The exponential is not decoration. Apply the loop rule while charging: ε = iR + q/C. The bigger q gets, the smaller the driving term ε − q/C becomes, so the current falls; the falling current fills the capacitor more slowly still. A rate of change proportional to the remaining gap always integrates to an exponential approach. The same structure governs radioactive decay, drug elimination with first-order kinetics, and the washout of an inhaled anaesthetic — which is why clinicians also count in half-lives and "five time constants".

Two limiting behaviours are worth more marks than the algebra:

  • At t = 0 an uncharged capacitor behaves as a short circuit (vC = 0, so the full emf sits across R and the current is at its maximum ε/R).
  • At t → ∞ a capacitor behaves as an open circuit (i = 0, so no current flows in that branch and the full emf sits across the capacitor).

Between those two, the capacitor voltage is continuous and can never jump; the current can and does jump the instant a switch moves.

Worked example 8 · Charging a capacitor through a resistor

A 12.0 V battery charges an initially uncharged C = 470 μF capacitor through R = 10.0 kΩ. Find τ, the initial current, the final charge, the capacitor voltage after one time constant, and the time to reach 10.0 V.

  1. τ = RC = (10.0×10³)(470×10⁻⁶) = 4.70 s.
  2. At t = 0 the capacitor is a short circuit: i₀ = ε/R = 12.0/10 000 = 1.20 mA.
  3. At t → ∞ it is an open circuit: Q = Cε = (470×10⁻⁶)(12.0) = 5.64×10⁻³ C = 5.64 mC.
  4. At t = τ: vC = 0.632 × 12.0 = 7.58 V.
  5. Time to 10.0 V: from vC = ε(1 − e−t/τ), e−t/τ = 1 − 10.0/12.0 = 0.1667, so t = τ ln 6 = 4.70 × 1.792 = 8.42 s.
  6. Sanity check at that instant: the remaining 2.0 V sits across R, so i = 2.0/10 000 = 0.20 mA, which is 16.7% of i₀ — matching e−t/τ = 0.1667 ✓

Worked example 9 · Setting the rate of a cardiac pacemaker

A simple pacemaker uses a relaxation oscillator: a capacitor charges through a resistor from a 3.0 V lithium cell and the trigger circuit fires a stimulus pulse when vC reaches 63% of the supply — that is, after exactly one time constant. With C = 0.68 μF, what R gives a paced rate of 72 min⁻¹?

  1. Required interval between beats: T = 60/72 = 0.833 s.
  2. The trigger point is one time constant, so τ = 0.833 s.
  3. R = τ/C = 0.833 / (0.68×10⁻⁶) = 1.23×10⁶ Ω.
  4. Check the direction of the dependence: to pace faster you need a shorter τ, so a smaller R or C. Halving R to 610 kΩ gives τ = 0.417 s and a rate of 144 min⁻¹.

R ≈ 1.2 MΩ

Exam point · which quantity is being asked about

During charging, charge and capacitor voltage rise as (1 − e−t/τ) while the current and the resistor voltage fall as e−t/τ. During discharging, everything falls as e−t/τ. A question that says "after one time constant" is therefore ambiguous until you decide whether it means 63% or 37% — and the answer depends on which quantity, not only on which process.

§ 10

Where the energy goes: battery, capacitor, resistor

The ideaFollowing the energy is a better check on your work than following any of the individual numbers. When a source fills a store of charge through an obstruction, exactly half of the energy the source gives up ends up in the store and the other half is lost as heat along the way. Choosing a better component does not shift that split; it only changes how long the whole business takes. Emptying the store is the mirror image — everything that was saved comes back out, and it comes out far faster than the voltage falls, which is precisely what makes a defibrillator possible.

Charging a capacitor from a battery is the cleanest energy audit in the whole of circuit theory, and it produces a result students refuse to believe the first time.

  • The battery pushes a total charge Q = Cε round the circuit, and it does so at a constant potential difference ε. So it gives up Wbattery = Qε = Cε².
  • The capacitor ends up holding UC = ½Cε² — only half of that, because the early coulombs arrive when the capacitor voltage is still low and therefore carry away less energy than the last ones.
  • Conservation of energy leaves WR = Cε² − ½Cε² = ½Cε² for the resistor.
Go deeper · doing the integral, and why the resistance cancels out of it

Doing the integral confirms it and shows why R cancels: WR = ∫₀∞ i²R dt = (ε²/R²)·R·∫₀∞ e−2t/τ dt = (ε²/R)(τ/2) = (ε²/R)(RC/2) = ½Cε². Make R smaller and the current is larger but the process is briefer, in exactly compensating proportion.

Exam point · the 50% theorem

Charging a capacitor through any resistance from a constant-voltage source is exactly 50% efficient. Half the energy is stored, half is dissipated, and the value of R changes only how long it takes, never the split. This is a favourite question because the obvious guess — "use a smaller resistor and waste less" — is wrong. (Practical escape: charge through an inductor or a switched-mode converter instead of a resistor, which is exactly what a defibrillator's charging circuit does.)

Discharging is simpler: with no source present, every joule that was stored ends up in the resistor. Note though that the energy falls twice as fast as the voltage, since U ∝ v², so the energy time constant is τ/2. By t = τ the voltage has fallen to 36.8% but the stored energy is down to 0.368² = 13.5%; by t = 2τ, 98.2% of the energy has already been delivered.

Worked example 10 · The full audit for a charging capacitor

Using the circuit of worked example 8 — ε = 12.0 V, C = 470 μF, R = 10.0 kΩ — find the energy supplied by the battery, the energy finally stored, and the energy dissipated in R. Then repeat with R = 100 Ω.

  1. Charge transferred: Q = Cε = 5.64×10⁻³ C.
  2. Wbattery = Qε = (5.64×10⁻³)(12.0) = 6.77×10⁻² J = 67.7 mJ.
  3. UC = ½Cε² = ½(470×10⁻⁶)(144) = ½(6.768×10⁻²) = 33.8 mJ.
  4. WR = 67.7 − 33.8 = 33.8 mJ — exactly half.
  5. With R = 100 Ω, τ falls from 4.70 s to 0.047 s, so the whole process takes a hundredth of the time. Q, Wbattery, UC and WR are completely unchanged; only the peak current changes, from 1.20 mA to 120 mA.

Worked example 11 · A defibrillator discharge

A defibrillator stores charge on C = 32 μF raised to V₀ = 5.0 kV and discharges it through a transthoracic resistance of R = 50 Ω. Find the stored energy, the charge, the peak current, the time constant, and the average power delivered during the pulse.

  1. U = ½CV₀² = ½(32×10⁻⁶)(5.0×10³)² = ½(32×10⁻⁶)(2.5×10⁷) = 400 J (clinical settings are typically 150–360 J).
  2. Q = CV₀ = (32×10⁻⁶)(5000) = 0.16 C.
  3. Peak current, at t = 0: I₀ = V₀/R = 5000/50 = 100 A.
  4. τ = RC = (50)(32×10⁻⁶) = 1.6×10⁻³ s = 1.6 ms, so the pulse is essentially over in 5τ = 8 ms.
  5. Average power over those 8 ms: P = 400/8×10⁻³ = 5.0×10⁴ W = 50 kW — from a device running off a battery. The trick is entirely one of timing: the same 400 J is gathered over several seconds at a few tens of watts and given back in 8 ms, a power ratio of order 10³.
  6. Energy check: by t = 2τ = 3.2 ms the fraction delivered is 1 − e⁻⁴ = 98.2%.

400 J · 0.16 C · 100 A peak · τ = 1.6 ms · ≈50 kW average

Real defibrillators put an inductor in series with the patient, which stretches the pulse to roughly 5–10 ms and caps the peak current at a few tens of amperes; modern biphasic units then reverse the polarity part-way through, achieving the same defibrillation success at lower delivered energy. The RC estimate above still gives the right order of magnitude for every quantity, which is all an exam wants. The physiological point is the one to remember: the capacitor exists because the energy can be gathered slowly from a small battery but the power must be enormous and brief — long enough to depolarise the whole myocardium at once, short enough not to burn it.

§ 11

Electrical safety: macroshock, microshock and the clinic

The ideaWhat injures you is the flow through your body, not the voltage sitting on the wire; the voltage matters only because it is what drives the flow. Dry skin is very nearly the whole of your protection, and it is a thin, fragile layer that sweat, gel, a graze or a needle removes completely. That is why the levels treated as safe in a hospital are hundreds of times lower than the ones quoted for ordinary life: anything that reaches the heart directly has walked straight past every defence the body has.

Definition & formula

Macroshock
Current applied at the body surface, which then spreads through the whole torso. Only a small fraction of it passes through the heart, so a large total current is needed to do harm.
Ibody = V / Rbody current, not voltage, is what injures — voltage only matters through this
Full definition, symbols and units
Microshock
Current delivered directly to the myocardium through a conductive path such as a temporary pacing wire, a fluid-filled catheter or a central line. The current is not diluted, so roughly a thousand times less of it is dangerous.

"High voltage is dangerous" is a useful slogan and a poor physical statement. The damage is done by current: it depolarises excitable membranes, tetanises muscle, and heats tissue at I²R. Voltage matters only because it is the numerator of I = V/Rbody. A 30 kV static discharge from a car door is harmless not because the current is small — its peak is of order amperes — but because the stored charge is only a few microcoulombs and the whole event is over in well under a microsecond, far too briefly to depolarise anything; 110 V across wet hands can kill because it drives ≈100 mA for as long as the hand stays clamped.

Three modifiers change all of those numbers. Frequency: 50–60 Hz is close to the most hazardous frequency there is, because it is slow enough to re-stimulate cardiac cells cycle after cycle. DC thresholds are about 4–5 times higher, and above ~100 kHz stimulation ceases altogether and only heating remains — which is precisely why electrosurgical units run at 300 kHz to 3 MHz and can pass amperes through a patient safely. Duration: the shock must persist through the vulnerable T-wave phase of at least one cardiac cycle; this is why an RCD that disconnects in 25 ms saves lives. Path: hand to hand, or hand to the opposite foot, drives the current straight across the thorax; a path that stays on one side — hand to the foot on that same side, or hand to elbow of the same arm — largely misses the heart.

Worked example 12 · The same 110 V outlet, three different skins

Estimate the current through a person who bridges 110 V mains hand to hand when the total path resistance is (a) 100 kΩ (dry, intact skin), (b) 1.5 kΩ (wet hands), and (c) 500 Ω (skin bypassed — electrode gel, an abrasion, or an indwelling line).

  1. (a) I = 110/1.00×10⁵ = 1.1×10⁻³ A = 1.1 mA — perceptible, a tingle, nothing more.
  2. (b) I = 110/1500 = 7.3×10⁻² A = 73 mA — past let-go, into the fibrillation band. Nothing about the source changed; only the skin did.
  3. (c) I = 110/500 = 220 mA — squarely lethal.
  4. Now the microshock case. To drive the ≈100 μA that can fibrillate a heart through an intracardiac catheter of R ≈ 500 Ω you need only V = IR = (100×10⁻⁶)(500) = 0.05 V = 50 mV.

Trap · the skin is the safety factor, and it is fragile

Essentially all of a dry person's protection lives in a 20 μm layer of dead keratinocytes. Sweat, saline, conductive gel, a graze or a needle removes it, and the resistance falls by two to three orders of magnitude. Every one of those is routine in a hospital. This is also why the quoted "body resistance" in a textbook problem ranges over a factor of a thousand — read the stem carefully to see which situation is intended.

Grounding, GFCI, and why the third pin exists

An appliance chassis is bonded to earth by the third conductor. If a live wire chafes and touches the casing, the fault current flows to earth through a path of a fraction of an ohm, so the current is enormous — and that enormous current is what trips the breaker or blows the fuse. The protective earth does not make the fault safe; it makes the fault loud. Without it the casing sits at 110 V, waiting, and the first person to touch it becomes the conducting path. A residual-current device adds a second layer: it compares the current in the live and neutral conductors and disconnects within tens of milliseconds if they differ by more than 5–30 mA, because any imbalance means current is leaving by some third route — quite possibly a person.

Exam point · macroshock versus microshock

The distinguishing question is where the current enters. Skin contact spreads the current through the torso and only a percent or so reaches the heart, so the fibrillation threshold is ≈100 mA. A catheter, pacing wire or guidewire delivers current straight to the myocardium with no dilution, so ≈100 μA can fibrillate — a factor of about 1000. Hence cardiac-floating (CF) equipment standards cap patient leakage current at tens of microamperes, hence equipotential bonding in cath labs and ICUs, and hence the rule that you never touch an exposed pacing wire and an earthed object at the same time — gloves on, and everything round the bed bonded to a single potential so that there is no pd to drive anything through the wire. A patient with an intracardiac line has, electrically speaking, no skin.

The two clinical RC devices close the loop on this chapter. The defibrillator is a capacitor discharge (§10) deliberately delivering a current far above the fibrillation threshold — high enough to depolarise the entire myocardium simultaneously so that the sinoatrial node can resume control. The pacemaker is an RC oscillator (§9) delivering pulses of a few volts and a few milliamperes — some four orders of magnitude less current, and, because each pulse lasts under a millisecond, about seven orders less energy — directly to the endocardium. The same three symbols, R, C and ε, describe a device that restarts a heart and a device that runs one for a decade.
