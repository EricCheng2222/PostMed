Ecology and Animal Behaviour

Ecology is the study of what is limiting. A population grows at a rate set by births minus deaths until something — food, space, a predator, a disease — starts to bite harder the denser it gets. A community's membership is set by which species can hold a niche against the others that want it. An ecosystem's productivity is capped by the energy arriving and by whichever nutrient runs out first. And an animal's behaviour is a budget problem in the same currency: time and energy spent one way cannot be spent another. Almost every ecology question on this paper is really asking which constraint is binding here, and if you can name the constraint you can usually derive the answer instead of recalling it.

§ 01

Populations: density, dispersion, regulation

The ideaA population is all the individuals of one species in one place, and almost everything you can say about it starts with two numbers: how many there are per unit of space, and how they are arranged in that space. Nobody counts them all, so the numbers come from sampling, and every sampling method makes assumptions that can be wrong in a predictable direction. Then the interesting question: what stops the population growing for ever? Some things push back harder the more crowded it gets, and some things do not care how crowded it is. That single distinction is what "regulation" means.

Definition & key facts

Population density
The number of individuals per unit area or volume. Note the unit: density is a ratio, so a population can grow while its density falls if the habitat is expanding, and can fall while density rises if the habitat is shrinking — which is exactly what happens under habitat fragmentation.
N̂ = M × CR Lincoln–Petersen mark–recapture: marked fraction of the second sample estimates the marked fraction of the whole

Dispersion
The spatial pattern of individuals within the population's range: clumped (much the commonest — resources are patchy and many animals are social), uniform (produced by direct antagonism: territoriality, or allelopathy in plants) or random (rare, because it requires individuals neither to attract nor to repel one another).
Demography
The study of the vital statistics of a population — birth rate, death rate, immigration, emigration, and how each varies with age and sex.
Metapopulation
A set of local populations linked by occasional dispersal. Local extinction is normal; persistence is a balance between extinction and recolonisation, so patch connectivity matters as much as patch quality.

The mark–recapture logic is worth deriving rather than memorising, because the derivation tells you how it fails. You mark M animals and put them back. If they mix completely with the rest, the fraction of the population that is marked is M/N. Take a second sample of C animals; the fraction of that sample which is marked, R/C, is an estimate of the same quantity. Set them equal — M/N = R/C — and rearrange. Every assumption is now visible: the population must be closed (no births, deaths, immigration or emigration between samples), marks must not fall off, and marking must not change an animal's chance of being caught again. That last one is the killer. If being trapped once makes an animal cautious, R falls and N̂ is too high; if trapping is rewarded with bait so the same individuals return, R rises and N̂ is too low. Examiners like this because the direction of the bias is derivable, not memorisable.

Worked example 1 — a mark–recapture estimate, and what trap-shyness does to it

A team captures, marks and releases 120 skinks on a 4-hectare island. A week later they capture 150 skinks, of which 30 are marked. (a) Estimate the population and its density. (b) Suppose marked skinks became trap-shy, so that only 20 of the 150 were marked animals. What would the estimate have been, and in which direction is the error?

  1. (a) Marked fraction of the second sample. 30 / 150 = 0.20, so 20% of the population carries a mark.
  2. Population. The 120 marked animals are that 20%, so N̂ = 120 / 0.20 = 120 × 150 / 30 = 600 skinks.
  3. Density. 600 / 4 ha = 150 skinks·ha⁻¹. Note that density needed the area and the population estimate did not — a distinction worth keeping straight.
  4. (b) With trap-shyness. N̂ = 120 × 150 / 20 = 900.
  5. Direction. 900 / 600 = 1.5, a 50% overestimate. Reasoning it out beats memorising it: recapturing fewer marked animals makes the marked fraction look smaller, which makes the population look larger. Baiting the traps produces the opposite bias.
N̂ = 600 skinks · 150 ha⁻¹ · trap-shyness inflates the estimate to 900 (+50 %)

Exam technique · mark–recapture in thirty seconds, including the bias direction

  1. Assign the three numbers by role, not by the order they appear — M is however many were marked and released; C is the whole second sample; R is the marked animals found inside C. Stems deliberately give them out of order, and a stem that says "150 skinks were caught, 30 of which were marked" has just given you C and R, not R and C.
  2. Write N̂ = M × C / R and cross-check the shape — the two sample numbers multiply on top, the recaptured number divides. If you cannot remember it, rebuild it in five seconds from M/N = R/C.
  3. Sanity check before you look at the options — because R ≤ M and R ≤ C, the estimate must be at least as large as both samples. Any answer smaller than M, or smaller than C, is arithmetically impossible and can be struck without further thought. In the note's example (M = 120, C = 150, R = 30) the floor is 150, which kills "24" on sight.
  4. Know the three option shapes that are always planted — M × R / C (the formula inverted, giving 24 here), M + C (the number of capture events, giving 270) and C² / R (the second sample used twice, giving 750). None is ever right. Recognising a shape is faster than recomputing, and it also gives you a second, independent route: N̂ / M = C / R, so here N̂ is exactly five times M — 600 — with no long multiplication at all.
  5. If the stem breaks an assumption, answer with the direction only — the chain is one line: R falls → the marked fraction looks smaller → N̂ goes up. So trap-shyness, marks falling off and emigration of marked animals all overestimate; baiting the traps, so the same individuals come back, underestimates. You never need a number for these, and the arithmetic option is usually the decoy.

Density-dependent and density-independent regulation

Split every factor that changes birth or death rates into two boxes by asking one question: does its strength depend on how crowded the population is?

  • Density-dependent — intensity rises with density, so the factor acts as negative feedback and can genuinely regulate a population around an equilibrium. Competition for food, nest sites or light; accumulation of toxic wastes; transmissible disease (a directly transmitted pathogen needs a threshold host density to spread — the same logic as herd immunity); predation, when predators aggregate on abundant prey; territoriality; and physiological stress in crowded mammals.
  • Density-independent — intensity is unrelated to density, so it can massively change population size but cannot regulate it. A hard frost, a hurricane, a drought, a volcanic eruption, a pesticide application. Insect populations in seasonal climates are often almost entirely governed this way, which is why their numbers oscillate wildly rather than settling.

The word to be careful with is regulate. Only density-dependent factors regulate; a density-independent factor is a hammer, not a thermostat. That is precisely why an exam sentence like "severe winters regulate the population" is wrong even though severe winters obviously kill a great many individuals.

There is no 口訣 for the two lists above and you should not build one. Every factor sorts itself the moment you ask the single question in the heading — does its intensity rise as the population gets more crowded? Disease, competition, territoriality and waste accumulation obviously do; a hurricane obviously does not care. A memorised list of examples would be a second thing to carry and would fail on the first example the examiner picked from outside it, whereas the question works on any factor at all, including one you have never seen. The same applies to the direction of mark–recapture bias in the technique box above: derive it, do not store it.

Three sentences about dispersion that sound right and are not

"Random dispersion is the default pattern in nature." It is the rarest: random spacing requires that individuals neither attract nor repel each other and that resources are uniformly distributed, which almost never both hold. Clumped is the norm. "Uniform dispersion shows that resources are evenly spread." It usually shows the opposite — that something is actively pushing neighbours apart, such as territorial defence or the release of growth-inhibiting chemicals by desert shrubs. "A high-density population is a healthy one." Density is a number, not a verdict; at high density per-capita birth rate typically falls and mortality rises, and at very low density many species do worse still — the Allee effect, where individuals fail to find mates, cannot mount group defence, or lose the benefits of social thermoregulation. The Allee effect is the reason a rare species can slide to extinction even after hunting stops.

§ 02

Exponential and logistic growth

The ideaIf every individual reproduces at the same rate no matter how many others are around, the population grows in proportion to its own size — and anything that grows in proportion to itself accelerates, which is what a J-shaped curve is. Nothing does that for long, because sooner or later there is not enough of something to go round. So build the shortage into the equation: multiply the growth by the fraction of the environment still unused. The result bends over and flattens against a ceiling, and it makes one surprising prediction that the exam loves — a half-full environment grows fastest, faster than a nearly empty one and far faster than a nearly full one.

Definition & key facts

Exponential growth
Growth under ideal, unlimited conditions, where the per-capita rate of increase r = b − d is constant. The population's absolute increase gets larger every year purely because there are more individuals doing the reproducing.
dNdt = rN→dNdt = rNK − NK exponential, then the same thing throttled by how full the environment already is
Full definition, symbols and units
Carrying capacity K
The maximum population size a particular environment can sustain given its current resource supply. It is a property of the environment–species pair, not of the species, and it changes with season, weather and human management.
Logistic growth
The model in which per-capita growth declines linearly with density, reaching zero at N = K. The resulting curve is sigmoid (S-shaped), with its steepest point — the inflection — at N = K/2.
Maximum sustainable yield
The largest harvest that can be taken indefinitely. In the logistic model it is rK/4, taken by holding the population at K/2.

Read the logistic equation as two factors multiplied together. The first, rN, is the reproductive machinery: more individuals, more offspring. The second, (K − N)/K, is the brake: it is the fraction of the carrying capacity still unused, so it equals 1 when the habitat is empty and 0 when it is full. Their product is small at both ends for opposite reasons — at low density there is plenty of room but nobody to breed; at high density there are plenty of breeders but no room — and therefore maximal somewhere in the middle. Multiply out and the maximum falls exactly at N = K/2, where the growth rate is rK/4.

This is the single most useful consequence of the model, and it is counter-intuitive enough to be examined regularly. A depleted fishery does not recover fastest when it is nearly empty; it recovers fastest when it is half full. Equally, a population sitting at K is not growing at all, so a protected herd at carrying capacity produces no surplus. The management implication — harvest down to about half the carrying capacity and hold it there — is what "maximum sustainable yield" means.

Exam technique · "per-capita" or "total" — the word the whole item turns on

  1. First, underline "per-capita" or "total". This decides the answer before any arithmetic. Per-capita — written as (1/N)(dN/dt), "growth rate per individual", or "rate of increase per capita" — is largest as N → 0, where it equals r, and falls in a straight line to zero at K. Total — "dN/dt", "the population grows fastest", "the largest annual surplus", "maximum sustainable yield" — peaks at N = K/2 with the value rK/4. Options for both are always on the page together, which is the whole point of the question.
  2. Then run the two-number recipe. Halve K, quarter rK. With r = 0.50 and K = 2000 that is N = 1000 and dN/dt = 250·yr⁻¹, and you are done in ten seconds without touching the equation.
  3. Kill options by structure, not by calculation. Anything claiming growth is fastest at K is wrong — dN/dt is exactly zero there, which is also why a protected herd sitting at carrying capacity yields nothing. Anything claiming the total rate is fastest near zero has confused it with the per-capita rate. And an option equal to rN at N = K/2 (i.e. rK/2, twice the right answer) is the standard "forgot the brake" distractor. That is two or three of five options gone with no arithmetic at all — and with −0.25 or −0.5 for a wrong answer, one elimination already makes answering better than leaving it blank.
  4. Cheap sanity checks if you did compute something. The logistic prediction must always be below the exponential one for the same r and N₀ (263 < 363 in the worked example below), and it must never exceed K. For doubling time, the rule of 70 checks the logarithm: 70 / (100r) ≈ ln2 / r, so r = 0.18 gives about 3.9 yr either way.
  5. Where the time goes. This section deserves the full 66 seconds if the stem hands you a life table or a two-part harvest calculation. A bare "at what N is growth fastest" is a ten-second question — answer K/2, move on, and bank the time for §07 or §08.
Worked example 2 — a deer herd, exponentially and logistically

Sixty deer are released onto an island where r = 0.18 yr⁻¹ and K = 800. (a) What is the doubling time while resources are still unlimited, and how many deer would there be after 10 years under pure exponential growth? (b) How many are there after 10 years under the logistic model? (c) At what herd size does the island produce the largest annual surplus, and how big is it? (d) Compare the annual increase at N = 60, N = 400 and N = 700.

  1. (a) Doubling time. t₂ = ln2 / 0.18 = 0.693 / 0.18 = 3.85 yr. The rule of 70 gives 70 / 18 = 3.89 yr — close enough for a one-mark check.
  2. Exponential after 10 years. N = 60 e^(0.18 × 10) = 60 × e^1.8 = 60 × 6.05 = 363 deer.
  3. (b) Logistic after 10 years. (K − N₀)/N₀ = 740/60 = 12.33 and e^(−1.8) = 0.1653, so N = 800 / (1 + 12.33 × 0.1653) = 800 / 3.04 = 263 deer — already more than a quarter below the exponential prediction, and the gap widens fast.
  4. (c) Largest surplus. At N = K/2 = 400, with dN/dt = rK/4 = 0.18 × 800 / 4 = 36 deer·yr⁻¹. That is the maximum sustainable yield, and 400 is the herd size a manager would hold.
  5. (d) Compare. At N = 60: 0.18 × 60 × (1 − 60/800) = 10.8 × 0.925 = 10.0 deer·yr⁻¹. At N = 400: 36.0. At N = 700: 0.18 × 700 × 0.125 = 15.8 deer·yr⁻¹. A herd of 700 produces less than half the surplus of a herd of 400, despite being nearly twice the size. This is the whole argument for harvesting a population down to its most productive density.

What the logistic model assumes, and where each assumption breaks

Textbook prose often presents the S-curve as what populations do. It is what a very simple model predicts, and the exam will sometimes hand you a sentence that is only true inside the model. Four assumptions to be able to name:

  • K is constant. Real carrying capacities move with rainfall, season and succession. A population can overshoot a K that has just dropped, then crash — the classic reindeer-on-an-island pattern, which the logistic model cannot produce at all.
  • Density feedback is instantaneous. Add a time lag between crowding and its consequences and the same equation generates damped oscillations, stable cycles or chaos. Real populations overshoot because this year's crowding is felt through next year's offspring.
  • All individuals are equivalent. There is no age or stage structure, so juveniles that eat but do not breed simply do not exist in the model.
  • Per-capita growth declines linearly. It often does not — and at very low density it can turn negative (the Allee effect), which the logistic model, where growth is always positive below K, flatly denies.

Boom-and-bust versus regulated growth

Two population trajectories are worth being able to tell apart on sight. A J-curve that soars and then crashes vertically is the signature of a population whose growth is limited by density-independent catastrophe — many insects, algal blooms, a colonising species on a new island. An S-curve that levels out and then fluctuates modestly around a plateau is the signature of density-dependent regulation. A third pattern, regular multi-year cycles, usually implicates a time-lagged interaction — the snowshoe hare and Canada lynx being the famous case, where the hare cycle is driven mostly by its own food supply and by predation together rather than by the lynx alone. The textbook simplification that "the lynx cycle drives the hare cycle" is the wrong way round: removing lynx does not abolish the hare cycle, and hare numbers cycle even where lynx are absent.

§ 03

Life histories, survivorship and human demography

The ideaAn organism has a finite budget and has to decide — over evolutionary time, not consciously — how to spend it: many cheap offspring or a few expensive ones, breed once or breed repeatedly, breed early or grow first. There is no free lunch, so every one of those choices trades against the others, and the pattern of trade-offs a species has settled on is called its life history. Plot how many of a birth cohort are still alive at each age and you get a picture of that strategy; tabulate survival and fertility together and you can compute whether the population is replacing itself at all.

Definition & key facts

Net reproductive rate R0
The average number of female offspring a newborn female is expected to produce over her whole life, weighting each age's fertility by the probability of surviving to that age. It is the multiplier per generation: R₀ > 1 means the population grows, R₀ = 1 exactly replaces itself, R₀ < 1 means decline.
R₀ = Σ lxmx·T = Σ x lxmxR₀·r ≈ ln R₀T per-generation multiplier, generation time, and the per-year rate that follows
Full definition, symbols and units
Life table
An age-specific summary of survival and fertility. A cohort life table follows one group of individuals born at the same time until all are dead; a static life table infers the same quantities from the age structure of a population at one moment, and is much easier to collect but assumes conditions have been constant.
Survivorship lx
The proportion of the original cohort still alive at the start of age class x. Always starts at 1.0 and can only fall.
Semelparity / iteroparity
One big terminal reproductive episode (Pacific salmon, agaves, many annual plants) versus repeated reproduction over a lifetime. Semelparity is favoured where adult survival between breeding seasons is low and offspring survival is unpredictable.

Survivorship curves — and why the axis matters

Plot lx against age with survivorship on a logarithmic axis and three shapes emerge. The log axis is not decoration: on it, a straight line means a constant per-capita mortality rate, because equal proportional losses become equal vertical drops. Draw the same data on a linear axis and Type II is a curve, not a line, and the diagnostic vanishes. Questions that show you a graph and ask which type it is are really testing whether you noticed the axis.

Which survivorship type is which shape — the one thing a graph question actually asks

I late cliff · II level line · III early crash

Three two-word phrases, each tagged with its roman numeral, and each carrying when the deaths happen — which is the whole of the curve shape, since the shape is just a picture of the timing. I — late cliff: survivorship runs flat along the top and then falls off a cliff at the end, so the curve is convex and death comes in old age. Few offspring, heavy parental care — humans, elephants, whales, and the K-selected column of the table below. II — level line: a straight diagonal, because the per-capita mortality rate is level, the same at every age — hydra, many rodents, lizards, some birds. III — early crash: the crash comes at the start and the survivors then flatten out, so the curve is concave — huge broods and no care, almost all lost in the first weeks: oysters, most fish, most plants, and the r-selected column. Read it off in numeric order and the curves come out in order too, from dying latest to dying earliest. If convex and concave are the pair that trips you, the cliff/crash wording settles it without them: concave caves in early, convex holds up and only then gives way. My own phrasing, not a hook in general circulation. One thing it deliberately does not carry, and the examiner does: the shapes are only diagnostic when survivorship is plotted on a logarithmic axis. On a linear axis Type II is a curve, not a line, and "level line" stops being true — so check the axis label before you use this at all.

Human demography and the demographic transition

Apply the same machinery to people and one pattern dominates the last two centuries. Pre-industrial societies sat at high birth rate and high death rate, so growth was slow and erratic (stage 1). Then public health, clean water, vaccination and food security cut the death rate — fast, and decades before anything changed about fertility. Births stayed high while deaths fell, and the gap between the two curves is the population explosion (stage 2). Fertility then falls too, as child survival improves, as children stop being farm labour and start being expensive, and above all as women gain education and contraception (stage 3). Finally both rates settle low, and growth returns to near zero — or below it (stage 4).

Two consequences are worth being able to state. First, population momentum: a country whose fertility has just fallen to the replacement level of about 2.1 children per woman keeps growing for decades, because its age structure is bottom-heavy and a huge cohort of young women has yet to pass through childbearing. Fertility and growth are not the same variable. Second, the mirror image: a country whose fertility has been far below replacement for a generation acquires a top-heavy age pyramid, and its population then falls even if fertility recovers. Taiwan is the clearest case in the world — a total fertility rate below 1.0, among the lowest ever recorded — and the resulting old-age dependency ratio is the background to a great deal of health policy the reader will meet professionally.

r/K is a teaching device that has been superseded — but it is still examined

The r/K scheme is a continuum, not two boxes, and the same organism sits at different points on different axes: a large tree makes millions of tiny seeds (r-like) but takes decades to mature (K-like). Modern life-history theory superseded it from the late 1970s onwards with age-specific mortality models, which predict when to breed from where in the life cycle mortality falls, and explain cases r/K cannot. Be able to use the r/K vocabulary, because the paper uses it — but do not accept an option that treats it as a rigid dichotomy, and be suspicious of any statement of the form "r-selected species always…". The other reliable trap: r-selected does not mean "has a high r" in a given census, it means selection has favoured traits that maximise r under low-density conditions.

Go deeper · life-history trade-offs, bet-hedging, and why nothing is optimal at everything

The reason life histories differ at all is that resource allocation is zero-sum. Three trade-offs recur and each has direct experimental support:

  • Offspring number versus offspring quality. Add eggs to a kestrel's clutch and the parents fledge more chicks but each is lighter and less likely to survive its first winter — and the parents themselves die younger. The observed clutch size is close to the one maximising lifetime, not annual, output.
  • Current reproduction versus survival. Reproduction is physiologically costly, so selection tunes how much to spend now against the chance of breeding again. Where adult mortality is high, spending everything now is optimal — which is the general explanation of semelparity, and explains why salmon that must survive a long river migration are semelparous while those with short migrations are not.
  • Growth versus reproduction. Delaying maturity buys a larger body and therefore a bigger clutch for ever after, at the cost of possibly dying before breeding at all. The optimum depends entirely on the juvenile mortality rate.

Bet-hedging is the case that most often defeats naive optimality reasoning. In a variable environment, natural selection maximises the geometric mean of fitness across generations, not the arithmetic mean — and a single zero drives a geometric mean to zero. So a strategy with a lower average output but no catastrophic years can beat a higher-average, high-variance one. Desert annuals whose seeds germinate over several years rather than all at once are the standard example: in any one year the staggered germinator loses, but it never loses everything.

§ 04

Competition and the niche

The ideaTwo species that make their living in exactly the same way in exactly the same place are competing for exactly the same things, and one of them will be slightly better at it. Given enough time and a stable environment, slightly better wins completely. So when you see two similar species living side by side, the right question is not "how do they coexist" but "what are they doing differently" — and the answer is usually something small and specific: a different part of the tree, a different-sized seed, a different time of day. The difference need not be large; it only needs to be real.

Definition & key facts

Ecological niche
The full set of biotic and abiotic conditions under which a species can persist, and the full set of resources it uses — Hutchinson's n-dimensional hypervolume. A niche is a species' occupation, not its address; the address is the habitat.
realised niche ⊆ fundamental niche what a species could occupy alone, minus what competitors and predators take away
Full definition, symbols and units
Competitive exclusion principle
Two species cannot coexist indefinitely in the same niche in a stable, resource-limited environment. Every one of those three qualifiers is doing work; drop any of them and coexistence is perfectly possible.
Resource partitioning
The differentiation of niches that allows similar species to coexist — by food size, microhabitat, foraging height, activity period or season.
Character displacement
The evolutionary signature of past competition: two species differ more in a competing trait where they occur together (sympatry) than where each occurs alone (allopatry). Darwin's ground finches differ in beak depth on shared islands and converge on single-species islands.

Gause's classic result is the cleanest demonstration: grow two Paramecium species separately and each follows a logistic curve to its own K; grow them together on the same bacterial food and P. aurelia drives P. caudatum to extinction within a fortnight. But the correct generalisation is narrower than the one students carry away. Pair P. caudatum instead with P. bursaria, which takes yeast from the bottom of the tube while P. caudatum takes bacteria from the suspension, and the two coexist indefinitely, because the niches are no longer identical. Competitive exclusion is not "similar species cannot coexist" — it is a statement about complete niche overlap under stable, limiting conditions, and the real world supplies few of those.

The barnacle experiment on Scottish rocky shores makes the niche distinction concrete and is the model for a whole family of exam questions. Chthamalus is found only high on the shore, Semibalanus only lower down. Scrape away Semibalanus and Chthamalus promptly colonises the lower zone and does well there — so its fundamental niche extends far below its realised one, and the boundary is drawn by competition (Semibalanus grows faster and simply undercuts and crushes it). Remove Chthamalus and Semibalanus still does not move up, because it desiccates. Generalise this: on a stress gradient, the limit at the benign end is usually set by competition, and the limit at the harsh end by physiological tolerance. That one sentence answers most questions of this shape, including the equivalents for plants on a moisture gradient and for mangroves along a salinity gradient.

How coexistence actually happens — the answers to "but they overlap"

Real communities are full of species with overlapping requirements, so know the escape routes. Resource partitioning — MacArthur's five warbler species all feed in the same spruce trees but at different heights and on different branch zones. Spatial and temporal heterogeneity — the environment is patchy and variable, so no single competitor is best everywhere or always. Disturbance — storms, fires and grazing keep resetting the community before exclusion can finish. Predation — a predator that preferentially eats the dominant competitor (a keystone predator, §06) protects everything else. Frequency-dependent effects — specialist pathogens hit a species harder as it becomes common, which is the leading explanation for the extraordinary tree diversity of tropical forests (the Janzen–Connell effect). The general principle behind all of them: coexistence is stable whenever each species limits itself more than it limits the other.

Niche sentences that are lifted from textbooks and are still wrong

"The niche is where an organism lives." That is the habitat. The niche is what it does there. "The realised niche is the part of the fundamental niche with the best conditions." No — it is what is left after other species have taken their share, and it is often the physiologically worse part, as in Chthamalus stranded up in the desiccating zone. "Competitive exclusion means two species can never occupy the same habitat." Two species share habitats constantly; what they cannot share indefinitely is a niche. "Character displacement is an example of competitive exclusion." It is the opposite outcome — the alternative to exclusion, reached by evolving apart rather than by one species disappearing.

§ 05

Predation, herbivory and symbiosis

The ideaBeing eaten is the strongest selection pressure there is, so anything that can be eaten has invested heavily in not being. The investments come in two flavours: don't be found, or be found and not be worth eating. Meanwhile the eater is under equal pressure to defeat whatever the prey has just evolved, so the two lineages push on each other indefinitely — neither ever wins. And once two species have been pushing on each other for long enough, the relationship can flip: a partner that started as a parasite can end up indispensable, which is how most of the great mutualisms began.

Definition & key facts

Symbiosis
Any close, long-term physical association between two species — including parasitism. The common exam error is to use "symbiosis" as a synonym for mutualism; mutualism, commensalism and parasitism are the three kinds of symbiosis, distinguished only by the sign each partner receives.
mutualism +/+ · commensalism +/0 · parasitism +/− the three symbioses — read the signs, not the intimacy
Full definition, symbols and units
Coevolution
Reciprocal evolutionary change: a trait in one species evolves in response to a trait in another, which then evolves in response. Strictly it requires the reciprocity to be demonstrated, which is why many textbook "coevolution" examples are really one-sided adaptation.
Aposematism
Conspicuous warning coloration advertising a genuine defence. It only works if predators can learn, which means it is frequency-dependent — a rare aposematic morph is at a disadvantage until enough predators have been educated.
Batesian vs Müllerian mimicry
Batesian: a harmless species mimics a defended one and free-rides on its reputation. Müllerian: two or more genuinely defended species converge on one warning pattern, sharing the cost of teaching predators.

Defences, and the arms race that produces them

Animal defences sort naturally into avoid-detection and deter-attack. Cryptic coloration (camouflage) and its variants — countershading, disruptive patterning, masquerade as a twig or a leaf — work before the predator has committed. Aposematic coloration works after: bright, memorable warning signals paired with a real toxin, sting or foul taste. Between them sit the mimicries, which are exam favourites precisely because the two kinds behave in opposite ways as the mimic becomes common. A Batesian mimic — a harmless hoverfly patterned like a wasp, or the scarlet king snake wearing the coral snake's bands — is parasitic on the model's reputation, so if mimics outnumber models the predators stop learning and the signal collapses. A Müllerian complex — several genuinely unpalatable Heliconius butterflies converging on one pattern — is mutualistic, and every additional individual improves the protection of all. Ask which direction increasing mimic abundance pushes the benefit and the two are never confusable again.

Batesian or Müllerian, decided by one letter and checked by one direction

Batesian = Bluff · Müllerian = Mutual

Batesian is a bluff: the mimic is harmless and free-rides on a defended model's reputation, so the relationship is parasitic — the hoverfly on the wasp, the scarlet king snake on the coral snake. Müllerian is mutual: every member of the complex is genuinely defended and they share the cost of educating predators, so the relationship is mutualistic — the unpalatable Heliconius butterflies converging on one pattern. Then check yourself with the direction, which is the part the exam actually turns on: more Batesian mimics → less protection (the lesson is diluted, so the mimic must stay rarer than its model), more Müllerian mimics → more protection for all (fewer deaths per species to teach each predator). The B-for-bluff, M-for-mutual pairing is in wide circulation and you will meet it elsewhere; the direction rule underneath it is the one that decides marks, so if the two ever come apart in your head, trust the direction. Note what the hook does not cover: aposematism itself is frequency-dependent for the same reason — a rare warning morph is at a disadvantage until predators have been taught — and cryptic coloration is a third, separate category that works before the predator commits rather than after.

Plants cannot run, so their defences are chemical and structural, and this is where the clinical hook is genuine rather than decorative. The plant secondary compounds — alkaloids, terpenoids, tannins, cyanogenic glycosides — exist to poison herbivores, and human pharmacology has raided the shelf wholesale: morphine and codeine from opium poppy, atropine from deadly nightshade, digoxin from foxglove, quinine from cinchona bark, vincristine and vinblastine from Catharanthus, paclitaxel from yew, caffeine, nicotine and capsaicin from their respective plants. A drug with a narrow therapeutic index and a plant origin is usually a herbivore deterrent that we have chosen to take deliberately.

Go deeper · parasitism as an ecological force, and the mutualisms that were once parasitisms

Parasites are the majority lifestyle on Earth by species count, and they shape communities out of all proportion to their biomass. Three consequences worth carrying:

  • Parasites regulate host populations. Because transmission of a directly transmitted parasite rises with host density, parasitism is a textbook density-dependent factor — and one that can produce the multi-year cycles otherwise attributed to predators. The population crashes in the red grouse cycle in Scotland were experimentally prevented by treating the birds for a nematode.
  • Parasites manipulate behaviour. Toxoplasma gondii must reach a cat to reproduce sexually, and infected rodents lose their innate aversion to cat odour. Dicrocoelium drives an infected ant to clamp onto a grass blade at dusk where a grazing sheep will eat it. These are extended phenotypes: genes in one organism building structures in another.
  • Parasite pressure explains sex. The Red Queen hypothesis: hosts must keep recombining to stay ahead of fast-evolving parasites, which is the leading explanation for why sexual reproduction persists despite its twofold cost.

The boundary with mutualism is porous, and the traffic runs both ways depending on the ledger. Mycorrhizal fungi supply phosphorus and receive carbon — but under high soil phosphorus the plant restricts carbon supply, and the relationship becomes closer to parasitic. Rhizobium nodules are policed: legumes withhold oxygen from nodules that fix too little nitrogen. And the two organelles of note 01, mitochondrion and chloroplast, are the endpoint of the process — endosymbionts that began as free-living bacteria and were reduced to dependency, which is why they retain their own circular genome and 70S ribosomes and why bacterial ribosome inhibitors have mitochondrial side effects.

Confusions the paper reuses in this area

"Symbiosis means both partners benefit." It means they live in close association; the sign is a separate question. "A successful parasite kills its host." A parasite that kills quickly loses its habitat; virulence evolves towards whatever maximises transmission, which is often intermediate — and is high where transmission does not need a mobile host, as with vector-borne and water-borne diseases. "Predator and prey population cycles show the predator controls the prey." Sometimes; often the prey's own food supply drives the cycle and the predator merely tracks it. "Herbivory is a form of predation with the same effects." Herbivores usually remove part of an individual rather than killing it, so plants can compensate by regrowth — and moderate grazing can even increase primary production. "Commensalism is common." True commensalism is difficult to demonstrate, because on close inspection the supposedly unaffected partner almost always gains or loses a little; treat +/0 as an approximation, not a category found cleanly in nature.

§ 06

Community structure, diversity and succession

The ideaA community is all the species living together in one place, and describing it takes two numbers, not one: how many kinds there are, and how evenly the individuals are shared out among them. A wood with one overwhelming species and seven rare ones is not diverse just because you can list eight names. Communities also have architects — species whose removal changes everything, sometimes species you would never have guessed from their abundance. And communities are not static: strip a patch bare and it fills up in a roughly predictable order, because early arrivals change the conditions in ways that suit later ones.

Definition & key facts

Species diversity
Two components together: richness, the number of species present, and evenness, how equally abundance is distributed among them. A diversity index combines both into one number; richness alone does not.
H = −Σ pi ln pi·D = Σ pi² Shannon (higher = more diverse) and Simpson's dominance (report 1 − D, or 1/D as an effective species number)

A species whose effect on community structure is large relative to its abundance. The relative clause is the definition; without it the term collapses into "important species".
Dominant species
The species with the greatest abundance or biomass. Dominance and keystone status are opposite kinds of importance and the exam tests the difference directly.
Foundation species / ecosystem engineer
A species that physically builds or modifies the habitat other species live in — coral, kelp, a beaver, a forest tree. Distinguished from a keystone species by mechanism: engineering the environment rather than exerting a strong trophic effect.
Succession
Directional change in community composition after a disturbance. Primary starts where there is no soil and no seed bank; secondary starts where soil and often root stocks and seeds survive.

Worked example 4 — why the community with the most species can be the least diverse

Three samples, each of 100 individuals. X: four species, 25 individuals each. Y: four species, 85/5/5/5. Z: eight species, 93 and seven singletons. Compute Shannon H and Simpson's 1 − D, and rank them.

  1. X. Every p = 0.25, so H = −4(0.25 ln 0.25) = ln 4 = 1.386. A perfectly even community always gives H = ln S, which is a useful sanity check. D = 4(0.25²) = 0.25, so 1 − D = 0.75 and the effective number of species 1/D = 4.0 — exactly the real richness, as it should be when evenness is perfect.
  2. Y. H = −[0.85 ln0.85 + 3(0.05 ln0.05)] = 0.138 + 0.449 = 0.588. D = 0.85² + 3(0.05²) = 0.7225 + 0.0075 = 0.730, so 1 − D = 0.270 and 1/D = 1.37 effective species.
  3. Z. H = −[0.93 ln0.93 + 7(0.01 ln0.01)] = 0.067 + 0.322 = 0.390. D = 0.93² + 7(0.01²) = 0.8649 + 0.0007 = 0.866, so 1 − D = 0.134 and 1/D = 1.16.
  4. Ranking. X (1.386) > Y (0.588) > Z (0.390). Sample Z has twice the richness of the others and the lowest diversity of the three, because 93% of the individuals belong to one species. Both indices agree, and both effective-species counts say the same thing in plainer language: Z behaves like a community of barely more than one species.
  5. What to take away. Simpson's D is the probability that two individuals drawn at random belong to the same species, so it is dominated by the commonest species; Shannon's H gives relatively more weight to rare ones. If a question offers "the community with the most species is the most diverse", it is testing exactly this, and the answer is no.
H: X 1.386 · Y 0.588 · Z 0.390 — richness 4, 4, 8. Richness alone is not diversity.

Exam technique · diversity-index items, and the checks that need no calculator

  1. Recognition rule. If a stem hands you several samples and one of them has more species but one overwhelming dominant, the examiner is testing richness against evenness and the intended answer is that the species-rich sample is the least diverse. The option "…because it has the most species" is planted, and it is planted because that sentence appears in textbook prose almost verbatim. Reading the abundances, not the species count, is the whole question.
  2. Ceiling check on Shannon H. H ≤ ln S always, with equality only when the community is perfectly even. So compute ln S first — ln 4 = 1.386, ln 8 = 2.079 — and strike every option above it. For an even sample you are already finished: H is ln S, no summation required.
  3. Bracket check on Simpson. D = Σp² is a probability, so 0 < D ≤ 1 and any option outside that is dead on arrival. Better still, convert: 1/D is the effective number of species and must lie between 1 and the real richness S. An option implying 1/D > S is impossible; one implying 1/D ≈ 1 is telling you the sample behaves like a monoculture.
  4. Watch which way each index runs. Higher H means more diverse. Higher D means more dominance, i.e. less diverse — which is why it is reported as 1 − D or 1/D. That sign flip is the second planted error, and it costs nothing to check: D is defined as the probability that two individuals drawn at random are the same species, so of course it rises as diversity falls.
  5. Where the time goes. A ranking question is answerable from evenness by inspection in about fifteen seconds; only compute H if two samples are genuinely close. Full logarithms for three samples will eat two minutes you do not have.

Keystone species and trophic cascades

Paine's experiment on the Washington coast is the founding case and the model for the exam question. Remove the starfish Pisaster ochraceus — never abundant, a small fraction of the biomass — from experimental plots and within a couple of years the mussel Mytilus californianus, released from predation, monopolises the rock and the number of species in the plot roughly halves — from about fifteen to eight in Paine's original removal. Pisaster is a keystone not because it eats a lot but because what it eats is the competitive dominant. That is the mechanism to state: a keystone predator prevents competitive exclusion.

Where the effect runs down several trophic levels it is a trophic cascade. Sea otters eat sea urchins; urchins eat kelp holdfasts. Remove otters — as the fur trade did — and urchin barrens replace kelp forests, taking the whole assemblage of fish and invertebrates that lived in the kelp with them. Return otters and the kelp returns. Note the alternating sign as you go down: removing a predator increases the herbivore and decreases the plant, and one more level would flip it again. Being able to propagate that sign is worth a mark on its own.

Keystone, dominant, foundation, invasive — four different claims

A dominant species is defined by abundance or biomass: the sugar maples in a maple forest. A keystone species is defined by influence out of proportion to abundance: Pisaster, the sea otter, the fig trees whose year-round fruiting feeds tropical frugivores through the lean season. A foundation species or ecosystem engineer is defined by physical construction: coral, kelp, beavers, and the trees themselves. An invasive species is defined by origin and spread, not by role. The stems that catch people out describe a low-biomass species whose removal collapses the community — that is a keystone, and the tempting wrong answer is "dominant" precisely because the species is obviously important.

Succession

The distinction the paper actually tests is soil. Primary succession begins on a surface that has never supported a community and has no soil and no seed bank — a new lava flow, the moraine left by a retreating glacier, a fresh sand dune, a new volcanic island. Soil itself has to be manufactured, from rock weathered by lichen acids and from the organic matter of the pioneers, and this takes centuries. Secondary succession begins where a disturbance has removed the community but left soil, seed bank and often surviving root stocks — after a fire, a hurricane, a clear-cut, or on abandoned farmland. It is measured in decades because the hard part is already done.

At Glacier Bay in Alaska, the retreating ice exposes bare moraine and the sequence is well documented, and it teaches the real mechanism. The step that matters is not the lichens people quote first: it is the arrival of nitrogen fixers — the mat-forming Dryas, then alder with its Frankia symbionts — because nitrogen is the one major plant nutrient that rock weathering cannot supply at all. Soil nitrogen climbs steeply under alder, spruce then invades and eventually shades the alder out, and soil pH falls from about 8 to about 5 as conifer litter accumulates. The pioneers changed the site in a way that made it unsuitable for themselves and suitable for their successors, which is the essence of facilitation.

Three mechanisms drive succession and they are not alternatives so much as a menu, and the exam does test that the first is not the only one. Facilitation: early species make conditions better for later ones (the alder story). Tolerance: later species simply do better under low-resource conditions and win by persistence, with no help from the pioneers. The three mechanisms of succession — and the guarantee that you produce all three

FIT

Facilitation — the pioneer improves the site for its successor and is then displaced by it (alder fixing nitrogen at Glacier Bay, then shaded out by the spruce it made possible). Inhibition — the pioneer actively resists replacement and goes only when disturbance or death removes it (intertidal algae holding the rock). Tolerance — the later species simply endures low resources better and wins by persistence, with no help and no hindrance from the pioneer. Each letter decodes itself, so the word is not carrying meaning you would otherwise have to store — it is carrying the count, and the count is the examined part: the trap is answering "facilitation" as though it were the only mechanism. The letters are unordered; there is no sequence here, and an option claiming that succession proceeds facilitation-then-tolerance-then-inhibition is inventing a schedule that does not exist. FIT also says nothing about primary versus secondary succession, which is a separate axis decided entirely by whether soil survived.

"Succession ends in a stable climax community determined by climate"

This is Clements's early-twentieth-century view, it is in a great many textbooks as background, and it is not how ecologists describe succession now — which makes it exactly the kind of plausible sentence you should be ready to reject. Three reasons. The endpoint is often not unique: the same site can settle into different self-maintaining states depending on which species arrived first and in what order (alternative stable states, priority effects). Disturbance is not an interruption of succession but part of the normal regime, so much of a "mature" landscape is a mosaic of patches of different ages. And communities are not superorganisms with a developmental programme — species respond individually to the environment, which is why community composition changes continuously along a gradient rather than in discrete zones. A related trap: the intermediate disturbance hypothesis predicts that diversity peaks at moderate disturbance — too much and only fugitive species survive, too little and competitive exclusion runs to completion — so "less disturbance always means more diversity" is wrong.

§ 07

Ecosystem energetics

The ideaTwo things move through an ecosystem and they behave completely differently. Energy arrives from the sun, gets passed along a chain of eaters, and leaks away as heat at every hand-over until there is none left — it goes in one end and out the other, and it never comes back. Atoms do come back: the same carbon and nitrogen atoms are used, released and used again indefinitely. So an ecosystem needs a permanent energy supply but not a permanent supply of matter. Everything about food chains — why they are short, why there are few large predators, why eating meat costs more land — follows from how much energy is lost at each step, which is most of it.

Definition & key facts

Net primary productivity (NPP)
The rate at which producers store chemical energy after paying their own respiratory costs: NPP = GPP − Rautotroph. NPP, not GPP, is the amount available to everything else in the ecosystem, and it is what an ecologist means by "productivity" unqualified.
trophic efficiency = production at level n+1production at level n ≈ 10 % a convention, not a law — measured values run from about 5 % to about 20 %
Full definition, symbols and units
Gross primary productivity (GPP)
Total energy fixed by photosynthesis per unit area per unit time, before any of it is respired.
Net ecosystem production (NEP)
NPP − Rheterotroph: whether the whole ecosystem, decomposers included, is a net carbon sink (positive) or source (negative). A mature forest can have large NPP and near-zero NEP; a drained peatland has negative NEP.
Production efficiency
Net secondary production divided by assimilated energy — how much of what a consumer digests ends up as new consumer tissue. Endotherms manage 1–3% because they burn most of it keeping warm; ectotherms about 10%; some insects reach 40%.

The mass present at one instant. Not the same as productivity: standing crop is a stock, productivity is a flow, and turnover time = standing crop / production.

Worked example 5 — the 10% rule, and what a diet costs in land

A grassland has NPP = 8000 kJ·m⁻²·yr⁻¹ and trophic efficiency is 10% at each step. (a) How much production reaches a tertiary consumer? (b) A person needs about 10 MJ per day. How much land is needed to feed one person on a purely plant diet, and on a purely beef diet? (c) Explain the English Channel data: phytoplankton standing crop 4 g·m⁻², zooplankton standing crop 21 g·m⁻².

  1. (a) Count the transfers, not the levels. Producers are level 1, a tertiary consumer is level 4, so there are three transfers: 8000 → 800 → 80 → 8 kJ·m⁻²·yr⁻¹. The off-by-one here is the single commonest arithmetic mistake in this topic.
  2. (b) Annual requirement. 10 000 kJ·d⁻¹ × 365 = 3.65 × 10⁶ kJ·yr⁻¹.
  3. Plant diet. The person is a primary consumer, eating plant production directly: 3.65 × 10⁶ / 8000 = 456 m², about a twentieth of a hectare.
  4. Beef diet. Now the person is a secondary consumer, and the cow has already discarded 90%: 456 × 10 = 4563 m², nearly half a hectare — ten times the land for the same calories. This is the quantitative core of every argument about the land and water cost of animal agriculture, and it falls straight out of the trophic ladder.
  5. (c) An inverted biomass pyramid. There is five times more zooplankton biomass than phytoplankton biomass, which looks like a violation until you separate stock from flow. Phytoplankton are cropped and replaced within days: at a production of roughly 1 g·m⁻²·d⁻¹ the turnover time is 4 g·m⁻² ÷ 1 g·m⁻²·d⁻¹ ≈ 4 days, whereas the zooplankton standing crop turns over in something like 21 ÷ 0.1 ≈ 210 days. A small, fast-replaced stock can support a large, slow one. The energy pyramid — a flow measured over a year — is still upright, and by the second law it always must be.
Tertiary consumer 8 kJ·m⁻²·yr⁻¹ · 456 m² on plants vs 4563 m² on beef · biomass pyramids may invert, energy pyramids never

Which pyramids can invert, and which cannot

Energy: never. Each level is fed by the one below and loses most of what it gets, so an inverted energy pyramid would violate the second law. Biomass: yes, in aquatic systems with fast-turnover producers, as above. Numbers: yes, in both directions — one oak tree supports thousands of caterpillars (inverted), and a parasite level always inverts the pyramid because parasites are smaller and more numerous than their hosts. So the only safe general statement is about energy. Note also the phrasing trap: a pyramid of productivity is an energy pyramid and cannot invert; a pyramid of standing crop is a biomass pyramid and can.

Exam technique · trophic arithmetic, and the filters that kill options without it

  1. Count transfers, and get it right by subtracting one. Transfers = trophic level − 1. Producers are level 1, so a primary consumer is one transfer, a secondary consumer two, a tertiary consumer three. Then move the decimal point that many places: 8000 → 800 → 80 → 8. The off-by-one — answering 80 for a tertiary consumer — is the single commonest mistake in this topic and it is always on the option list, one power of ten either side of the truth.
  2. Translate the wording into a level number first. "A fish that eats fish that eat zooplankton" is level 4. "A hawk eating a snake that ate a mouse that ate seeds" is level 4. Write the number down before you look at the options; the stem is testing your ability to parse the chain, not your division.
  3. Land-cost questions are the same recipe upside down. Each step up the chain multiplies the land needed by ten. Work out the plant-diet area once, then multiply by 10 per animal step. A one-step diet (beef) is 10×; there is no need to redo the annual-requirement arithmetic.
  4. Use pyramid type as a pure elimination filter. Energy or productivity: can never invert — an inverted energy pyramid violates the second law, so any option asserting one is wrong before you read the numbers. Biomass or standing crop: can invert, in aquatic systems where the producers turn over in days. Numbers: can invert either way — one oak and a thousand caterpillars, or any host-and-parasite level. So the only safe universal statement an option can make is about energy, and an option that generalises from biomass to "all pyramids" is overclaiming.
  5. Read the noun the stem used. "Productivity" and "production" are flows and therefore energy; "standing crop" and "biomass" are stocks. A question that gives you g·m⁻² is talking about a stock; one that gives you g·m⁻²·yr⁻¹ is talking about a flow. The unit alone settles which pyramid rule applies, which is a five-second check.
  6. No hook for the NPP/NEP definitions and none needed. Start with everything the plants fixed (GPP), subtract each set of respirers in turn: minus the plants' own respiration gives NPP, minus the consumers' and decomposers' respiration gives NEP. The order of subtraction is the order of who breathes, so an option defining NPP as "total energy captured by photosynthesis" has described GPP, and one saying energy cycles while nutrients flow has the two exactly backwards.

Productivity statements that get quoted wrongly

"The ocean is the most productive part of the biosphere." Per unit area the open ocean is close to a desert — roughly 125 g·m⁻²·yr⁻¹ against a tropical rainforest's ~2200, about eighteen times less — because light and nutrients rarely coincide. It still contributes about a quarter of global NPP, but only because it covers some 65% of the planet's surface; it is all marine systems together that come to roughly half of global production. Highly productive marine systems exist, but they are estuaries, coral reefs, upwelling zones and coastal shelves, not the open sea. "NPP is the total energy captured by photosynthesis." That is GPP; NPP is what survives the plant's own respiration. "Energy cycles through the ecosystem while nutrients flow through it." Exactly backwards, and it appears in options often enough to be worth a reflex. "Decomposers occupy the top trophic level." They are not a level at all — they take detritus from every level, which is why they are drawn beside the pyramid rather than on top of it.

§ 08

Biogeochemical cycles

The ideaAtoms are not consumed, only moved, so every element has a set of places it sits and a set of processes that shift it between them. Two questions decide how a cycle behaves. Does the element have a gas phase? If it does, it can travel anywhere quickly and local shortages get topped up from the air; if it does not, it moves only as fast as rock weathers and water flows, so it runs out locally and stays out. And is the abundant form usable? Nitrogen gas is four-fifths of the air and almost none of it is available to living things, which is why the whole cycle is organised around the small number of organisms that can crack it open.

Definition & key facts

Biogeochemical cycle
The movement of a chemical element between biotic and abiotic reservoirs. Every cycle has slow-exchange reservoirs (rock, deep ocean, fossil deposits) and fast-exchange ones (atmosphere, soil solution, living biomass); human impact almost always consists of moving material from the slow set into the fast set faster than the return route can cope.

Nitrification
Aerobic oxidation of NH₄⁺ to NO₂⁻ (Nitrosomonas) and then NO₂⁻ to NO₃⁻ (Nitrobacter) by chemoautotrophic bacteria that live on the energy released.
Denitrification
Anaerobic reduction of NO₃⁻ back to N₂ by bacteria using nitrate as a terminal electron acceptor instead of oxygen. This is the only large route by which nitrogen leaves the biological pool for the atmosphere.

Which nitrifier makes which ion — the one genuinely arbitrary pair in the cycle

Nitrosomonas → nitrous → nitrite

This is not a folk hook, it is the chemical naming rule you already use, applied to the bacterium's name. In inorganic nomenclature the -ous / -ite pair always denotes the lower oxidation state and -ic / -ate the higher: nitrous acid → nitrite, NO₂⁻ (亞硝酸鹽); nitric acid → nitrate, NO₃⁻ (硝酸鹽). Nitrosomonas carries the low-state prefix, so it does the low-state step: NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻. Nitrobacter has no such prefix, so it finishes the job: NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻. The Chinese terms make the same point even faster — 亞 in 亞硝酸鹽 is the "-ous", so 亞硝酸 goes with Nitrosomonas. This covers only the two nitrification steps. Nothing else in the cycle needs a hook, and here is why: the process names describe themselves. Fixation fixes N₂; nitrification makes nitrate; assimilation takes it into biomass; ammonification makes ammonium; denitrification undoes nitrification and removes the nitrogen. The oxygen requirement is derivable too and should never be stored: nitrification is an oxidation, so it needs O₂; denitrification is a reduction that uses NO₃⁻ as the terminal electron acceptor, which an organism only bothers to do when O₂ has run out — hence anaerobic, hence waterlogged soils losing nitrogen.

Each element alliterates with the place it limits, so the pairing decodes itself and there is no order to get wrong. P for ponds — phosphorus limits most fresh water, and the reason is in the table above: P has no gas phase, so a lake's shortfall cannot be topped up from the air, whereas a lake's nitrogen shortfall can be, by cyanobacterial fixation. N for nearly all else — nitrogen limits nearly everything that is not a lake: most terrestrial systems and most of the sea, because the enormous atmospheric reservoir is locked behind the N≡N triple bond and only prokaryotes can open it. Fe for the far ocean — iron limits the large "high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll" regions far from any coast, where N and P are present but sit unused for want of the iron that the nitrate-reducing and photosynthetic enzymes need. My own phrasing, not a hook in general circulation. Two limits on it. The iron line applies to the HNLC regions specifically, not to the ocean generally — coastal and upwelling waters are productive and nitrogen-limited, which is why the hook says far. And the line encodes only which nutrient; the why is Liebig's law of the minimum plus the gas-phase column of the table, and it is the why that answers a stem asking what happens when you fertilise.

Why a plant that cannot get nitrogen from air houses a bacterium that can

Nitrogenase is the whole story of the nitrogen cycle in one enzyme. It reduces the strongest common bond in chemistry, at enormous ATP cost, and it is destroyed by oxygen. So the legume builds a nodule, supplies photosynthate, and manufactures leghaemoglobin — an oxygen buffer that keeps free O₂ near zero while still delivering enough to the bacteroid's own respiration to pay the ATP bill. Free-living cyanobacteria solve the same problem differently, by confining nitrogenase to thick-walled heterocysts that have shut down oxygen-evolving photosystem II. If an option tells you that the legume fixes nitrogen, or that root nodules contain a fungus, or that nitrogenase works better in aerated soil, all three are wrong for the same underlying reason.

Go deeper · the carbon and phosphorus cycles in more detail, and why the difference matters

Carbon. The atmospheric reservoir is small and fast: photosynthesis and respiration move roughly a sixth of it every year, which is why the seasonal sawtooth in the Keeling curve is visible at all — it is the Northern Hemisphere's forests breathing in and out. The ocean holds around fifty times as much carbon as the atmosphere, mostly as bicarbonate, and it exchanges with the air on a timescale of centuries because deep water mixes slowly. Fossil fuels are carbon that left the fast cycle hundreds of millions of years ago; burning them returns it in decades, which is the entire mechanism of the current disturbance. Atmospheric CO₂ has gone from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to about 420 ppm — a 50% increase. Roughly a quarter of the emitted CO₂ has dissolved into the ocean, where it forms carbonic acid; surface ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1 unit, which because the scale is logarithmic is a 26% rise in hydrogen ion concentration. The consequence for corals and shelled plankton comes through the carbonate equilibrium: extra H⁺ converts CO₃²⁻ to HCO₃⁻, so the carbonate ion those organisms need to build CaCO₃ becomes scarcer.

Phosphorus. The absence of a gas phase changes everything. Phosphorus enters ecosystems only by weathering of apatite and similar minerals, moves as PO₄³⁻ in soil water, binds tightly to soil particles rather than travelling far, and ultimately washes to the sea and settles as sediment — from which it returns only through geological uplift, on a timescale of tens of millions of years. Three consequences follow directly. Phosphorus is the classic limiting nutrient of fresh water, because the nitrogen shortfall in a lake can be made up by cyanobacterial fixation from the air and the phosphorus shortfall cannot. Phosphorus fertiliser must be mined, so it is a genuinely finite resource in a way that nitrogen fertiliser is not. And phosphate added to a lake stays in the system, cycling between water, sediment and biomass — which is why lake recovery after phosphate control is slow.

§ 09

Climate, biomes and aquatic zonation

The ideaWhere you are on the planet decides how much sunlight arrives and how the air moves, and those two things between them decide how warm and how wet a place is. Warmth and water are what plants need, so the plants of any region are the ones adapted to that particular combination — and since the plants build the structure everything else lives in, naming the temperature and the rainfall is almost enough to predict the whole community. That is why deserts occur in a band at the same latitude on every continent, and why an unrelated set of plants on the other side of the world can look uncannily like the ones at home.

Definition & key facts

Biome
A major community type recognised by the growth form of its dominant vegetation — forest, grassland, shrubland, desert, tundra — and defined by climate rather than by taxonomy. Two biomes of the same name on different continents share structure and physiognomy, not species.
biome ≈ f(mean annual temperature, mean annual precipitation) two numbers predict most of it; seasonality and soil supply the rest
Full definition, symbols and units
Hadley circulation
Air heated at the equator rises, dumps its moisture as it cools (hence equatorial rainforest), travels poleward at altitude and descends dry at about 30° N and S — which is where nearly every major desert on Earth is found.
Rain shadow
Air forced up a mountain range cools and precipitates on the windward side, then descends warm and dry on the leeward side. The reason the arid interior sits behind the range.
Photic and aphotic zones
The lit surface layer where photosynthesis exceeds respiration, and the dark water beneath it. The depth of the boundary depends on turbidity, which is why coastal water is shallow-photic and clear open ocean is deep-photic.
Thermocline · turnover
The steep temperature gradient separating warm surface water from cold deep water. In temperate lakes it breaks down in spring and autumn, and the resulting turnover mixes oxygen down and nutrients up — which is what drives the seasonal productivity peaks.

One point about tropical rainforest is worth stating because it contradicts an intuition and is examined. The soils are poor, not rich. High temperature and constant moisture make decomposition almost instantaneous, so nutrients spend almost no time in the soil: they are taken straight back up by a dense shallow root mat, often via mycorrhizae, and the ecosystem's nutrient capital sits in the living biomass rather than in the ground. That is why clearing rainforest for agriculture yields two or three good harvests and then nothing — and why temperate grassland, where cold winters slow decomposition and organic matter accumulates, has the deepest and most fertile soils in the world.

There is no 口訣 for the biome list and building one would be a waste of a slot. Every biome sits at a known place on two axes, and the axes are printed on the diagram above: warm-to-cold left to right, wet-to-dry top to bottom. Given a temperature and a rainfall you can walk to the right box; given a biome you can recite its climate. An eight-item acronym would give you the names in an order nobody asks for, while leaving you unable to answer the question that is actually set, which is always of the form "which biome would you expect at X °C and Y cm" or "why are deserts at 30° on every continent". Learn instead the three generators that the diagram encodes: the Hadley cell (rising wet air at the equator → rainforest; descending dry air at ~30° → desert), the rain shadow, and the fact that the cold-and-very-wet corner is empty for a physical reason — cold air cannot hold the water vapour. Those three produce the map; the names are then just labels.

Aquatic systems are not classified by climate but by light, nutrients, salinity, depth and flow. The vocabulary you need: photic versus aphotic by light penetration; pelagic (open water) versus benthic (bottom), with the deepest ocean floor the abyssal zone; littoral (shallow, rooted plants) versus limnetic (open water) in a lake; intertidal, neritic (over the continental shelf) and oceanic in the sea. Lakes run from oligotrophic — deep, clear, nutrient-poor, well oxygenated at depth — to eutrophic — shallow, turbid, nutrient-rich, and often anoxic at the bottom because decomposition consumes the oxygen faster than mixing replaces it.

Three high-productivity marine systems recur in questions. Estuaries, where river nutrients meet tidal mixing, are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth and are the nursery for a large fraction of commercial fish. Coral reefs are the opposite paradox: they occupy warm, clear, nutrient-poor water and achieve high productivity only because the coral houses photosynthetic zooxanthellae and recycles nutrients internally — which is also why thermal stress that expels the zooxanthellae (bleaching) is lethal. Upwelling zones on western continental margins bring deep nutrient-rich water to the lit surface, and support most of the world's fisheries on a tiny fraction of the ocean's area.

Climate and biome sentences to check twice

"Seasons are caused by the Earth's varying distance from the sun." They are caused by the 23.5° axial tilt; the Earth is closest to the sun in early January, during the northern winter. "Rainforest soil is fertile because the vegetation is so lush." The opposite, as above. "Tundra is a cold desert with very little water." Precipitation is low, but permafrost blocks drainage, so the summer surface is waterlogged — plants there are limited by temperature, nutrients and the short growing season rather than by liquid water. "Biomes are defined by their characteristic species." They are defined by vegetation structure and climate; convergent evolution means unrelated species fill the same roles on different continents, which is the whole reason the concept is useful. "A lake's productivity peaks in midsummer." In a temperate lake it peaks in spring and again in autumn, at turnover, when mixing lifts nutrients back into the lit surface layer; midsummer stratification starves the surface of nutrients.

§ 10

Conservation biology and global change

The ideaSpecies go extinct for reasons that are mostly not mysterious: we take their space, we move their enemies around, we take too many of them, and we change the chemistry of the air and water they live in. Of those, taking their space is by far the biggest, and it does more damage than the raw loss of area suggests — because a habitat cut into pieces is worse than a habitat that is merely smaller. And once a population gets small, a second set of problems begins that has nothing to do with the original threat: small populations lose genetic variation and suffer bad luck, and both make them smaller still.

Definition & key facts

Biodiversity
Measured at three levels, and conservation arguments turn on which one is meant: genetic diversity within a population, species diversity within a community, and ecosystem diversity across a landscape. A captive population can preserve a species while losing most of its genetic diversity.
S = cAz→S₂S₁ = (A₂/A₁)z·z ≈ 0.25 the species–area relationship: shrink a habitat and you can predict what it eventually costs
Full definition, symbols and units
Habitat fragmentation
The breaking of continuous habitat into isolated patches. It costs more than the area lost, because it also multiplies edge (altered light, wind, temperature, predators and parasites penetrating from outside) and because it blocks dispersal between patches.
Minimum viable population · effective population size
The smallest size at which a population has an acceptable probability of persisting. The number that matters genetically is Ne, the effective population size, which counts only breeding individuals and is reduced further by unequal sex ratios and unequal reproductive success — commonly a fraction of the census count.
Extinction vortex
The self-reinforcing spiral in which a small population loses genetic variation to drift, suffers inbreeding depression, breeds less successfully, becomes smaller, and drifts faster. This is why a species can continue to decline after the original threat has been removed.
Biomagnification
Increase in the tissue concentration of a persistent, fat-soluble pollutant at successive trophic levels. Distinguish it from bioaccumulation, which is the build-up within a single organism over its lifetime; biomagnification is the step between levels.

Worked example 6 — what does clearing 90% of a forest actually cost in species?

A forest is reduced to 10% of its original area and the remaining fragment is otherwise undamaged. Using S = cAz with z = 0.25: (a) what proportion of species is eventually retained? (b) How much area would have to survive to keep half the species? (c) How does the answer change if z is 0.15 or 0.35, and what does that range mean?

  1. (a) Take the ratio. The constant c cancels: S₂/S₁ = (A₂/A₁)^z = 0.10^0.25 = 0.562. About 56% of species remain and 44% are eventually lost — far more than the naive "10% of the area, 10% of the species", and far less than "90% of the area gone, 90% of the species gone". Both of those are common wrong intuitions and the power law sits between them.
  2. (b) Invert it. (A₂/A₁)^0.25 = 0.5 → A₂/A₁ = 0.5⁴ = 0.0625. Keeping just 6.25% of the area still retains half the species — which is the mathematical reason a small reserve is worth far more than nothing, and also why the last few percent of a habitat is so disproportionately valuable.
  3. (c) Sensitivity. 0.10^0.15 = 0.708 (29% lost) and 0.10^0.35 = 0.447 (55% lost). z is higher for true islands and for taxa with poor dispersal, and lower for samples nested inside a continuous continent, because in the continental case immigration keeps topping the sample up.
  4. The caveat that matters. This is an equilibrium prediction, and equilibrium arrives slowly: species persist in a fragment for decades or centuries while being committed to extinction — the extinction debt. A newly fragmented forest that still contains all its species has not escaped the calculation; it has not yet paid.
10 % of area retains ≈56 % of species (44 % lost) · 6.25 % of area retains half · z = 0.15–0.35 spans 29–55 % loss

Exam technique · species–area questions without a calculator

  1. Always take the ratio first. S₂/S₁ = (A₂/A₁)z — the constant c cancels, so you never need it and a stem that withholds it is not withholding anything. If an option or a working step still contains c, something has gone wrong.
  2. With z = 0.25, the power is a fourth root, and a fourth root is two square roots. 0.100.25: √0.10 ≈ 0.316, √0.316 ≈ 0.56. That is the whole calculation, by hand, in about fifteen seconds. Going the other way, keeping a fraction f of the species needs f1/z = f⁴ of the area: half the species needs 0.5⁴ = 6.25% of the area.
  3. Bracket the answer before computing, and eliminate on the bracket. Because 0 < z < 1, the retained species fraction always lies strictly between the retained area fraction and 1. Keep 10% of the area and you must retain more than 10% of the species and fewer than all of them — so "the species fall in proportion to the area" (10% left) and "an intact fragment loses nothing" are both structurally impossible whatever z turns out to be, and those are precisely the two wrong intuitions the examiner fishes with. One inequality, no arithmetic, two options gone; and with −0.25 or −0.5 marking, eliminating even one already makes answering better than a blank.
  4. Direction check on z. Larger z means a steeper loss: z = 0.15 → 29% of species lost, z = 0.35 → 55% lost, for the same 90% area loss. z is higher for true islands and poor dispersers, lower for a plot nested inside a continent, because there immigration keeps refilling the sample. If an option has that backwards, it is wrong regardless of its arithmetic.
  5. If the stem mentions time, the answer is extinction debt. A fragment that still holds all its species has not disproved the calculation; the species–area result is an equilibrium prediction and equilibrium arrives over decades to centuries. "The species are still there, so the model failed" is a distractor, not a refutation.

Eutrophication, and why the algae are not what kills the fish

Run fertiliser, sewage or detergent phosphate into a lake and the limiting nutrient stops limiting. Algae and cyanobacteria bloom, and the surface turns green. The sequence that follows is worth reciting in order, because the exam distractor is always the shortcut. The bloom shades out submerged plants, which die. The bloom itself is short-lived and dies too. Then the decomposers multiply on all that dead organic matter, and their aerobic respiration strips the oxygen out of the deep water. Fish and benthic invertebrates die of hypoxia, in water that is producing more oxygen at the surface than it ever did. Add the nocturnal respiration of the bloom itself, which pulls dissolved oxygen down every night, and cyanobacterial toxins on top. The headline to reject: "the algae use up the oxygen" is at best a quarter true — the decomposers do most of it, and they do it after the algae are dead. The same mechanism at river-mouth scale produces the coastal dead zones, of which the Gulf of Mexico hypoxic zone below the Mississippi is the standard example.

Ozone depletion and the greenhouse effect are two different problems

This pairing is a reliable source of distractors, so hold the two apart deliberately. Stratospheric ozone absorbs UV-B. Chlorine radicals released when UV splits CFCs destroy it catalytically — Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂, then ClO + O → Cl + O₂, regenerating the chlorine — so one chlorine atom accounts for on the order of a hundred thousand ozone molecules before it is scavenged. The consequence is more UV-B at the surface: skin cancer, cataracts, immunosuppression, and damage to phytoplankton and amphibian eggs. The fix was the Montreal Protocol of 1987, and the ozone layer is recovering. The greenhouse effect is a separate mechanism: gases transparent to incoming short-wave radiation absorb outgoing long-wave radiation and re-emit it downward. It is not caused by ozone loss and does not cause it. Two further points where the textbook shorthand is loose: CO₂ is not the most potent greenhouse gas per molecule — methane is roughly 28 times stronger and nitrous oxide over 250 times, on a hundred-year basis — but CO₂ dominates the forcing because of how much of it there is and how long it lasts. And water vapour is the largest greenhouse contributor overall, but it acts as a feedback rather than a driver: its concentration is set by temperature, so it amplifies a change rather than initiating one. Finally, ozone near the ground is a pollutant and a component of photochemical smog — good up high, bad nearby.

Island biogeography and reserve design

MacArthur and Wilson modelled the number of species on an island as an equilibrium between two rates. Immigration falls as the island fills up — each new arrival is less and less likely to be a species not already present — and is higher for islands near a mainland source. Extinction rises as the island fills up, because more species means each has a smaller population, and is lower on large islands. Where the two curves cross is the equilibrium species number. Note that it is a dynamic equilibrium: the identity of the species keeps turning over even while the count stays put, which is the prediction that distinguishes the model from a static one. The ranking it gives is large-and-near > large-and-far ≈ small-and-near > small-and-far, and the reason a habitat fragment behaves like an island is that the surrounding farmland or city is, to most of its species, sea.

Applying this to reserves produced the SLOSS debate — a Single Large Or Several Small reserve of the same total area? The honest answer is that it depends on what is being protected, and an exam option asserting either as a general rule is overclaiming. A single large reserve is better for wide-ranging species that need core area away from edges, and it holds larger, less extinction-prone populations. Several small reserves capture more habitat types, spread the risk of a single fire or epidemic, and may collectively hold more species where the landscape is heterogeneous. Two things are agreed regardless: movement corridors between fragments restore gene flow and allow recolonisation after local extinction — though they can also carry fire and disease — and reducing the edge-to-area ratio, a compact reserve rather than a long thin one, matters as much as raw hectares. Where money is limited, the standard triage tools are biodiversity hotspots (small areas combining exceptional endemism with exceptional threat) and umbrella species (protecting a wide-ranging species protects everything under its range). A flagship species is chosen for public appeal, which makes it a fundraising strategy rather than an ecological argument — a distinction the paper has been known to test.

§ 11

Animal behaviour

The ideaBehaviour is a trait like any other: it varies, it is partly heritable, and it affects how many offspring an animal leaves — so it evolves. That means every behaviour invites two quite different kinds of explanation, and confusing them is the main source of error here. One kind is about machinery: what stimulus triggered it, what nerves and hormones carried it out, how it developed in this individual. The other is about consequences: why animals that do this leave more descendants than animals that do not. Both are correct answers to "why"; they are answers to different questions. The hardest case is behaviour that helps someone else at the animal's own expense — and that turns out to have a clean solution once you count copies of genes rather than bodies.

Innate, learned, and why the dichotomy is false

The traditional split is between behaviour that appears fully formed without relevant experience — fixed action patterns, taxes and kineses, migration, many courtship displays — and behaviour modified by experience. It is a useful sorting device and the paper uses it. But the sentence "this behaviour is genetic, not learned" is, strictly, never right. Every behaviour requires both a genome and an environment to develop in, and the useful question is how much the outcome varies when you vary each. Even the most rigid FAP has a threshold that experience tunes; even the most flexible learning runs on innate machinery that constrains what can be learned. Rats learn to associate a novel taste with later nausea after a single pairing hours later, but cannot learn to associate a light with nausea at all — the mechanism is general-purpose only within limits set by evolution.

Foraging, mating and communication as economic problems

Optimal foraging theory treats a foraging animal as maximising net energy gain per unit time, subject to constraints. It works well enough to make quantitative predictions: crows carrying whelks aloft to drop them on rocks choose a height close to the one that minimises total flying effort per opened shell, because dropping from lower means more repeat trips. Where predictions fail, the usual reason is that the animal is also minimising something else — predation risk above all. A mule deer that feeds in the open forest edge rather than the richer meadow is not foraging badly; it is buying safety, and the correct model has two currencies.

Mating systems follow from who is needed to raise the young and from how certain paternity is. Where offspring are helpless and two parents are needed, monogamy is common. Where one parent suffices, the other — usually the male, whose minimum investment is smaller — is free to seek additional mates, and polygyny follows, bringing with it strong sexual selection and hence sexual dimorphism in size, weapons and ornament. Note the predictive rule about certainty of paternity: in species with external fertilisation, where a male can guard the eggs he has just fertilised, paternal care is common (many fish); with internal fertilisation, paternity is less certain and male care is rarer. Sexual selection itself splits into intrasexual (competition among members of one sex — antlers, fights, sperm competition) and intersexual (mate choice — the peacock's train, birdsong complexity), and the two produce different kinds of trait.

Communication is any transmission of a signal that alters the receiver's behaviour, and the modality is chosen by the physics of the habitat. Visual signals need line of sight and daylight; auditory signals travel around obstacles and work at night; chemical signals (pheromones) are cheap to produce, persist, and travel far, which is why they dominate long-distance mate attraction in moths and social organisation in ants and bees; tactile signals require contact and carry high information, as in the honeybee waggle dance, which encodes direction relative to the sun in the angle of the run and distance in its duration. A signal is only stable over evolutionary time if it is, on average, honest — which usually means it is costly to fake.

The problem of altruism

A behaviour that lowers the actor's own reproductive success and raises someone else's should be eliminated by natural selection, and yet alarm calls, helpers at the nest, and sterile worker castes exist. The wrong answer, and the most common one, is that such behaviour exists "for the good of the species". It cannot: a population of self-sacrificers is invaded and taken over by the first individual that accepts help and gives none. The right answer is to stop counting bodies and count gene copies. A gene that causes its bearer to help a relative is helping copies of itself, present in that relative with probability r. Hamilton's rule states the condition exactly: the behaviour spreads when rB > C. Haldane's remark that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins is the break-even case, since 2 × 0.5 = 8 × 0.125 = 1.

The evidence is specific rather than anecdotal. Belding's ground squirrels give alarm calls that demonstrably raise the caller's risk — and it is the females who call, because females remain near their birthplace and are therefore surrounded by kin, while males disperse and are surrounded by non-relatives. Change the relatedness and the behaviour changes with it, which is what makes it a test of the hypothesis rather than a story. Where relatedness is low, cooperation still occurs but requires a different mechanism: reciprocal altruism, which is stable only when individuals interact repeatedly, recognise each other, and can withhold help from cheats — the conditions under which a tit-for-tat strategy beats defection in an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma.

Exam technique · Hamilton's rule, and deriving r instead of storing it

  1. Build r on the spot: halve once per meiosis along the path, then add the independent paths. There is no 口訣 for the relatedness table and you should not want one. Parent–offspring is one step, so 0.5. A grandparent is two steps by one route, so 0.25. A half-sibling is two steps by one route, so 0.25. A full sibling is two steps by two routes — mother and father — so 2 × 0.25 = 0.5. A first cousin is four steps by two routes, 2 × 0.0625 = 0.125. The same rule delivers the values the distractors are built from: a first cousin once removed is five steps by two routes, 0.0625, and a second cousin six steps by two routes, 0.03125. Five seconds of counting beats a memorised ladder you cannot check.
  2. Then it is one line of arithmetic. Multiply each relative's benefit by its r, add them, compare the total with C. Two full sibs and four first cousins saved: 2(0.5) + 4(0.125) = 1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5, against a cost of 1.0 — so rB > C and the behaviour is favoured. Haldane's "two brothers or eight cousins" is the same sum at break-even, 2 × 0.5 = 8 × 0.125 = 1.
  3. Kill the wrong-ladder option by inspection. The standard distractor uses 0.25 for a full sibling and 0.0625 for a first cousin — every value shifted one rung down. If two option values are exactly half of two others, one of those pairs is the planted ladder, and the rule in step 1 tells you which.
  4. Kill the group-selection option on sight. Any option containing "for the good of the species", "benefits the population as a whole" or "regardless of relatedness" is wrong in this topic without exception — a population of unconditional self-sacrificers is invaded by the first individual that takes help and gives none. Equally, any option claiming a direct fitness loss can never be repaid is denying indirect fitness, which is half of inclusive fitness. That is often two of five gone before any arithmetic.
  5. If the stem varies who is nearby rather than giving numbers, it wants the Belding's test. Females stay near their birthplace and are surrounded by kin, so females call; males disperse and are surrounded by non-relatives, so they do not. Change the relatedness, change the behaviour — that is the prediction being tested, and no calculation is required.
  6. One exception to the halving rule, and it is examined. Haplodiploidy breaks it: because Hymenopteran males are haploid, full sisters share all of their father's genome, giving r = 0.75 between sisters, 0.25 to a brother, and the ordinary 0.5 to a daughter. Those three numbers do have to be stored, because you cannot get them by counting meioses.
Go deeper · eusociality, haplodiploidy, and why the textbook argument is not enough

The standard account runs: in the Hymenoptera (ants, bees, wasps) males are haploid and females diploid, so full sisters share their father's entire genome and half their mother's, giving r = 0.75 between sisters against only 0.5 to their own daughters. A worker therefore propagates more copies of her genes by raising sisters than by reproducing — and eusociality follows. It is an elegant argument and it is examined, so know it.

It is also insufficient, and being able to say why is the kind of thing that separates a confident answer from a memorised one. Three problems. First, the same haplodiploid worker is related to her brothers by only 0.25, so averaged over the brood she raises, her relatedness is back to 0.5 — the 0.75 advantage only survives if workers can bias the sex ratio towards sisters, which in some species they do and in others they do not. Second, termites are diploid and fully eusocial, as are naked mole-rats, so haplodiploidy is plainly not necessary. Third, most haplodiploid insects are not eusocial at all, so it is not sufficient either.

The better-supported modern account is the monogamy hypothesis: eusociality has arisen only in lineages whose ancestral females were strictly lifetime-monogamous. Under lifetime monogamy a female is related to her siblings by exactly 0.5 — the same as to her own offspring — so the kin-selected cost of helping instead of breeding is zero, and any small efficiency gain from cooperative brood care (a defensible nest, division of labour, insurance against the death of a single foundress) tips the balance. Haplodiploidy then makes it easier still, but it is a facilitator rather than the cause. The general lesson is worth more than the example: Hamilton's rule is the framework, and arguments about r are only as good as the full accounting of B and C that accompanies them.

Behaviour sentences the paper likes and you should refuse

"Imprinting is a form of classical conditioning." It has no unconditioned stimulus and needs no reinforcement, and it is confined to a sensitive period and effectively irreversible — none of which is true of conditioning. "Habituation is fatigue." It is learning: a habituated animal responds immediately to a different stimulus, which a fatigued one could not. "Innate behaviours are unaffected by the environment." No behaviour develops without an environment; what is innate is the reliability with which it develops across the normal range. "Animals behave for the good of the species." Selection has no such foresight; the correct framework is inclusive fitness. "Altruism proves that group selection operates." Apparent altruism among relatives is explained by kin selection without invoking group selection at all. "Learned behaviour is more advanced than innate behaviour." Each is favoured under different conditions: innate responses are cheap, fast and right the first time, which is what you want when a mistake is fatal and the situation is predictable; learning is expensive and slow but tracks a changing environment. A short-lived insect that must get it right once has every reason to be hard-wired.
