Biochemistry · Note 07

Lipid Metabolism, Cholesterol and Lipoproteins

A fatty acid is a long row of –CH2– groups, and almost everything here follows from that. Because those carbons are nearly fully reduced, burning them releases more energy per carbon than anything else the body stores; because they are nearly featureless, the cell can only take them apart — and only build them — two carbons at a time. That gives a spiral rather than a linear pathway, an ATP yield that is a formula rather than a number, an acetyl-CoA that can never become glucose again, and a molecule so insoluble that transporting it needs a protein-coated particle. The same two-carbon units also become cholesterol, membrane lipids and every prostaglandin you will ever prescribe against.

§ 01

Getting fatty acids out and switched on

The ideaFat sits as a dense greasy droplet inside a fat cell, and it has to be cut up, floated through watery blood and handed to a muscle before any of it can be burned. Three problems, in that order: dismantle the stored molecule, carry the greasy pieces through water, then tag each piece so the cell that received it cannot let it drift out again. The tagging step costs energy, and it is why a fatty acid is committed the moment it enters a cell.

Definition & key reaction

Lipolysis（脂解作用）
Hydrolysis of stored triacylglycerol into three free fatty acids and glycerol, carried out inside the adipocyte by three lipases acting in sequence on the surface of the lipid droplet.
fatty acid + CoA-SH + ATP → acyl-CoA + AMP + PPi activation: one ATP molecule but two high-energy phosphate bonds
Full definition, symbols and units
Free fatty acid (FFA / NEFA)
A fatty acid not esterified to glycerol. Long-chain FFAs are essentially insoluble in water, so plasma FFA travels non-covalently bound to serum albumin — about seven binding sites per molecule, of which only one or two are occupied at rest and three or more during active lipolysis. Turnover is fast: the plasma half-life is 1–2 minutes.
Acyl-CoA synthetase (fatty acid–CoA ligase, ACSL)
Sits on the outer mitochondrial membrane and on the ER. Forms a thioester between the fatty acid carboxyl and the thiol of coenzyme A, through an acyl-adenylate intermediate — so ATP is cleaved to AMP + PPi, and inorganic pyrophosphatase then hydrolyses the PPi. Charge acyl-CoA formation at 2 ATP equivalents, never 1.
Perilipin-1
The coat protein of the adipocyte lipid droplet. Unphosphorylated it is a barrier and it sequesters the ATGL coactivator CGI-58; phosphorylated by PKA it releases CGI-58 and admits the lipases. It is the gate, not an enzyme.

Note 01 § 08 and § 09 already dealt with what a triacylglycerol is, why it packs into an anhydrous droplet while glycogen drags roughly three grams of water per gram of sugar, and how chain saturation sets melting point. That structural material is not repeated here. This note starts one step later, at the moment the droplet has to be taken apart.

Three lipases, one gate

The classical name hormone-sensitive lipase is a historical accident. The enzyme that makes the first cut — triacylglycerol to diacylglycerol — is adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), and it sets the basal rate; HSL then works mostly on diacylglycerol, and monoacylglycerol lipase finishes. What hormones actually control is less the lipases than the coat. PKA phosphorylates perilipin-1, which both opens the droplet surface to HSL and releases the ATGL coactivator CGI-58. One phosphorylation therefore switches on two enzymes at once, which is why lipolysis can rise more than tenfold within minutes of an adrenaline surge.

Insulin turns it off by a route worth remembering, because caffeine exploits the same route in the opposite direction: insulin activates phosphodiesterase 3B, which destroys cAMP, while methylxanthines such as caffeine and theophylline inhibit phosphodiesterases so cAMP survives longer. Neither hormone touches the lipase itself — what changes is the lifetime of the second messenger.

Glycerol has exactly one destination, and it is not the fat cell

Adipose tissue lacks glycerol kinase. Glycerol released by lipolysis cannot be re-used locally; it leaves in the blood and is phosphorylated in liver (and kidney), entering gluconeogenesis or glycolysis as dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Two consequences, both examined. Plasma glycerol is therefore a clean marker of whole-body lipolysis. And to re-esterify fatty acids the adipocyte must build glycerol-3-phosphate from glucose via DHAP — so fat storage is chemically dependent on carbohydrate uptake. That is why insulin is the storage signal, and why an adipocyte cut off from glucose cannot store fat even when fatty acids are abundant.

Dietary fat, in one paragraph

Bile salts emulsify dietary triacylglycerol; pancreatic lipase, held on the droplet surface by colipase, hydrolyses positions 1 and 3 to give 2-monoacylglycerol plus two free fatty acids; mixed micelles ferry those to the enterocyte brush border. Inside the enterocyte they are re-esterified and packaged — with apoB-48, and with the help of microsomal triglyceride transfer protein — into a chylomicron, which leaves by lymph rather than by the portal vein because the particle is far too large to cross a capillary endothelium. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids skip all of this: they are water-soluble enough to enter portal blood directly, bound to albumin. That single exception is the entire basis of MCT feeds, which return in § 02 and § 05. Orlistat, incidentally, acts at the very first step — inhibit pancreatic lipase and the triacylglycerol is never hydrolysed, so it passes through unabsorbed.

Why activation costs two, not one

Acyl-CoA synthetase does not use ATP the way a kinase does. It attacks the α-phosphate, displacing pyrophosphate and forming a fatty acyl–AMP intermediate; CoA-SH then displaces AMP. The products are therefore AMP + PPi: two phosphoanhydride bonds spent. That is exactly what the "2 ATP equivalents" means — AMP has to be rephosphorylated twice to become ATP again, first by adenylate kinase (ATP + AMP → 2 ADP) and then by oxidative phosphorylation. It is two, not three: the adenylate kinase step is the second equivalent, not an extra one on top. Inorganic pyrophosphatase then hydrolyses the PPi, and that strongly exergonic mop-up is what makes activation effectively irreversible — the same pull-it-forward trick used by every polymerase in note 05 and by every aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase.

Where the mark is actually lost

Every fatty-acid ATP question turns on two things: activation costs 2 ATP equivalents, and it is charged once per molecule, not once per turn of the spiral. An option offering 108 for palmitate belongs to someone who forgot the activation entirely; an option offering 94 belongs to someone who charged it seven times. Both appear on real papers, because both are what a hurried candidate produces under time pressure.

§ 02

The carnitine shuttle and the malonyl-CoA brake

The ideaThe machinery that burns fat sits inside the mitochondrion, but an activated fatty acid cannot cross the inner membrane — the very tag that switched it on is what blocks it. So the cell swaps that tag for a smaller one, carries the molecule across on a dedicated ferry, and swaps it back on the far side. Because the swap is the only way in, whoever controls the swap controls fat burning completely. And the control signal the cell chose is a molecule made by the pathway that builds fat, so the two can never run fast at the same time.

Definition & controlling step

Carnitine shuttle（肉鹼穿梭）
The three-protein system that carries long-chain (C14–C20) acyl groups from cytosolic acyl-CoA into the mitochondrial matrix as acylcarnitine esters, regenerating matrix acyl-CoA at the far end.
acyl-CoA + carnitine ⇌[CPT-1] acylcarnitine + CoA-SH rate-limiting step of fatty-acid oxidation; inhibited by malonyl-CoA
Full definition, symbols and units
CPT-1 (carnitine palmitoyltransferase I)
Outer mitochondrial membrane, active site facing the cytosol. Three isoforms: CPT-1A (liver), CPT-1B (muscle and heart), CPT-1C (brain). The muscle isoform is far more sensitive to malonyl-CoA than the liver isoform — by roughly two orders of magnitude — which is why muscle fatty-acid oxidation shuts down so sharply after a carbohydrate meal.
CACT (carnitine–acylcarnitine translocase, SLC25A20)
An inner-membrane antiporter: one acylcarnitine in, one free carnitine out, strictly one-for-one, so the matrix carnitine pool is conserved.
CPT-2
Inner membrane, active site facing the matrix. Runs the reverse transesterification — acylcarnitine + CoA-SH → acyl-CoA + carnitine.

Why the door is where it is

Putting the control point at the membrane rather than inside the spiral is a design decision, and it is worth seeing why it is the right one. β-Oxidation is a spiral: the product of each turn is the substrate of the next. Regulate an enzyme inside the spiral and you regulate every turn simultaneously, accumulating half-processed intermediates. Regulate entry and the matrix either receives substrate or does not, and the spiral itself can be left to run flat out whenever it has something to run on. Commit early, commit once — the same logic as any committed step in a branched pathway.

The signal chosen is beautifully economical. Malonyl-CoA is the product of acetyl-CoA carboxylase, the committed enzyme of fatty-acid synthesis (§ 07). Its concentration is high precisely when the cell is building fat and low precisely when it is not. So one metabolite guarantees that synthesis and oxidation are never simultaneously fast, and the cell never wastes the round trip — building palmitate for 42 ATP equivalents and burning it back for 106 in the same minute. Muscle makes the point even more sharply: it carries the isoform ACC2, whose malonyl-CoA has essentially no synthetic fate, since muscle has almost no fatty-acid synthase. That malonyl-CoA exists only to speak to CPT-1.

Two shuttles, opposite directions — never fuse them

The carnitine shuttle carries acyl groups into the matrix so they can be oxidised. The citrate shuttle (§ 07) carries acetyl groups out of the matrix so they can be built into fat. Different cargo, opposite direction, opposite purpose. A stem that says "acetyl-CoA is carried into the mitochondrion by carnitine" is testing exactly this, and it is wrong twice over.

What happens when the shuttle fails

Every defect in this pathway produces the same physiological signature, and once you can derive it you never need to memorise a single one of these diseases. Block fatty-acid oxidation and, during a fast, three things happen at once. Ketone bodies cannot be made, because they are made from the acetyl-CoA that β-oxidation supplies. Gluconeogenesis stalls, because it needs the ATP and NADH that β-oxidation supplies and because pyruvate carboxylase requires acetyl-CoA as an allosteric activator. And tissues that normally live on fat — heart, skeletal muscle — fall back on glucose, consuming it faster. The result is hypoketotic hypoglycaemia: a low blood glucose with inappropriately low ketones. A hypoglycaemic child with high ketones has some other problem; a hypoglycaemic child with no ketones has a fatty-acid oxidation defect until proved otherwise.

§ 03

β-Oxidation: one spiral, four reactions

The ideaTo break a long, featureless hydrocarbon chain you first have to create a weak point in it. The cell does this the same way twice: put a double bond in, add water across it, then oxidise the new alcohol to a ketone. A carbon two places along from the business end is now a ketone, and a ketone next to a thioester is easy to snip. Cut there, take two carbons off, and you are holding a chain exactly like the one you started with — only shorter. So the pathway is not a line, it is a loop that feeds itself, and the chain spirals down two carbons at a time.

Definition & the four reactions

β-Oxidation（β-氧化）
The mitochondrial spiral that shortens a fatty acyl-CoA by two carbons per turn, oxidising the β-carbon (C3) to a ketone before cleaving it off as acetyl-CoA. Each turn releases one FADH2, one NADH and one acetyl-CoA.
Cn-acyl-CoA + FAD + NAD⁺ + H₂O + CoA-SH → Cn−2-acyl-CoA + acetyl-CoA + FADH₂ + NADH + H⁺ one turn of the spiral; repeat until only acetyl-CoA is left
Full definition, symbols and units
1 · Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (FAD)
Creates a trans-Δ² double bond. Four chain-length-specific isoforms — VLCAD (bound to the inner membrane), LCAD, MCAD, SCAD. The FADH2 does not hand its electrons to complex I; it passes them to electron-transferring flavoprotein (ETF) and then to ETF–ubiquinone oxidoreductase, entering the chain at coenzyme Q. That is exactly why it is worth 1.5 ATP and not 2.5.
2 · Enoyl-CoA hydratase
Adds water across the double bond, stereospecifically, giving L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA. Remember the L: fatty-acid synthesis makes the D isomer (§ 07).
3 · L-3-Hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (NAD⁺)
Oxidises the new hydroxyl to a ketone, giving 3-ketoacyl-CoA and NADH.
4 · β-Ketothiolase (thiolase)
A second CoA-SH attacks the C3 carbonyl; the chain breaks between C2 and C3, releasing acetyl-CoA and leaving an acyl-CoA two carbons shorter, already activated and ready for the next turn. No further ATP is spent — this is why activation is a one-off cost.

Reading the chemistry rather than remembering it

You can reconstruct the four steps from first principles if you ask what has to happen. A saturated chain is inert — there is no electrophile to attack, so nothing can cleave it. The pathway therefore has to install reactivity, and the cheapest way to make a carbon electrophilic is to give it a carbonyl. Getting from –CH₂– to –C=O takes exactly three moves: dehydrogenate to make an alkene, hydrate the alkene to an alcohol, oxidise the alcohol to a ketone. Now C3 is a ketone flanked by a thioester at C1, so C2 is doubly activated and a thiolate can attack C3 cleanly. The result is a retro-Claisen cleavage, which is precisely the reverse of the condensation used to build fatty acids in § 07 — a symmetry worth holding on to.

Two details are worth a moment because examiners use them as discriminators. First, the two oxidation steps use different electron acceptors, and not arbitrarily: making a C=C from a C–C is worth less free energy than making a C=O from a C–OH, so the first oxidation is coupled to the weaker acceptor (FAD) and the second to NAD⁺. That is a general rule, not a fact about fatty acids — succinate dehydrogenase uses FAD for the same reason. Second, the FADH2 here never reaches complex I; ETF delivers it to ubiquinone, which is why it buys 1.5 ATP. If a stem tells you that β-oxidation's FADH2 yields 2.5 ATP, that is the error being tested.

The spiral is aerobic, all the way down

Nothing in β-oxidation makes ATP directly — there is no substrate-level phosphorylation anywhere in it. Every ATP comes from re-oxidising FADH2 and NADH, and from burning the acetyl-CoA in the TCA cycle, all of which need oxygen. So fatty acids are useless as a fuel under anaerobic conditions, and useless to a cell with no mitochondria. Red blood cells cannot oxidise fatty acids at all. That is also why fat cannot power a sprint: the pathway is high-yield but slow and strictly aerobic, whereas glycolysis is low-yield, fast and works without oxygen.

Go deeper · where β-oxidation is regulated once the substrate is inside

CPT-1 governs entry, but the spiral itself is not entirely passive. Three feedback loops matter.

  • NADH/NAD⁺ ratio. Step 3 needs NAD⁺. If the respiratory chain is not consuming NADH — hypoxia, or the enormous NADH load produced by ethanol oxidation — step 3 stalls and β-oxidation slows. This is a large part of why alcohol causes hepatic steatosis: fatty acids arrive but cannot be burned, so they are esterified and stored.
  • Acetyl-CoA / CoA-SH ratio. Thiolysis needs free CoA-SH. When acetyl-CoA accumulates because the TCA cycle is saturated — or because oxaloacetate has been drained into gluconeogenesis — free CoA falls and the spiral backs up. In the liver the overflow is diverted into ketone bodies (§ 06), which is the elegant solution to exactly this problem.
  • Malonyl-CoA, continuously. Because CPT-1 is inhibited rather than switched, the flux is graded: hepatic malonyl-CoA falls progressively over the first hours of a fast, and fatty-acid oxidation rises progressively with it. Nothing here is a binary switch.

Peroxisome-proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) sits above all of this as the transcriptional layer: fasting and fibrate drugs activate it, and it induces CPT-1, the acyl-CoA dehydrogenases, HMG-CoA synthase 2 and lipoprotein lipase together. One transcription factor, the whole fat-burning programme — which is also why fibrates lower plasma triacylglycerol (§ 10).

§ 04

The ATP arithmetic, as a method

The ideaDo not memorise that palmitate gives 106. Memorise how to get there, because the exam will hand you a different chain and the memorised number will be useless. There are only four quantities to count — how many times the loop runs, how many two-carbon pieces come off, and the two kinds of electron carrier the loop produces — and one fixed charge for switching the molecule on at the start. Count those and the answer falls out for any fatty acid at all.

Formula & the counting rules

The counting rules for a saturated, even-numbered chain of n carbons
Rounds of the spiral = n/2 − 1. Acetyl-CoA produced = n/2. FADH2 = NADH = number of rounds. Activation costs 2 ATP equivalents, once. The last round produces two acetyl-CoA, which is why rounds are one fewer than you first expect.
ATPnet = (n/2 − 1)(1.5 + 2.5) + (n/2)(10) − 2 = 7n − 6 saturated, even-numbered chain, Lehninger 8e P/O values, complete oxidation to CO₂ and H₂O
Full definition, symbols and units
Why 7n − 6 and not something you must look up
Expand it once and it stops being magic. Each round gives 1.5 + 2.5 = 4 ATP, so the rounds contribute 4(n/2 − 1) = 2n − 4. Each acetyl-CoA is worth 10 ATP in the TCA cycle, contributing 10(n/2) = 5n. Subtract the 2 for activation: 2n − 4 + 5n − 2 = 7n − 6. The formula is a derivation, not a fact.
The P/O convention matters
Lehninger 8th edition (and every recent paper) uses NADH = 2.5, FADH2 = 1.5, and hence acetyl-CoA = 10 in the TCA cycle. Older textbooks used 3, 2 and 12, which gives 129 for palmitate rather than 106. If a stem states its convention, use the stem's; if not, use 2.5/1.5 and expect 106.
Where the acetyl-CoA's 10 comes from
One turn of the TCA cycle: 3 NADH (7.5) + 1 FADH2 (1.5) + 1 GTP (1) = 10. This is covered in note 06; here it is simply the exchange rate.

Do it once, slowly, on palmitate

Palmitate is C16, so the chain must be cut into eight two-carbon pieces. Cutting a rope into eight pieces takes seven cuts, not eight — and that single sentence is the whole reason rounds are n/2 − 1. The seventh round starts with a four-carbon butyryl-CoA and produces two acetyl-CoA at once, which is where the extra one comes from.

Double bonds are modelled the way natural fatty acids actually present them — alternating odd- and even-numbered positions after chain-shortening, so the first costs 1.5 ATP (an isomerase replaces a dehydrogenase) and the second costs 2.5 ATP (an NADPH is consumed by 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase). Worked example 1 — palmitate, and turning it into a general rule

(a) Compute the net ATP yield from the complete oxidation of one molecule of palmitate (16:0). (b) Derive a formula valid for any saturated even-numbered chain and test it on butyrate (4:0) and stearate (18:0).

  1. (a) Eight acetyl-CoA means seven cleavages, so 7 rounds.
  2. Each round: 1 FADH2 (1.5) + 1 NADH (2.5) = 4 ATP. 7 × 4 = 28 ATP.
  3. Eight acetyl-CoA through the TCA cycle: 8 × 10 = 80 ATP.
  4. Gross 28 + 80 = 108; activation cost 2 (ATP → AMP + PPi), charged once. 108 − 2 = 106 ATP.
  5. (b) Generalise: rounds = n/2 − 1, acetyl-CoA = n/2, so ATP = 4(n/2 − 1) + 10(n/2) − 2 = 2n − 4 + 5n − 2 = 7n − 6.
  6. Test on butyrate, n = 4: 7(4) − 6 = 22. Check the long way — 1 round (1 FADH2 + 1 NADH = 4) and 2 acetyl-CoA (20), gross 24, minus 2 = 22 ✓.
  7. Test on stearate, n = 18: 7(18) − 6 = 120. Long way — 8 rounds (32) plus 9 acetyl-CoA (90) = 122, minus 2 = 120 ✓.

The three ways this arithmetic goes wrong

One. Using n/2 rounds instead of n/2 − 1 — this is the commonest: eight rounds instead of seven adds one spurious FADH2 and one spurious NADH, so a C16 answer comes out as 112 − 2 = 110 instead of 106. Two. Charging activation per round, or forgetting it entirely: the two errors give 94 and 108 respectively for palmitate, and both usually appear as options. Three. Mixing conventions — counting acetyl-CoA at 12 while counting FADH2 at 1.5. Decide on 2.5/1.5/10 at the start of the question and stay there. A fourth, subtler one: assuming the FADH2 from β-oxidation is worth 2.5 because it "comes from a dehydrogenase". It enters at ubiquinone, so it is 1.5.

§ 05

Awkward chains: unsaturated, odd, very long, branched

The ideaThe four-step loop only works on a plain, even, medium-length chain. Real fats are not always like that. A kink in the wrong place, an odd number of carbons, a chain too long to fit the machinery, a methyl branch sticking out — each defeats one specific step, and for each the cell keeps one small accessory enzyme that fixes exactly that problem and nothing else. Learn which step is defeated and the accessory enzyme is obvious; learn the enzyme names first and nothing sticks.

Definition & the four exceptions

Auxiliary enzymes of β-oxidation

Unsaturated chains: the position of the kink decides the penalty

Natural unsaturated fatty acids have cis double bonds; the spiral needs a trans-Δ² bond. So the question is never "does a double bond cost energy" but "where does the double bond end up once the chain has been shortened to reach it". Chain-shortening moves the bond two carbons closer to the thioester each round, so a bond that started at an odd-numbered position stays odd, and one that started even stays even.

An odd-position bond arrives at Δ3. The isomerase simply repositions it to trans-Δ², so the chain enters at step 2 and the FADH2 of that round is never made — lose 1.5 ATP. An even-position bond arrives at Δ4. Step 1 runs, so the FADH2 is made, but the resulting 2,4-diene needs an NADPH from the reductase before the isomerase can finish the job — lose about 2.5 ATP. The counterintuitive result is worth stating explicitly: the even-numbered bond is the more expensive one, even though it is the one that keeps its FADH2.

Worked example 3 — oleate, linoleate and an odd chain

Odd chains: three carbons that behave completely differently from two

Every fatty acid with an odd number of carbons ends the same way: the final round cleaves a five-carbon acyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA. Propionyl-CoA has three carbons, and three carbons are enough to reach the TCA cycle as a genuine anaplerotic input rather than as fuel. That one difference carries the entire clinical weight of this section.

The route is short and every step is examinable. Propionyl-CoA carboxylase, using biotin and ATP, adds a carboxyl to give D-methylmalonyl-CoA. A racemase (epimerase) inverts it to the L form. Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, using adenosylcobalamin, rearranges the carbon skeleton into succinyl-CoA. Succinyl-CoA is a four-carbon TCA intermediate, so it can leave the cycle as malate, become oxaloacetate and then phosphoenolpyruvate, and become glucose.

"Can fatty acids be converted to glucose?" — answer it precisely

For the ordinary even-chain fatty acid the answer is no, and the reason is structural, not regulatory: acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle by condensing with a four-carbon oxaloacetate, and by the end of one turn two carbons have left as CO2. There is no net gain of oxaloacetate, so nothing can be drained off to gluconeogenesis. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is also physiologically irreversible, so you cannot simply run back. Plants get round this with the glyoxylate cycle; animals have no such pathway.
But two parts of a triacylglycerol molecule can reach glucose: the glycerol backbone, and the propionyl-CoA from an odd chain, because both enter below acetyl-CoA and add net carbon to the four-carbon pool. (Note the wording: two parts of the molecule, not two carbon atoms — glycerol contributes all three of its carbons.) A stem that says "no part of a fat molecule can become glucose" is wrong; a stem that says "acetyl-CoA cannot become glucose" is right.

The clinical use of that pathway is the cleanest vitamin-diagnosis question in all of metabolism. Block the mutase — by B12 deficiency — and methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates and is hydrolysed to methylmalonic acid, which rises in plasma and urine. Vitamin B12 also serves methionine synthase, so B12 deficiency also raises homocysteine. Folate deficiency, however, blocks only methionine synthase, so it raises homocysteine but leaves methylmalonic acid normal. Both produce an identical megaloblastic anaemia; only B12 deficiency produces subacute combined degeneration of the cord, and only B12 deficiency raises MMA. Measuring methylmalonic acid separates them — and the reason it works is a fatty-acid pathway.

Peroxisomes: the pre-processing plant

Very-long-chain fatty acids above about C22 are not accepted by the mitochondrial dehydrogenases at all. They are handled first in the peroxisome, which runs the same four-step chemistry with one crucial change: the first oxidation is done by acyl-CoA oxidase, which passes its electrons straight to O2 to make hydrogen peroxide, disposed of by catalase. Those electrons never reach the respiratory chain, so that step yields no ATP — the energy appears as heat. Peroxisomal β-oxidation shortens the chain to about C8 and then hands it to the mitochondrion as a carnitine ester. Peroxisomes also do not go to completion; they are a pre-processing plant, not a furnace.

Two further peroxisomal jobs matter clinically. α-Oxidation deals with phytanic acid, a chlorophyll-derived branched fatty acid from dairy and ruminant fat whose methyl group on the β-carbon leaves no hydrogen for acyl-CoA dehydrogenase to abstract. Phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase opens a short three-enzyme sequence — hydroxylate the α-carbon, cleave that carbon off as formyl-CoA (which ends up as CO2), oxidise the remaining aldehyde back to an acid — after which the methyl branch sits at an α-position and ordinary β-oxidation can proceed. "Loses one carbon as CO2" is the usual shorthand for that sequence, not a single decarboxylation. And peroxisomes build plasmalogens, the ether phospholipids that make up a large share of myelin and cardiac membrane.

§ 06

Ketone bodies

The ideaThe brain cannot burn fat, because fatty acids do not cross into it usefully and it has no way to store them. But during a long fast the liver has more two-carbon units than it can burn and the brain has almost no glucose left. So the liver welds pairs of those units into a small, water-soluble molecule that travels freely in blood and crosses into the brain, where it is unwelded again and burned. Ketone bodies are not a pathological accident — they are a transport format for fat, invented to feed an organ that cannot receive fat directly.

Making them, and the trap in the middle of the pathway

The sequence is short: thiolase runs backwards to join two acetyl-CoA into acetoacetyl-CoA; HMG-CoA synthase adds a third acetyl-CoA to give β-hydroxy-β-methylglutaryl-CoA; HMG-CoA lyase then splits off acetoacetate and releases the third acetyl-CoA again. Net, three acetyl-CoA in, one acetoacetate and one acetyl-CoA out. Acetoacetate is then either reduced to β-hydroxybutyrate or spontaneously decarboxylated to acetone.

HMG-CoA appears twice on this page, in two compartments

Mitochondrial HMG-CoA, made by HMGCS2 and cleaved by HMG-CoA lyase, is the ketone-body pathway. Cytosolic HMG-CoA, made by HMGCS1 and reduced by HMG-CoA reductase, is the cholesterol pathway (§ 09). Same molecule, different compartment, opposite fate, and completely different regulation — a statin does not affect ketogenesis. HMG-CoA also appears a third time, as an intermediate of leucine catabolism in the mitochondrion, which is precisely why leucine is a purely ketogenic amino acid. If a question asks "which of these is the substrate of HMG-CoA reductase", the answer is a cytosolic pool that the ketone pathway never touches.

Using them, and why the liver is excluded

In a peripheral cell, β-hydroxybutyrate is oxidised back to acetoacetate, which then has to be re-activated with a CoA. The cell does not spend an ATP on this: SCOT steals a CoA from succinyl-CoA, which costs the cell the GTP that succinyl-CoA synthetase would otherwise have made. Thiolase then splits acetoacetyl-CoA into two acetyl-CoA. So acetoacetate is worth about 2 × 10 − 1 = 19 ATP, and β-hydroxybutyrate about 19 + 2.5 = 21.5 ATP because it delivers an extra NADH on the way in.

The liver lacks SCOT entirely. This is not an oversight — it is what makes the system work. If liver could consume ketone bodies it would consume its own product, and no ketones would ever reach the brain. An organ that exports a fuel must be unable to burn it, which is the same reason the liver exports glucose while muscle cannot (muscle has no glucose-6-phosphatase). Recognising that pattern answers a whole family of questions.

Worked example 4 — fuel reserves, and what the numbers force the body to do

A 70 kg adult carries roughly 15 kg of triacylglycerol and about 500 g of glycogen (100 g hepatic, 400 g muscle), and uses about 2000 kcal per day. (a) How long does each store last on its own? (b) The brain uses about 120 g of glucose a day. What does that force the body to do, and how do ketone bodies change it?

  1. (a) Fat: 15 000 g × 9 kcal·g⁻¹ = 135 000 kcal, i.e. 135 000 / 2000 ≈ 68 days.
  2. Glycogen: 500 g × 4 kcal·g⁻¹ = 2000 kcal — about one day. And muscle glycogen cannot leave muscle, so the systemically useful store is the 100 g in liver, worth a few hours.
  3. Add the storage water: glycogen is hydrated at roughly 3 g water per gram, so its effective density is about 4/(1+3) = 1 kcal per gram of wet tissue against 9 for fat — a ninefold difference. Storing 135 000 kcal as glycogen would require roughly 135 kg of hydrated tissue.
  4. (b) The brain's 120 g·day⁻¹ cannot come from fat, because acetyl-CoA cannot become glucose (§ 05). With hepatic glycogen gone within a day, glucose must be made from amino acids — and the measured rate of muscle protein breakdown in early fasting is about 75 g·day⁻¹, the classic 12 g of urinary nitrogen. It is not the ~200 g a day that 120 g of glucose would need outright, because glycerol and Cori-cycle lactate carry part of the load and the brain starts switching over almost immediately. Even 75 g·day⁻¹ costs functional muscle within two to three weeks, long before the fat runs out.
  5. Ketone bodies solve exactly this. After 3–4 days the brain begins to oxidise them, and after several weeks they supply roughly two-thirds of its energy, cutting its glucose requirement to about 40 g·day⁻¹. Protein breakdown falls to about 20 g·day⁻¹ — a protein-sparing effect that converts survival time from weeks into the two months the fat store actually allows.
  6. Note what still cannot use them: erythrocytes have no mitochondria, and the renal medulla, cornea and lens are equally committed to glycolysis, so the ~40 g·day⁻¹ of glucose they consume between them is obligatory no matter how long the fast.

Three clinical facts that are really biochemistry

1 · The dipstick lies in severe DKA. The nitroprusside reaction detects acetoacetate and acetone but not β-hydroxybutyrate. In severe ketoacidosis the high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio pushes the equilibrium towards β-hydroxybutyrate, so the dipstick underestimates the ketone load — and then appears to worsen during successful treatment as β-hydroxybutyrate is reoxidised back to acetoacetate. Measure serum β-hydroxybutyrate instead.
2 · Alcoholic ketoacidosis behaves the same way, for the same reason: ethanol oxidation floods the cell with NADH, so ketones are overwhelmingly β-hydroxybutyrate and the dipstick can be nearly negative. The same NADH excess simultaneously blocks gluconeogenesis, which is why these patients are hypoglycaemic rather than hyperglycaemic.
3 · Ketone bodies are acids, and the acidosis is theirs. With pKa values near 3.6 and 4.4 they are fully dissociated at pH 7.4, so each one contributes a proton and an unmeasured anion — a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Acetone contributes none, being neutral.

§ 07

Building fatty acids: the committed step

The ideaMaking a fatty acid is not simply running the breakdown backwards, and the reason is thermodynamic: joining two-carbon units head to tail is uphill, so the cell cheats. It temporarily adds a carbon dioxide to one of the units, which makes the joint easy to form, and then throws that same carbon dioxide away at the moment of joining. The molecule that carries the temporary extra carbon is the entire control point of fat synthesis — and, as § 02 showed, it is also the molecule that shuts down fat burning. One metabolite, both halves of the switch.

The carboxylation trick

Condensing two acetyl units directly, as a Claisen condensation, is thermodynamically unfavourable — which is exactly why the reverse reaction (thiolysis, step 4 of β-oxidation) runs so readily. The cell gets round this by making one partner into malonyl-CoA. Now the condensation is coupled to a decarboxylation: the carboxyl group that ACC installed leaves as CO2 at the moment the bond forms, and that loss of a gas makes the overall step strongly exergonic. The ATP spent by ACC is, in effect, the price of pulling the condensation forward.

The labelling question that follows directly

The CO2 fixed by acetyl-CoA carboxylase is the same CO2 released in the condensation. It is borrowed, not incorporated. So if you supply 14C-labelled bicarbonate to a lipogenic cell, the palmitate produced is unlabelled, even though every carbon of it passed through malonyl-CoA. Supply 14C-labelled acetyl-CoA instead and every carbon is labelled. Examiners love this because it separates people who know the mechanism from people who know the map.

One elongation cycle, and why it is not β-oxidation in reverse

Each cycle adds two carbons and runs: condense (β-ketoacyl-ACP synthase, releasing CO2), reduce the new ketone with NADPH to give a D-3-hydroxyacyl-ACP, dehydrate to a trans-Δ²-enoyl-ACP, and reduce again with NADPH to the saturated chain. Seven cycles take the chain from acetyl to palmitoyl, at which point a thioesterase hydrolyses it off — and C16 is where the enzyme stops. Everything longer is made by separate ER elongases.

Notice how many things are inverted relative to § 03, and notice that none of the inversions is arbitrary. Different compartment (cytosol, so the two pathways cannot even meet). Different carrier (ACP, not CoA, so the intermediates are physically held inside one complex). Different cofactor (NADPH, not NAD⁺ and FAD — the cell keeps its NADPH pool highly reduced and its NADH pool oxidised, precisely so that biosynthesis and catabolism can run under different driving forces). And different stereochemistry at the 3-hydroxy intermediate, D rather than L, which is the single cleanest way an examiner can force you to prove you know the two pathways are separate enzymes rather than one reversible one.

Worked example 5 — what palmitate costs to build, and the efficiency of storing energy as fat

(a) How many ATP and NADPH are consumed in making one palmitate from cytosolic acetyl-CoA? (b) Express that in ATP equivalents, and compute the round-trip efficiency of storing energy as fat and getting it back.

  1. (a) Palmitate is C16, so it is built from 8 acetyl units. One is the primer; the other seven must be carboxylated to malonyl-CoA, at 1 ATP each — 7 ATP.
  2. Each of the seven elongation cycles uses two NADPH (one at the ketoacyl reductase, one at the enoyl reductase): 7 × 2 = 14 NADPH.
  3. Overall: 8 acetyl-CoA + 7 ATP + 14 NADPH → palmitate.
  4. (b) Valuing NADPH at the same 2.5 ATP as NADH: 7 + 14 × 2.5 = 42 ATP equivalents.
  5. But the eight acetyl-CoA were themselves worth 8 × 10 = 80 ATP had they been burned. Total chemical potential invested = 80 + 42 = 122 ATP equivalents.
  6. Oxidising the palmitate returns 106. Round-trip efficiency = 106/122 = 0.87, i.e. about 87 %.
  7. Two readings of that number. It is remarkably good for a storage system — but it also means a futile synthesis-and-oxidation cycle would waste roughly 13 % of the energy passing through it, which is exactly the waste the malonyl-CoA switch of § 02 exists to prevent.
  8. One more accounting point, easy to miss: the citrate shuttle costs an extra ATP per acetyl group exported (at ATP-citrate lyase), and returns roughly one NADPH per acetyl group (at malic enzyme). If a question asks you to include transport, add 8 ATP and subtract 8 of the 14 NADPH from the pentose-phosphate requirement.

Regulating ACC, on three timescales

The same enzyme is controlled allosterically in seconds, covalently in minutes and transcriptionally in hours, and the three layers all say the same thing.

  • Allosteric. Citrate activates, by polymerising ACC into long active filaments; citrate accumulates when the mitochondrion has more acetyl-CoA and ATP than it can use, so it is a direct report of energy surplus. Palmitoyl-CoA — the end product — depolymerises and inactivates it. Classic feed-forward activation plus feedback inhibition on one protein.
  • Covalent. AMPK phosphorylates and inactivates ACC whenever AMP rises, i.e. whenever the cell is short of energy. So does PKA under glucagon and adrenaline. Insulin activates protein phosphatase 2A, which removes those phosphates and switches ACC back on. Metformin activates AMPK, which is part of why it reduces hepatic lipogenesis.
  • Transcriptional. Insulin induces SREBP-1c and glucose induces ChREBP; between them they raise the expression of ACC, FAS, ATP-citrate lyase and the NADPH-producing enzymes together. A sustained high-carbohydrate diet therefore does not just switch lipogenesis on, it builds more of the machinery.

Beyond palmitate: elongation, desaturation, and what humans cannot do

FAS releases palmitate and stops. Longer chains are made by ER elongases, which use malonyl-CoA and NADPH exactly as FAS does but work on acyl-CoA rather than acyl-ACP. Double bonds are inserted by desaturases — mixed-function oxidases requiring O2, NADH, cytochrome b5 and its reductase, in which both the fatty acid and NADH are oxidised while O2 is reduced to two molecules of water. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase (Δ9) converts stearate to oleate, and Δ5 and Δ6 desaturases act closer to the carboxyl end.

The limit is absolute and it is the source of one clean exam fact: mammals have no desaturase that acts beyond C9, counting from the carboxyl end. We therefore cannot make a double bond at Δ12 or Δ15, and so cannot synthesise linoleate (18:2, ω-6) or α-linolenate (18:3, ω-3). Those two are the essential fatty acids. Arachidonate (20:4, ω-6) is made from linoleate by desaturation and elongation, so it is essential only when linoleate is absent — conditionally essential. Essential fatty acid deficiency, seen historically in patients on fat-free parenteral nutrition, gives a scaly dermatitis, poor wound healing and hair loss, and is diagnosed by a rising ratio of mead acid (20:3 ω-9) to arachidonate as the body improvises with the wrong precursor.

Go deeper · where NADPH comes from, and why it is NADPH and not NADH

NADH and NADPH differ by a single phosphate on the adenosine 2′-OH, and they carry the same hydride. The point of having two is that the cell can hold them at different redox poise: cytosolic NAD⁺/NADH ≈ 700 (strongly oxidised, good for accepting electrons from catabolism) while NADP⁺/NADPH ≈ 0.01 (strongly reduced, good for donating electrons to biosynthesis). The phosphate is simply a molecular tag that lets enzymes tell the two pools apart. Any question asking "why NADPH" has this as its answer.

Three sources supply it in a lipogenic cell:

  • The oxidative branch of the pentose phosphate pathway — glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, two NADPH per glucose-6-phosphate. This is the major source in adipose tissue and lactating mammary gland.
  • Malic enzyme, on the return leg of the citrate shuttle — one NADPH per acetyl group exported, so up to 8 of the 14 needed for palmitate.
  • Cytosolic isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH1), a minor contributor.

The link to G6PD deficiency is worth making, because it is where this shows up clinically: the same NADPH pool also keeps glutathione reduced. A G6PD-deficient erythrocyte has no other NADPH source at all, which is why oxidative stress from primaquine, sulfonamides, fava beans or infection causes haemolysis with Heinz bodies and bite cells. The lipogenic function and the antioxidant function compete for one pool.

§ 08

Complex lipids and the sphingolipidoses

The ideaA free fatty acid is never the final product. It is fitted onto a backbone — usually glycerol, sometimes an amino alcohol — and the head group bolted onto that backbone decides what the molecule is for: storage, membrane, signal, or insulation. Four clinical facts that fall straight out of the assembly routes

  • Lung surfactant is a phosphatidylcholine. Specifically dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, made by type II pneumocytes from about week 24 and surging near term. Sphingolipids are degraded in the lysosome by removing terminal groups one at a time. § 09

Cholesterol, HMG-CoA reductase and the statins

The ideaCholesterol is built from the same two-carbon units as everything else on this page, but instead of being strung into a line they are assembled into five-carbon branched blocks, joined into a long chain, and then folded up and zipped into four fused rings in one spectacular reaction. The rings are the problem: once formed, no human enzyme can break them open again. So cholesterol can only be made, stored or exported — never burned. That single structural fact is why the body needs an elaborate transport system for it, why it accumulates in arteries, and why the only drug target that matters is the enzyme that commits carbon to the pathway in the first place.

Follow the carbons once

Three acetyl-CoA condense to HMG-CoA in the cytosol — the same molecule that ketogenesis makes in the mitochondrion (§ 06), and the point at which the two pathways diverge for good. HMG-CoA reductase then reduces the thioester all the way to a primary alcohol, using two NADPH, and mevalonate is produced. From mevalonate onwards the chemistry is isoprenoid: three ATP convert it to the C5 isopentenyl pyrophosphate, and C5 units are then strung together — C10, C15 — before two C15 pieces are welded tail-to-tail into the C30 hydrocarbon squalene. Squalene monooxygenase installs an epoxide, and oxidosqualene cyclase folds the whole linear molecule into the four-ring steroid nucleus in a single concerted reaction. What emerges is lanosterol, and about nineteen further steps trim three methyl carbons to give cholesterol.

Two consequences are worth stating explicitly. First, everything after mevalonate is expensive but unregulated; the cell makes its decision at the reductase and then simply runs the rest. Second, the intermediates are not only cholesterol precursors. Farnesyl pyrophosphate branches off to give coenzyme Q10, dolichol (needed for N-linked glycosylation), haem A, and the farnesyl and geranylgeranyl anchors that tether Ras and Rho to the membrane. Block the reductase and you dial down all of them, which is the mechanistic hypothesis behind statin myopathy and the reason nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates — which inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase and so strip osteoclasts of their prenylated signalling proteins — sit on this same pathway.

The ring cannot be broken — and everything follows

Humans have no enzyme that degrades the steroid nucleus to CO2. Cholesterol therefore has only three fates: it is incorporated into membranes, converted to a steroid product (bile acids, the adrenal and gonadal steroids, vitamin D3 via 7-dehydrocholesterol in skin), or excreted. Quantitatively, essentially all elimination is as bile acids and biliary cholesterol, with cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) the rate-limiting enzyme of bile acid synthesis. Since roughly 95 % of bile salts are reclaimed in the terminal ileum by the enterohepatic circulation, interrupting that circulation forces the liver to make more from cholesterol — which is exactly how bile acid sequestrants such as cholestyramine lower LDL, and also why terminal ileal disease or resection causes both bile-salt diarrhoea and gallstones.

Regulation: four layers on one enzyme

HMG-CoA reductase is a textbook case of an enzyme controlled at every available level, and each level has a different time constant.

  • Transcription (hours). Low cell sterol frees SREBP-2, which induces the reductase and the LDL receptor. High sterol keeps SCAP–SREBP-2 locked in the ER by Insig.
  • Regulated degradation (hours). Sterols — particularly lanosterol and oxysterols — make the reductase bind Insig, which recruits a ubiquitin ligase and sends the enzyme to the proteasome. Its half-life falls several-fold.
  • Phosphorylation (minutes). AMPK phosphorylates and inactivates it, exactly as it does ACC. Cholesterol synthesis is expensive, so a cell short of ATP stops making it.
  • Hormones. Insulin and thyroid hormone increase activity; glucagon and glucocorticoids decrease it. Clinically this shows as the hypercholesterolaemia of untreated hypothyroidism — reduced LDL-receptor expression as well as altered synthesis — which is why thyroid function is checked before labelling someone with primary dyslipidaemia.

Statins: the mechanism is not the one most people give

Statins are competitive inhibitors: they carry a group that mimics the mevalonate half of HMG-CoA, and they bind the reductase with dissociation constants in the nanomolar range against a Km for HMG-CoA in the micromolar range — roughly a thousandfold tighter. So far, so ordinary. But the reason plasma LDL falls by a quarter to a half is not primarily that the liver makes less cholesterol. It is that the hepatocyte's internal sterol level drops, SREBP-2 is released, and the liver puts more LDL receptors on its surface — so it clears LDL out of the plasma faster. The drug works by turning up the disposal machinery, not by turning down production.

That distinction is worth holding because it explains the clinical facts around it. Homozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia, in which there are no functional LDL receptors to upregulate, responds poorly to statins — a diagnostic clue in itself. PCSK9 inhibitors work on the same lever from the other side: PCSK9 normally binds the LDL receptor and directs it to lysosomal destruction after each round of endocytosis, so blocking PCSK9 lets receptors recycle instead, and the two drug classes are additive. And people with natural loss-of-function PCSK9 variants have lifelong low LDL and markedly reduced coronary risk, which is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that LDL is causal rather than merely correlated.

Go deeper · esterification, and the two enzymes that are constantly confused

Free cholesterol is amphipathic and intercalates into membranes; a cholesteryl ester, with a fatty acid on the 3-OH, is entirely hydrophobic and can only sit in the oily core of a droplet or a lipoprotein. Esterification is therefore the packaging operation, and two different enzymes do it in two different places:

  • ACAT (acyl-CoA : cholesterol acyltransferase, now SOAT) works inside the cell, on the ER, using acyl-CoA as the acyl donor. It is activated when free cholesterol rises — the third arm of the response to LDL uptake, alongside suppressing the reductase and suppressing the LDL receptor.
  • LCAT (lecithin : cholesterol acyltransferase) works in plasma, on the surface of HDL, using the sn-2 acyl chain of phosphatidylcholine as the donor, and is activated by apoA-I. Esterifying surface cholesterol drives it into the particle core, which is what converts a flat discoidal nascent HDL into a spherical mature one and keeps the surface concentration low enough for more cholesterol to keep flowing in.

The mnemonic that survives the exam: ACAT is activated by cholesterol and works inside the cell; LCAT works in the liquid — plasma. LCAT deficiency produces corneal opacities, haemolytic anaemia and renal failure with almost no cholesteryl ester in plasma.

Lipoproteins and where the traffic fails

The ideaFat and cholesterol have to travel through blood, which is water. The solution is a droplet: oil in the middle, a single layer of water-facing molecules around the outside, and proteins stuck through that layer which act as address labels and switches. The whole system is one idea repeated — the same kind of particle, carrying different cargo, wearing different labels. If you know which label a particle wears, you know where it goes and which enzyme is allowed to act on it, and every one of the classic lipid disorders becomes a missing label or a missing enzyme.

HDL is not just "the good one"

HDL does three jobs, and the exam asks about all of them. It is the acceptor for cholesterol effluxed from peripheral cells through ABCA1 — including from the macrophage foam cells in a plaque. And it is the circulating lending library of apolipoproteins: a newly secreted chylomicron has no apoC-II or apoE until HDL donates them, and it hands them back when it becomes a remnant. That last role means an HDL deficiency also impairs triglyceride clearance, which is not obvious from the "good cholesterol" slogan.

Worked example 6 — reading a lipid panel, and knowing when the formula breaks

A fasting sample gives total cholesterol 268 mg/dL, HDL-C 42 mg/dL, triglyceride 150 mg/dL. (a) Calculate LDL-C and non-HDL-C. (b) A second patient has TC 268, HDL-C 42 and triglyceride 620 mg/dL. What changes?

  1. (a) Friedewald: LDL-C = TC − HDL-C − TG/5.
  2. VLDL-cholesterol is estimated as 150/5 = 30 mg/dL. The divisor is 5 because a VLDL particle carries roughly five times as much triacylglycerol as cholesterol.
  3. LDL-C = 268 − 42 − 30 = 196 mg/dL. That is very high — above 190 is one of the diagnostic thresholds for familial hypercholesterolaemia.
  4. Non-HDL-C = 268 − 42 = 226 mg/dL. This counts every apoB-containing particle — LDL, IDL and VLDL together — and needs no assumption at all.
  5. (b) At TG = 620 the estimate fails. Friedewald is invalid above about 400 mg/dL, because at high triglyceride the VLDL particles are triglyceride-enriched and the fixed 5 : 1 ratio no longer holds, so TG/5 overestimates VLDL-cholesterol and LDL-C is reported too low. It is also invalid in a non-fasting sample (chylomicrons present) and in type III dysbetalipoproteinaemia, where remnants have an abnormal composition.
  6. The practical answer: report non-HDL-C, or measure LDL directly. Non-HDL-C is 226 mg/dL in both patients — and the second patient additionally needs treating for pancreatitis risk, which rises steeply above about 1000 mg/dL.
  7. In SI units the same calculation uses TG/2.2: with TC 6.94, HDL-C 1.09 and TG 1.70 mmol·L⁻¹, LDL-C = 6.94 − 1.09 − 0.77 = 5.08 mmol·L⁻¹. Check which unit system a stem is using before dividing by anything.

First, notice that the disorders of triglyceride clearance (type I) cause pancreatitis while the disorders of cholesterol clearance (type IIa) cause atherosclerosis — the cargo determines the complication. Second, LDL becomes atherogenic only after it is oxidised in the subendothelial space; the macrophage scavenger receptors that then take it up are not downregulated by intracellular cholesterol, unlike the LDL receptor. That one missing feedback loop is what allows a macrophage to gorge itself into a foam cell, and it is the molecular reason plaques form at all.

§ 11

Eicosanoids: the COX and LOX branches

The ideaOne particular twenty-carbon fatty acid, sitting quietly in the membrane, is the raw material for a whole family of short-range signals — fever, pain, inflammation, clotting, bronchospasm, labour. It is not stored as a signal; it is cut out of the membrane on demand, and then sent down one of two production lines. Almost every anti-inflammatory drug you will ever prescribe works by blocking that release, or by blocking one of the two lines. Learning where each drug cuts in is the same as learning why each drug has the side effects it has.

Definition & the branch point

Eicosanoid（類二十碳酸）
Any signalling molecule derived from a C20 polyunsaturated fatty acid, in practice arachidonate (20:4, ω-6). Prostaglandins, thromboxanes, prostacyclin and leukotrienes are all eicosanoids. They act locally and are destroyed within seconds — autocrine and paracrine signals, never circulating hormones.

Phospholipase A2
Cleaves the sn-2 acyl chain from a membrane phospholipid — which is exactly where arachidonate sits. This is the rate-limiting step, because free arachidonate is otherwise essentially absent. Glucocorticoids induce annexin-1 (lipocortin), which inhibits it, so steroids shut down both branches at once. That is the mechanistic reason steroids are broader anti-inflammatories than NSAIDs.
Cyclooxygenase (prostaglandin H synthase)
One protein, two activities: a cyclooxygenase that adds two O2 to give PGG2, and a peroxidase that reduces it to PGH2. PGH2 is the common precursor; which product a cell makes depends on which downstream synthase it expresses. COX-1 is constitutive — gastric mucosa, platelets, kidney; COX-2 is largely inducible at sites of inflammation, though constitutive in kidney and vascular endothelium.
Aspirin
The only NSAID that is irreversible: it transfers an acetyl group onto Ser530 in the COX-1 channel (the equivalent serine in COX-2), permanently blocking arachidonate's access. All other NSAIDs are reversible competitive inhibitors.

Why low-dose aspirin is antithrombotic and high-dose aspirin is not more so

This is the most satisfying mechanism-to-clinic chain in the whole note, and it turns entirely on one structural fact: platelets have no nucleus.

Aspirin acetylates COX irreversibly. In a platelet, the enzyme it destroys can never be replaced, because the cell cannot transcribe a new gene — so thromboxane A2 production is lost for the platelet's entire remaining life, seven to ten days, and recovery depends only on new platelets entering the circulation at roughly 10 % per day. In an endothelial cell, which has a nucleus, new COX protein appears within hours and prostacyclin production recovers. A small dose, given once daily, is therefore enough to keep the platelet pool permanently inhibited while barely touching the endothelium — and since platelets are exposed to the drug in the portal circulation before the liver can clear most of it, the selectivity is better still. Raising the dose does not increase the antiplatelet effect; it only starts to suppress endothelial PGI2 as well, which is counterproductive.

Everything else about aspirin falls out of the same picture. Gastric injury: COX-1 in gastric mucosa makes the PGE2 that drives mucus and bicarbonate secretion and restrains acid, so inhibiting it removes the mucosal defence. Renal impairment in a volume-depleted patient: prostaglandins dilate the afferent arteriole, and when renal perfusion depends on that dilation, an NSAID drops the glomerular filtration rate. Antipyresis: PGE2 raises the hypothalamic set point, so blocking its synthesis lowers a fever but not a normal temperature. And ibuprofen taken shortly before aspirin can abolish the cardioprotection, because the reversible drug occupies the channel and blocks aspirin's access to Ser530 — a genuine, clinically advised interaction that only makes sense once you know the two drugs bind the same site by different chemistry.

COX-2 selectivity was a trade, not a free win

Celecoxib and its relatives spare gastric COX-1, so they cause fewer ulcers. But endothelial PGI2 is largely a COX-2 product while platelet TXA2 is a COX-1 product — so a COX-2-selective drug removes the antithrombotic side of the balance and leaves the prothrombotic side intact. That imbalance is why rofecoxib was withdrawn, and it is the standard exam illustration that selectivity is only useful if you know what the spared isoform was doing. In the same spirit: paracetamol (acetaminophen) is a weak, largely central COX inhibitor with essentially no anti-inflammatory and no antiplatelet action, so it is not an NSAID in the pharmacological sense, and its toxicity is a hepatic one — NAPQI depleting glutathione — that has nothing to do with prostaglandins.

The substrate-shunting question

In aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (Samter's triad: asthma, nasal polyps, aspirin sensitivity), inhibiting COX pushes arachidonate down the 5-LOX branch, raising cysteinyl leukotrienes and provoking bronchospasm. The management follows from the diagram: use a leukotriene-directed drug (montelukast, or zileuton) rather than more COX inhibition. The same shunting logic explains why ω-3 supplementation blunts inflammation — EPA competes with arachidonate for the same enzymes and yields series-3 prostaglandins and series-5 leukotrienes, which are markedly less potent. Whenever a stem gives you a pathway with two branches and blocks one, ask what happens to the substrate that has nowhere else to go.
