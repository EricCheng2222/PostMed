Oscillations and Simple Harmonic Motion

This whole chapter grows out of one line: a = -ω²x. Once a system's acceleration is proportional to its displacement and points back at equilibrium, the sinusoid, the phase relations, the period, the energy split and the resonance curve are all forced — you are not memorising six formulas, you are reading off six consequences. The other half of the chapter is learning to spot that line in disguise: as a spring, as a swinging rod, as a twisted wire, as a molecular bond.

§ 01

Hooke's law and the restoring force

The ideaStretch or squash something springy and it pushes back — and it pushes back harder the further you move it. Because that push is always aimed at the resting position, letting go does not end the story: the object is hauled back, overshoots, is hauled back again, and keeps going. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that one habit, so it is worth meeting it slowly.

Definition & formula

Restoring force
A force that always points back towards a fixed equilibrium position, and whose magnitude grows with the displacement away from it.
F = -kx ideal spring, inside the elastic limit
Full definition, symbols and units
Hooke's law
The statement that for an ideal spring the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement from equilibrium.
Spring constant k
The force needed per unit extension — a property of the object, not of the motion.
U = ½kx²elastic PE stored; x from natural length
k∥ = k₁ + k₂springs in parallel (side by side)
1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂springs in series (end to end)
Frestoring force (N)
kspring constant / stiffness (N·m⁻¹)
xdisplacement from equilibrium (m)

The minus sign carries the entire physics. It says the force is always aimed at the equilibrium point, so the system is pulled back whichever way it is pushed. Everything downstream — the oscillation, the sinusoid, the period — is a consequence of that one sign; drop it and you get runaway exponential growth instead of oscillation.

The proportionality is the second half. A force that merely restores (a stretched rubber band, a pendulum at 60°) produces oscillation, but not simple harmonic oscillation. Only the linear restoring force gives a period that is independent of amplitude, and only that gives you a clock.

Sign-convention trap: where is x measured from?

x is the displacement from the equilibrium position — not from the wall, not from the pivot, and, for a hanging spring, not from the spring's natural length. A spring hanging with a mass on it sits stretched by d = mg/k; that stretched position is the equilibrium, and x is measured from there (see §05). Get this wrong and gravity contaminates every subsequent equation.

Combining springs — and the half-spring result

Two springs in parallel both stretch by the same x and their forces add, so k∥ = k₁ + k₂: stiffer. Two springs in series carry the same force and their extensions add, so the compliances (1/k) add: softer. The consequence students are asked to produce on the spot: cut a spring in half and each half has twice the original k, because for a given pull each half stretches only half as much. Halving the spring therefore shortens the period by a factor √2.

What is examined here

Series/parallel combinations feeding straight into T = 2π√(m/k); the cut-spring result; and the area under an F–x graph being the stored energy ½kx² (a triangle, hence the ½ — this is not "force × distance" with the full force, because the force grows from zero).

Why any of this matters biologically

Every stable chemical bond, every folded protein, every elastic tissue behaves Hookean near its resting configuration: the initial straight portion of a stress–strain curve for tendon, artery wall or lung tissue is exactly a spring constant. Bond stretching as a spring is also the model behind infrared vibrational spectroscopy, where the absorbed frequency is (1/2π)√(k/μ) with μ the reduced mass of the two bonded atoms.

§ 02

The one condition: a = -ω²x

The ideaThere is one exact test for the kind of back-and-forth motion this note is about: the further the object has strayed from its resting place, the harder it is hauled back — and in strict step, so twice as far means twice the pull. Anything that passes that test settles into a rhythm fixed entirely by how the system is built, not by how hard you set it going. A big shove and a gentle nudge on the same swing take the same time to come back, and that is why oscillators make good clocks.

Definition & formula

Simple harmonic motion (SHM)
Motion in which the acceleration is proportional to the displacement from a fixed equilibrium point and is directed towards it.
a = -ω²x ⟺ d²x/dt² + ω²x = 0 the definition — everything else follows

Combine Newton's second law with Hooke's law and the mass cancels out of the force but not out of the acceleration: ma = -kx gives a = -(k/m)x. The bracket k/m is a positive constant fixed by the hardware. Call it ω². That is the whole derivation, and it is worth noticing what it does not contain: no A, no initial velocity, no mention of how the motion was started. ω is a property of the system; A and φ are properties of the kick. That separation is the origin of isochronism — same period whatever the amplitude — and it is why oscillators make good clocks and bad amplitude meters.

The master method

For any problem in this chapter, do exactly this:

  1. Displace the system by a small amount and write the net restoring force (or torque).
  2. Force everything into the form a = -(positive constant) × x, or, for a rotation, α = -(positive constant) × θ.
  3. Read off ω² = that constant, then T = 2π/ω.

You never need to solve a differential equation. Steps 1 and 2 are where the marks are, and step 2 is usually where a small-angle or small-displacement approximation is smuggled in.

Worked example 1 — reading ω straight off the equation of motion

A 0.30 kg body moves along x with acceleration a = -64x (SI units). Find the period, and the maximum speed if the amplitude is 3.0 cm.

  1. The equation already has the form a = -ω²x, so ω² = 64 rad²·s⁻² and ω = 8.0 rad·s⁻¹. The mass is a distractor — it is already baked into the 64.
  2. T = 2π/ω = 6.283/8.0 = 0.785 s.
  3. v_max = ωA = 8.0 × 0.030 = 0.24 m·s⁻¹.
  4. (If you did want k: k = mω² = 0.30 × 64 = 19.2 N·m⁻¹.)
T = 0.785 s · v_max = 0.24 m·s⁻¹

The single most expensive trap in this chapter

In SHM the acceleration is not constant — it is proportional to x, so it changes continuously and reverses twice per cycle. The constant-acceleration (SUVAT) equations v = u + at, v² = u² + 2as are therefore illegal here, always. The SHM replacement for "v² = u² + 2as" is v = ±ω√(A² - x²).

Why SHM turns up everywhere

Expand any smooth potential about a stable minimum: U(x) ≈ U₀ + ½U″(x₀)(x - x₀)² — the linear term vanishes because it is a minimum. So every stable equilibrium in physics is a spring with k_eff = U″(x₀) provided the displacement is small enough. Molecular bonds, nuclei in a crystal, a marble in a bowl, an ion in a trap: all SHM in the small-oscillation limit. This is the reason the chapter is worth its exam weight.

§ 03

x(t), v(t), a(t) and their phases

The ideaFollow an oscillator through one full round trip and three quantities take turns being large: how far it is from the middle, how fast it is moving, and how hard it is being hauled back. They never peak together. At the ends of the swing it is momentarily still yet pulled hardest; racing through the middle it feels no pull at all. This section is about that staggered timing and how to read it straight off a graph.

Differentiating a cosine twice returns a cosine with a minus sign and a factor ω² — which is precisely the defining condition. That is why the solution is sinusoidal and why nothing else is. Each differentiation does two things: it multiplies the amplitude by ω, and it advances the phase by a quarter cycle. Both facts are worth holding as one sentence.

Note the structural fact hiding in that table: x and a peak together in time but in opposite directions, while v peaks exactly where they both vanish. That is not a coincidence to be memorised — it is what "the force is proportional to minus the displacement" looks like plotted.

Fixing φ from the way the motion starts

Only two starts appear in practice. Released from rest at the extreme (x = A, v = 0 at t = 0) gives φ = 0, so x = A cos ωt. Given a push through the equilibrium point (x = 0, v = +v₀ at t = 0) gives φ = -π/2, so x = A sin ωt with A = v₀/ω. Cosine for "let go", sine for "flicked" — and the two differ only by where you started the clock, never by anything physical.

Two confusions worth pre-empting

"Zero velocity means zero acceleration." At the turning point the speed is momentarily zero but the restoring force is at its largest. Same as a ball at the top of its flight: instantaneously stationary, still accelerating at g.

"Leads" means later. It does not. If v leads x by 90°, the velocity graph reaches its peak a quarter period before the displacement graph reaches its own. Physically: the oscillator is moving fastest on its way towards the extreme it has not yet reached.

The workhorse formula

v = ±ω√(A² - x²) answers every "what is the speed at this displacement?" question without ever mentioning t. It also encodes both extremes at once: put x = 0 and you get ωA; put x = ±A and you get zero. Derive it from energy (§08) if you forget it.

§ 04

Angular frequency, period, frequency

The ideaThere are three ways of saying how fast something repeats: how long one full round trip takes, how many round trips happen each second, and how quickly the motion sweeps round if you picture it as a turning wheel. All three describe the same rhythm, and converting between them is nothing but arithmetic. The section exists because the last two differ by a fixed factor of a little over six, and quietly using one where the other belongs is the most expensive slip in the chapter.

Definition & formula

Period T
The time for one complete cycle — the shortest time after which both position and velocity repeat.
ω = 2πf = 2πT always true, for any periodic motion
Full definition, symbols and units

The number of complete cycles per second.

The rate at which the phase angle advances, in radians per second.

ω and f describe the same thing in different currency: f counts cycles, ω counts radians, and one cycle is 2π radians. The factor 2π is not decoration — it is the exchange rate, and it is where marks are lost more often than anywhere else in this chapter.

The reason ω rather than f appears in every SHM formula is that a cosine takes an angle, not a count. cos(2πft) and cos(ωt) are the same function; writing ω just saves carrying a 2π through every differentiation. §09 makes the "angular" part literal: ω really is the angular velocity of a point going round a circle whose shadow is the oscillation.

Units will not save you

Hz and rad·s⁻¹ are both dimensionally s⁻¹, because the radian is dimensionless. So a dimensional check cannot catch the classic error of substituting f where ω belongs. Concretely, for the oscillator in §05 (k = 40 N·m⁻¹, m = 0.25 kg): ω = 12.6 rad·s⁻¹ but f = 2.01 Hz. Using 2.01 in v_max = ωA gives an answer 6.3× too small and it still "has the right units". Read the question: "angular frequency" means ω, "frequency" or an answer in Hz means f.

Memory hook

ω = 2πf, T = 2π/ω. The 2π is the price of using radians — pay it once at the start of the problem and never again.

§ 05

The mass–spring oscillator

The ideaHang a weight on a spring, set it going, and the rate at which it bobs is settled by exactly two things: how stiff the spring is and how heavy the weight is. Stiffer is quicker, heavier is slower, and nothing else gets a vote — not how far you pulled it, and, more surprisingly, not gravity. The same spring and weight bob at precisely the same rate lying flat on a bench, hanging in a lift, or standing on the Moon.

Definition & formula

Mass–spring oscillator
A mass whose only net force, measured from its equilibrium position, is the linear restoring force of a spring.
T = 2πmk independent of amplitude and of g

The derivation is one line — ma = -kx ⟹ ω² = k/m — but the shape of the answer deserves a sentence. k is in the denominator of T because stiffness hurries the mass back; m is in the numerator because inertia resists being hurried. The square root means the response is blunt: to double the period you must quadruple the mass, or reduce the stiffness to a quarter.

Why gravity drops out

Hang the same spring vertically. It stretches by d = mg/k before it comes to rest; that stretched position is the new equilibrium. Now measure x downwards from there. The net force is

F = mg - k(d + x) = mg - mg - kx = -kxgravity cancels exactly

Gravity has done nothing except move the origin. So the period is the same lying on a frictionless table, hanging in a lift, or on the Moon. Contrast the pendulum in §06, whose entire restoring force is gravity and whose period therefore scales as 1/√g. The spring is blind to gravity; the pendulum is blind to mass. Those two blindnesses are the most reliably examined facts in the chapter.

Worked example 2 — a full kinematic profile

A 0.25 kg block on a frictionless surface is attached to a spring of k = 40 N·m⁻¹, pulled 8.0 cm from equilibrium and released. Find ω, T, f, the maximum speed, the maximum acceleration, and the speed as it passes x = 4.0 cm.

  1. ω = √(k/m) = √(40/0.25) = √160 = 12.65 rad·s⁻¹.
  2. T = 2π/ω = 6.283/12.65 = 0.497 s, so f = 1/T = 2.01 Hz. (Note ω and f differ by the factor 2π — §04.)
  3. v_max = ωA = 12.65 × 0.080 = 1.01 m·s⁻¹, reached at x = 0.
  4. a_max = ω²A = 160 × 0.080 = 12.8 m·s⁻², reached at x = ±A. Note ω² = 160 exactly, so no rounding creeps in here.
  5. At x = 4.0 cm use v = ω√(A² - x²) = 12.65 × √(0.0064 - 0.0016) = 12.65 × 0.0693 = 0.876 m·s⁻¹. Sanity check: at half amplitude the speed should be √3/2 = 86.6 % of v_max, and 0.866 × 1.012 = 0.876 ✓.
ω = 12.6 rad·s⁻¹ · T = 0.497 s · f = 2.01 Hz · v_max = 1.01 m·s⁻¹ · a_max = 12.8 m·s⁻² · v(A/2) = 0.876 m·s⁻¹
Worked example 3 — getting k from a static measurement

A 0.60 kg mass hung on a spring stretches it by 12 cm. It is then pulled down a little and released. Find k and the period. (g = 9.8 m·s⁻²)

  1. At the new equilibrium the spring force balances the weight: kd = mg ⟹ k = (0.60 × 9.8)/0.12 = 5.88/0.12 = 49 N·m⁻¹.
  2. T = 2π√(m/k) = 2π√(0.60/49) = 2π√0.01224 = 2π × 0.1107 = 0.695 s (f = 1.44 Hz).
  3. Shortcut worth knowing: substituting k = mg/d gives T = 2π√(d/g), so a hanging spring has the same period as a simple pendulum whose length equals the static extension. Check: 2π√(0.12/9.8) = 0.695 s ✓ — and notice that m has disappeared from this version, because a heavier mass stretches the spring proportionally more.
k = 49 N·m⁻¹ · T = 0.695 s

Ratio questions

Because T ∝ √(m/k), almost every exam variant is a ratio: four times the mass → twice the period; four times the stiffness → half the period; two identical springs in parallel → k doubles → T falls by √2; the same two in series → k halves → T rises by √2. You rarely need to compute a number.

Amplitude independence is exact here

For a mass–spring system the period is independent of amplitude as an exact consequence of Hooke's law, with no approximation anywhere — right up to the elastic limit or until the coils clash. For a pendulum (§06) amplitude independence is only an approximation, valid for small swings. Do not carry the spring's exactness over to the pendulum.

§ 06

The simple pendulum and small angles

The ideaA weight on a string swings at a rate set by just two things: the length of the string and the strength of gravity where you are standing. How heavy the weight is makes no difference at all — a light bob and a heavy one on identical strings keep perfect time with each other. There is a catch, and most of this section is about it: the tidy rule only holds while the swings stay small, so it is worth knowing how small "small" has to be and what it costs when you cheat.

Resolve the weight at angle θ: the component along the string is balanced by the tension, and the component perpendicular to the string, mg sin θ, is what restores. Its torque about the pivot is -mgL sin θ, and with I = mL² Newton's second law for rotation gives α = -(g/L) sin θ.

That is not SHM. It has sin θ where the definition demands θ. Only after replacing sin θ by θ does the equation collapse to α = -(g/L)θ, and only then can you read off ω = √(g/L). Everything you know about pendulum periods is conditional on that substitution, so it is worth knowing exactly what it costs.

Why the bob's mass does not matter

m appears twice and cancels: once in the restoring torque (because the restoring agent is the weight, ∝ m) and once in the moment of inertia (∝ m). It is the same cancellation of gravitational and inertial mass that makes all objects fall at the same rate. Compare the spring, where the restoring force comes from the spring — it has no idea how much mass is attached — so there is nothing for the inertia to cancel against and T ∝ √m survives.

Go deeper · what the small-angle approximation actually costs, degree by degree

How much does sin θ ≈ θ actually cost?

Two different errors are in play and confusing them is a standard trap. The error in the force is large and grows fast; the error in the period is much smaller. Two reasons: the force error quoted in the table is its peak value, reached only at the ends of the swing, while the period responds to the error averaged over the whole cycle — roughly half as big — and the period depends on the restoring strength through a square root, which halves any fractional error again.

Which way does the error go?

For θ > 0, θ > sin θ. So the small-angle model assumes a restoring torque that is too strong, and therefore predicts a period that is too short. A real pendulum swinging widely is always slower than 2π√(L/g) — a wide-swinging pendulum clock loses time. Getting the direction of the correction right is worth as much as getting its size right.

Worked example 4 — the seconds pendulum, and correcting a slow clock

(a) How long is a pendulum whose period is 2.00 s? (b) Such a clock is found to lose 60 s per day. By how much must the bob be raised? (g = 9.8 m·s⁻²)

  1. (a) Rearrange T = 2π√(L/g): L = gT²/(4π²) = (9.8 × 4.00)/39.48 = 39.2/39.48 = 0.993 m. This is the classic "seconds pendulum" — it takes one second per one-way swing, hence a two-second period. Halving the period to 1.00 s would need L = 0.248 m, a quarter of the length, because L ∝ T².
  2. (b) Losing time means the period is too long. The fractional error is ΔT/T = 60/86400 = 6.94 × 10⁻⁴.
  3. Since T ∝ √L, fractional changes relate as ΔT/T = ½ ΔL/L, so ΔL/L = 2 × 6.94 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.39 × 10⁻³.
  4. ΔL = 1.39 × 10⁻³ × 0.993 = 1.38 × 10⁻³ m. The pendulum must be shortened by about 1.4 mm — i.e. raise the bob.
L = 0.993 m · shorten by ≈ 1.4 mm

What is examined here

T ∝ √L and T ∝ 1/√g. Quadruple the length to double the period. Take the clock up a mountain or towards the equator (smaller g) and it runs slow. Take it into a lift accelerating upwards and the effective g rises, so it runs fast; in free fall the effective g is zero and it does not swing at all. And L is measured from the pivot to the centre of mass of the bob, not to its top — a favourite one-mark deduction.

Where this shows up in the body

A swinging leg is a pendulum: with T ∝ √L, longer legs have a longer natural swing period, which is why tall people walk with a slower, longer stride and small children take quick short steps. Walking near the leg's natural period is the metabolically cheapest cadence, because the swing is then driven mostly by gravity rather than by muscle.

§ 07

Physical and torsional pendulums

The ideaReal swinging things are rarely a neat weight on a light string — they are rods, legs, hanging signs, or a disc twisting on a wire. The same reasoning still applies, but now you have to allow for the fact that the object's material is spread out rather than gathered at one point. Bunch that material close to the pivot and the thing swings faster; fling it out towards the far end and it swings more slowly.

The recipe from §02 works unchanged, just in angular variables. Tilt the body by θ: its weight acts at the centre of mass, a distance d from the pivot, so the restoring torque is τ = -mgd sin θ ≈ -mgdθ. Newton's second law for rotation, Iα = τ, gives α = -(mgd/I)θ, hence ω² = mgd/I. Put I = mL² and d = L and you recover the simple pendulum — it is the special case where all the mass sits at the far end.

The mass still cancels, because for a rigid body of fixed shape I ∝ m. What does not cancel is how the mass is distributed: spreading it inwards reduces I more than it reduces d, so the body swings faster. That is the whole content of the formula.

Worked example 5 — a swinging rod, and a twisting disc

(a) A uniform rod of length 1.00 m hangs from a pivot at one end and swings through a small angle. Find its period and compare it with a simple pendulum of the same length. (b) A disc of mass 0.40 kg and radius 5.0 cm hangs from a wire of torsion constant κ = 0.020 N·m·rad⁻¹ and is given a small twist. Find its period.

  1. (a) For a uniform rod about one end, I = ⅓mL² and the centre of mass is at d = L/2. So T = 2π√(⅓mL² / (mg·L/2)) = 2π√(2L/3g) — the mass has vanished as promised.
  2. T = 2π√(2 × 1.00 / (3 × 9.8)) = 2π√(2/29.4) = 2π√0.06803 = 2π × 0.2608 = 1.64 s.
  3. A simple pendulum of L = 1.00 m gives T = 2π√(1.00/9.8) = 2.01 s. The rod is faster by a factor √(2/3) = 0.816, because its equivalent simple length is only L_eq = 2L/3 = 0.667 m — most of the rod's mass is nearer the pivot than the bob of the simple pendulum is.
  4. (b) For a disc about its central axis I = ½mR² = ½ × 0.40 × (0.050)² = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m².
  5. T = 2π√(I/κ) = 2π√(5.0 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.020) = 2π√0.025 = 2π × 0.1581 = 0.993 s.

Why torsional oscillators are the precision instruments

A twisted wire obeys τ = -κθ linearly over large angles — there is no sin θ to approximate away. So a torsional oscillator is genuinely isochronous over a wide amplitude range and, because κ can be made tiny, exquisitely sensitive to small torques. Hence the torsion balance in the Cavendish and Coulomb experiments, and the balance wheel and hairspring of a mechanical watch — a torsional oscillator, which is also why a watch keeps time in any orientation while a pendulum clock must stand upright.

Two things people write down wrongly

I must be taken about the pivot, not about the centre of mass — use the parallel-axis theorem I = I_cm + md² if you are given I_cm. And d is the pivot-to-centre-of-mass distance, which for a uniform rod pivoted at its end is L/2, not L. Substituting d = L is the single commonest slip in this section.

§ 08

Energy in SHM

The ideaAn oscillator holds one fixed quantity of energy and passes it back and forth between two pockets. At the ends of the swing every bit of it is stored in the stretch; racing through the middle every bit of it is in the motion; anywhere in between the two pockets share it. As long as nothing rubs, the total never changes — which makes energy the quickest way to answer "how fast is it going here?" without thinking about time at all.

Definition & formula

Total mechanical energy of an oscillator
The constant sum of the kinetic and elastic potential energies of an undamped oscillator; it is fixed at the moment the oscillator is set going and never changes thereafter.

Evaluate the total energy at the easiest point in the cycle. At a turning point the oscillator is momentarily at rest, so all the energy is potential: E = ½kA². At the centre it is all kinetic: E = ½mv_max² = ½m(ωA)². The two expressions agree because mω² = k — a useful identity in its own right.

Setting ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kA² and solving for v regenerates the workhorse of §03, v = ±ω√(A² - x²), with no calculus. Energy is usually the fastest route to any "speed at this displacement" question.

Worked example 6 — where the energy is

Take the oscillator of Worked example 2: m = 0.25 kg, k = 40 N·m⁻¹, A = 8.0 cm. Find the total energy; the kinetic and potential energies at x = 4.0 cm; and the displacement at which they are equal.

  1. E = ½kA² = 0.5 × 40 × (0.080)² = 0.5 × 40 × 6.4 × 10⁻³ = 0.128 J. Cross-check with ½mv_max² = 0.5 × 0.25 × (1.012)² = 0.128 J ✓.
  2. At x = 4.0 cm: U = ½kx² = 0.5 × 40 × 1.6 × 10⁻³ = 0.032 J, which is (x/A)² = ¼ of E — as it must be, since U ∝ x².
  3. So K = E - U = 0.128 - 0.032 = 0.096 J, three-quarters of the total. Half the amplitude means a quarter of the energy is potential, not half. (And v = √(2K/m) = √(0.768) = 0.876 m·s⁻¹, agreeing with §05.)
  4. K = U requires ½kx² = ½E = ¼kA², so x = A/√2 = 0.080/1.414 = 0.0566 m = 5.7 cm, where each store holds 0.064 J and the speed is v_max/√2 = 0.716 m·s⁻¹.
E = 0.128 J · at 4.0 cm: U = 0.032 J, K = 0.096 J · K = U at x = 5.7 cm

Two consequences worth carrying forward

Energy goes as amplitude squared. Double the amplitude and the energy is four times larger, while the period does not budge. This is the same E ∝ A² that governs the intensity of waves and sound in note 08 and of electromagnetic waves later — a 3 dB, factor-of-two change in energy corresponds to only a factor √2 in amplitude.

The energy oscillates at twice the frequency of the displacement. K and U each complete a full cycle in T/2, because cos² and sin² repeat twice per period of the cosine. The oscillator passes through "all kinetic" twice per cycle, once going each way. Their time averages are both E/2, because the average of cos² over a cycle is ½.

Do not double-count gravity on a vertical spring

With x measured from the hanging equilibrium, the combined gravitational-plus-elastic potential energy is exactly ½kx² + constant. So use E = ½kA² as it stands and do not add a separate mgh term. Adding one is the standard way to get a vertical-spring energy question wrong, and it also breaks the symmetry of the answer: the motion is symmetric about the hanging equilibrium, so the energy expression must be too.

Ratios to have ready

At x = A/2: U = E/4, K = 3E/4, speed = (√3/2)v_max ≈ 0.87 v_max. At x = A/√2 ≈ 0.71A: U = K = E/2, speed = v_max/√2 ≈ 0.71 v_max. At x = (√3/2)A ≈ 0.87A: U = 3E/4, K = E/4, speed = v_max/2. Notice how displacement and speed swap roles between the first and last of those — the pattern is (x/A)² + (v/v_max)² = 1.

§ 09

SHM as the shadow of circular motion

The ideaShine a light sideways at a ball going steadily round a circle and watch the shadow it throws on the wall. The shadow slides back and forth, dawdling at the two edges and racing through the middle — and it moves in exactly the same way as a bobbing spring or a swinging pendulum. That is not a loose comparison but a precise match, and it lets you settle awkward timing questions by thinking about a turning wheel instead of doing any algebra.

Definition & formula

Reference circle
The auxiliary circle of radius A on which a point moves at constant angular speed ω, such that its projection onto a diameter reproduces the simple harmonic motion exactly.
x = A cos θ, θ = ωt + φ projection of uniform circular motion of radius A

Take a point going round a circle of radius A at constant angular speed ω and shine a light from the side. The shadow on the wall performs simple harmonic motion — and the correspondence is exact, not an analogy. It is worth adopting because it derives the entire §03 table geometrically, without differentiating anything.

Three payoffs. First, the amplitudes ωA and ω²A come free from circular-motion results you already know. Second, the phase relations become geometry: the velocity vector is perpendicular to the radius and 90° ahead of it, the acceleration is antiparallel to the radius, and their shadows inherit exactly those angles. Third, isochronism becomes obvious — the radius A does not appear in ω anywhere, so the time to go round is independent of the size of the circle.

Timing questions, solved in one line

"How long does the oscillator take to travel from x = A to x = A/2?" On the reference circle that is a phase change of arccos(½) = 60° = π/3, so t = (π/3)/ω = T/6. Likewise A to 0 is 90°, i.e. T/4; A to -A is 180°, i.e. T/2. The trap is to assume the time is proportional to the distance travelled — going from A to A/2 covers only half the way to the centre but eats two-thirds of the T/4 needed to reach it (T/6 out of T/4), because the oscillator is still slow near the extreme. Proportionality would have predicted T/8.

§ 10

Damping: the three regimes

The ideaReal oscillators rub, push air and stir fluid, so they lose energy and die away instead of going on for ever. How much resistance there is changes the character of the motion completely: a little, and the thing wobbles for a while before settling; a lot, and it never wobbles at all but sags slowly back into place. Between those two lies a sweet spot that arrives back at rest as quickly as possible without ever going past it — exactly what you want from a measuring needle, a car suspension or a door closer.

Go deeper · why a galvanometer or balance pointer wants exactly critical damping

Why an instrument wants exactly critical damping

Consider a moving-coil galvanometer, or the pointer of an analytical balance. You want the needle to go to its reading and stop, as fast as possible, so the operator can take the value and move on. If it is underdamped the needle swings past the true reading and oscillates around it; you must wait several cycles, and a reading taken early is biased in whichever direction the needle happened to be swinging. If it is overdamped it never overshoots but crawls asymptotically towards the value, and you wait even longer for the last few percent. Critical damping is the single setting that reaches the reading soonest without ever passing it — which is why galvanometers are built with a specific coil resistance and why "critical damping resistance" appears on their specification sheets. The same argument settles car shock absorbers, self-closing doors, and the levelling of a lift.

The clinical version: the arterial line

An invasive blood-pressure line is a fluid-filled catheter plus transducer — a damped oscillator with a natural frequency. Underdamped (long compliant tubing, whip): the trace overshoots, overestimating systolic and underestimating diastolic pressure. Overdamped (air bubble, clot, kink): the trace is blunted and systolic is underestimated. The bedside test is the fast-flush (square-wave) test — count the oscillations after the flush. Note that the practical optimum here is slightly under critical (ζ ≈ 0.6–0.7), which trades a tiny overshoot for extra usable bandwidth; a pointer instrument that must never overshoot at all uses ζ = 1.

Two traps

"Only critical damping stops the oscillation." No — overdamped systems do not oscillate either. What is unique about critical damping is that it is the fastest non-oscillatory return.

"Damping does not change the frequency." It does: ω_d = √(ω₀² - b²/4m²) is always less than ω₀, so a damped oscillator is always a little slower than the same system undamped. The shift is second order in b, so it is tiny when damping is light — which is exactly why the approximation ω_d ≈ ω₀ is usually safe and why the exam likes asking about the sign of the shift rather than its size.

§ 11

Driving, resonance and Q

The ideaStop leaving the oscillator alone and start pushing it rhythmically, and it ends up moving at whatever rhythm you impose — not at the one it would have chosen. What its own preferred rhythm decides is how big the response is. Push at the rate the thing already likes and tiny repeated shoves pile up into an enormous motion, which is how a child on a swing gets high on almost no effort. This section is about how tall and how narrow that sweet spot is, and about the single number that describes both.

Start a driven oscillator and two things happen at once: a transient at the system's own damped frequency, which dies away, and a steady state at the driver's frequency, which does not. Only the steady state survives, so after the transient the oscillator moves at whatever frequency you feed it — never at its own. What its own frequency controls is the size of that response.

Look at the denominator of A(ω). The term (ω₀² - ω²)² vanishes when the driving matches the natural frequency, leaving only the damping term (bω/m)² to keep the amplitude finite. That is resonance in one sentence: at resonance nothing opposes the build-up except the damping, so a lightly damped system responds enormously and an undamped one would respond without limit.

The quality factor is one number wearing three hats

Q can be defined by the sharpness of the resonance (Q = f₀/Δf), by the height of the resonance (A(ω₀) = Q × F₀/k), or by the persistence of the free decay (|ΔE|/E = 2π/Q per cycle, so the amplitude falls to 1/e after about Q/π cycles). They are the same number because they all measure the same thing: how many oscillations the system gets through before the damping has taken its energy.

Worked example 7 — Q from a decay, and the bandwidth it implies

A tuning fork sounds at 440 Hz. After it is struck, its amplitude falls to 1/e of its initial value in 2.0 s. Find Q, the number of oscillations in that time, the fractional energy lost per cycle, and the width of its resonance.

  1. The amplitude decays as e^(-t/τ) with τ = 2m/b = 2.0 s. Since Q = ω₀m/b = ω₀τ/2, Q = πf₀τ = π × 440 × 2.0 = 2.8 × 10³.
  2. Number of cycles in that time: N = f₀τ = 440 × 2.0 = 880. Consistent with the rule of thumb Q = πN: π × 880 = 2765 ✓.
  3. Fractional energy lost per cycle: 2π/Q = 6.283/2765 = 2.3 × 10⁻³, about 0.23 % — which is precisely why a fork rings for seconds rather than milliseconds.
  4. Resonance width: Δf = f₀/Q = 440/2765 = 0.16 Hz. A fork will barely respond to a driver even one hertz off tune — the physical basis of using one as a frequency standard.
Q ≈ 2.8 × 10³ · 880 cycles · 0.23 % of E lost per cycle · Δf ≈ 0.16 Hz

Two of those rows are worth dwelling on. In MRI, an RF field drives proton spins at the Larmor frequency; because the resonance is so narrow, a deliberately imposed gradient in the magnetic field means only one thin slice of tissue is in resonance at a time — that is how spatial information gets into the image at all. In ultrasound imaging, exactly the opposite is wanted: a piezoelectric crystal driven at its thickness resonance would ring for many cycles, smearing echoes in depth, so a backing layer is bonded on to lower Q on purpose. Short pulse, broad bandwidth, sharp axial resolution. Sensitivity and resolution trade against each other through Q, and that trade-off is entirely this section's physics.

Where exactly is the peak?

The amplitude resonance sits at ω_r = √(ω₀² - b²/2m²), which is always below ω₀; damping never pushes the peak up. The shift is second order in the damping, so for anything with Q above about 5 it is invisible and "resonance is at ω₀" is a perfectly good working statement. Note also that velocity resonance — and therefore maximum power transfer from the driver — occurs exactly at ω₀, where the driving force is in phase with the velocity and the oscillator's displacement lags the driver by exactly a quarter cycle.

What is examined here

Q = f₀/Δf and 2π/Q as the fractional energy lost per cycle are the two numerical handles. Conceptually: the steady-state frequency is the driver's, never the oscillator's; the amplitude at resonance is finite only because of damping; and more damping means a lower, broader peak shifted slightly down in frequency. As for the famous engineering cautionary tales — soldiers breaking step on bridges is genuine forced resonance, and the Millennium Bridge sway was pedestrians synchronising with a lateral mode; the Tacoma Narrows collapse is usually quoted here but was aeroelastic flutter, a self-excited instability rather than simple forced resonance. Worth knowing if you want to use the example.
