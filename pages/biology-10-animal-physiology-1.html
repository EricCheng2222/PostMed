Animal Physiology I — Circulation, Gas Exchange, Digestion, Osmoregulation

Every organ system on this page exists to solve the same problem, and it is a geometry problem. Diffusion is superb over a micrometre and useless over a millimetre, because the time it takes rises with the square of the distance. Any animal thicker than about a millimetre therefore has to do three things: grow a thin, large, wet surface where exchange can happen; move bulk fluid past that surface on both sides so the gradient never runs down; and pump that fluid to within a diffusion distance of every cell. Lungs, gills, guts and kidneys are four versions of the same surface, and the heart and the vessels are the bulk-transport system that keeps them supplied. Once you see the shared logic, the details stop being four separate lists.

§ 01

Why size forces a circulatory system

The ideaMolecules wandering at random cover short distances quickly and long distances astonishingly slowly — going ten times further takes a hundred times longer, not ten times longer. A single cell can therefore supply itself by diffusion alone, and a jellyfish two cells thick can too. As soon as an animal is thicker than about a millimetre, the inside would suffocate before oxygen arrived. Everything that follows on this page — hearts, lungs, guts, kidneys — is the answer to that one arithmetic fact.

Definition & key facts

Exchange surface
Any thin, moist, highly folded epithelium across which substances move between the body and its surroundings — the alveolus, the gill lamella, the intestinal villus, the glomerulus. All four obey the same equation and all four solve the same three constraints: large area, short path, maintained gradient.
rate of exchange ∝ D · A · Δ[X]x Fick's law of diffusion — the design brief for every surface on this page
Full definition, symbols and units
Diffusion time
The mean time for a molecule to random-walk a distance x is t ≈ x²/2D in one dimension. The square is the whole point: diffusion is not slow, it is slow at scale.
Surface-to-volume ratio
For a sphere, SA/V = 3/r. Doubling the radius doubles the demand per unit of surface. Volume (which consumes O₂) grows as r³; surface (which supplies it) grows as r².
Bulk flow
Movement of a whole fluid down a pressure gradient, carrying its solutes with it. Bulk flow is fast, distance-insensitive and costs energy; diffusion is free but only works over microns. Every circulatory system is bulk flow bolted onto diffusion at both ends.
Gastrovascular cavity
A single dead-end sac (cnidarians, flatworms) that is simultaneously the gut and the transport system. It works only because the body wall is thin enough for diffusion to finish the job.

Run the numbers once and you never have to re-argue this. Oxygen's diffusion coefficient in water is about 2 × 10⁻⁹ m²·s⁻¹. Crossing a 1 µm capillary wall takes about a quarter of a millisecond. Crossing 1 mm — the thickness of a rather thin worm — takes about four minutes. Crossing 1 cm takes about seven hours. No tissue with a working metabolism can wait seven hours, so the interior of any animal thicker than roughly a millimetre must be supplied by something other than diffusion.

Notice that the fix is never "diffuse faster" — D is a property of the molecule and the medium and cannot be tuned. The fix is always to shorten x and enlarge A, and then to use bulk flow to carry material between surfaces. That is why a mammalian blood–gas barrier is only 0.2–0.6 µm thick, why the alveolar surface is 70–100 m², and why no cell in your body sits more than about 20–50 µm from a capillary. The circulatory system does not replace diffusion; it delivers the fluid to within a diffusion distance of everything.

The shape of the exam question

This section is rarely examined for its own sake, but it is the hidden premise of a whole class of questions — "why do larger animals need X", "why is the alveolar surface folded", "why can an insect not grow to the size of a dog". The examiner is testing whether you can reach for surface-to-volume and the x² scaling rather than reciting an adaptation. When an option says an adaptation "increases the rate of diffusion", check which term of Fick's law it actually changes: almost always A or x, never D.

Three ways of getting round the problem, and their limits

Animals have found exactly three solutions and every phylum uses some combination of them. Stay thin — flatworms are flattened precisely so that no cell is far from the surface, and their branched gastrovascular cavity brings the "gut" to within diffusion range of everything as well. Bring the outside in — the insect tracheal system pipes air itself, not a carrier fluid, to the cells, which is beautifully efficient over short distances and is the main reason insects have a hard ceiling on body size. Move a fluid — a circulatory system, which is the vertebrate answer and the subject of the next ten sections.

The three constraints, in the order they get examined

Area, path length, gradient. Every exchange adaptation you will ever be asked about is one of these three. Folding (villi, lamellae, alveoli) buys area; a one-cell-thick epithelium buys path length; ventilation and perfusion — and counter-current flow — buy gradient. If an option does none of the three, it is not an exchange adaptation.

§ 02

Circulatory plans: open or closed, single or double

The ideaTwo independent design choices are being made, and candidates routinely blur them. The first is whether the fluid stays inside vessels the whole way round or is dumped into the body cavity and sloshes back — open versus closed. The second, which only arises in animals with lungs, is whether blood passes through the heart once or twice per circuit — single versus double. The second choice exists because passing through a capillary bed destroys pressure, and if you want two capillary beds in series you must re-pressurise between them.

Definition & key facts

Open circulation
One or more hearts pump haemolymph into open sinuses (the haemocoel), where it directly bathes the tissues, then drains back through ostia. There is no distinction between blood and interstitial fluid. Arthropods and most molluscs.
single: heart → gills → body → heart · double: heart → lungs → heart → body → heart the whole point of "double" is a second pressure boost between two capillary beds
Full definition, symbols and units
Closed circulation
Blood is confined to vessels throughout; exchange happens only across capillary walls, and the interstitial fluid is a separate compartment derived from it. Annelids, cephalopods and all vertebrates.
Single circulation
Blood passes through the heart once per circuit. Fish: heart → gill capillaries → systemic capillaries → heart. Two capillary beds in series with one pump.
Double circulation
Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit — a pulmonary (or pulmocutaneous) loop and a systemic loop. Amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals.
Portal system
Two capillary beds in series within the systemic circulation, joined by a vein rather than returned to the heart — hepatic portal (gut → liver), hypophyseal portal, and the renal glomerulus → peritubular capillaries.

Three statements about open circulation that sound right and are not

"Open circulation is simply less efficient." It is lower-pressure and less controllable, but it is also far cheaper to run, and for a small, moderately active animal that is a reasonable trade. Evolution did not fail to invent a closed system; it declined to pay for one.

"Insect haemolymph carries oxygen to the tissues." It generally does not. Insects deliver O₂ through the tracheal system, straight to the cells; haemolymph carries nutrients, hormones and wastes. This is why the low pressure of an open system is not the handicap it looks like — and why insect haemolymph is usually colourless.

"All molluscs have open circulation." Cephalopods — squid, octopus — are closed, with a systemic heart and two branchial hearts, and they are among the most active invertebrates alive. The exam likes this exception precisely because the textbook sentence "molluscs have open circulatory systems" is quotable and wrong.

Chambers: counting them is the easy part, understanding the mixing is not

A fish heart is described as two-chambered (atrium and ventricle), with a sinus venosus upstream and a conus or bulbus arteriosus downstream that smooths the pulse. All the blood in it is deoxygenated, and the plan is simply "one pump in series with two beds".

An amphibian has two atria and one ventricle. The textbook line is that oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix — and they do, but far less than the anatomy suggests. A ridge in the ventricle and a spiral valve in the conus arteriosus keep the two streams substantially separate, so the left-atrial (oxygenated) blood goes preferentially to the systemic arches and right-atrial blood to the pulmocutaneous arch. Note also that the loop is pulmocutaneous: a frog's skin is a major gas exchange surface, and an entirely submerged frog can survive on cutaneous exchange alone. Being able to shunt blood away from the lungs during a dive is a feature, not a defect.

Most reptiles have three chambers with a partial interventricular septum; the muscular ridge plus pressure differences again separate the streams, and crocodilians have a genuinely complete septum with four chambers. Birds and mammals both have four chambers with total separation — and they evolved that condition independently, from different three-chambered reptilian ancestors. It is a classic case of convergent evolution driven by the same requirement: endothermy has a metabolic rate roughly ten times that of an equivalent ectotherm, and that demands both undiluted arterial O₂ and a high-pressure systemic circuit.

Chamber counts, including the exception the examiner wants

Fish 2 · Frog 3 · Lizard 3½ · Croc, Bird, Beast 4

Ours rather than a standard hook, but one rising count carries the whole comparative table. Say the numbers first — two, three, three and a half, four — and the animals hang themselves on them. Fish 2 — one atrium and one ventricle, all of it deoxygenated. Frog 3 — amphibian, two atria and one ventricle. Lizard 3½ — the non-crocodilian reptiles, three chambers with a partial interventricular septum. Croc, Bird, Beast 4 — four chambers with complete separation, and the point of naming all three together is that the crocodile belongs with the birds and mammals, not with the other reptiles. Read the half honestly: it stands for the incomplete septum, not for half a chamber, so if you are asked how many chambers a lizard has the answer is three. The count also says nothing about mixing, and mixing is where the marks are: a three-chambered heart separates its two streams far better than the chamber count suggests, because of the ventricular ridge and the spiral valve. Note finally that birds and mammals arrived at four chambers independently, so "birds are descended from mammals" or any shared-ancestry option is wrong.

The trap that gets set almost every year

"The left ventricle pumps more blood than the right." False, and it must be false: the two are in series, so over any period longer than a few beats they must eject identical volumes, or blood would accumulate on one side. The left ventricle has thicker myocardium because it generates about five times the peak systolic pressure (120 against 25 mmHg) and pushes that identical flow across a pressure difference roughly nine times as large — which is exactly what it must do against a systemic resistance roughly nine times the pulmonary, since Q = ΔP/R has to give the same Q on both sides. Same volume, different pressure — that is the whole of it. The related trap is "pulmonary arteries carry oxygenated blood": in an adult they do not; arteries are defined by direction (away from the heart), not by oxygen content, and the pulmonary artery and the umbilical vein are the two standard counterexamples.

Exam technique · comparative-circulation options die on four rules, not on recall

  1. Kill on "in series" — the two ventricles are in series, so any option that gives them different volumes per minute is impossible. Same for any option that lets one side of the heart "pump harder and therefore more blood". Thicker wall means more pressure, never more volume. This single rule kills at least one option most times it appears.
  2. Kill on the definition of artery and vein — direction, not oxygen. Any option that defines an artery as "carrying oxygenated blood" is wrong on its face, and the two counterexamples to quote are the pulmonary artery and the umbilical vein.
  3. Kill on the two exceptions this topic is built around — cephalopods are closed (so "all molluscs have open circulation" is a textbook sentence that is false), and insect haemolymph carries little or no O₂ (tracheae do that job, so "open circulation limits insect size because haemolymph delivers oxygen slowly" is wrong for the reason it gives, even where its conclusion is popular). Distractors on this paper are often lifted verbatim from textbook prose — sounding familiar is not evidence.
  4. If the stem says "two capillary beds in series", answer pressure — that is the recognition cue for single-versus-double circulation, and the answer is always some form of "a bed destroys pressure, so you need a pump before each one". Portal systems are the same cue pointed at the systemic side.
  5. Spend ten seconds here, not sixty — comparative-plan questions are recall-plus-elimination and should be answered fast, banking time for the arithmetic in § 03, § 06 and § 11. If you have eliminated even one of the five options, answer and move on: with −0.25 marking, a one-option elimination already makes answering better than a blank.
§ 03

The heart: the cardiac cycle, the conduction system and the ECG

The ideaThe heart is two pumps side by side, each with a filling chamber and an ejecting chamber, and four one-way valves that make the whole thing a ratchet. Nothing about the sequence needs memorising if you keep one rule in mind: a valve is opened and closed by the pressure difference across it, never by a nerve or a muscle. Work out which chamber currently has the higher pressure and you have derived which valves are open, which sounds are heard, and what the volume is doing.

Definition & key facts

Cardiac output
The volume of blood ejected by one ventricle per minute — the single number that describes how well the circulation is working. At rest it is close to the whole blood volume, so your blood makes roughly one complete circuit per minute.
CO = HR × SV = 72 min⁻¹ × 70 mL ≈ 5.0 L·min⁻¹ and SV = EDV − ESV, so anything that changes filling changes output
Full definition, symbols and units
Systole / diastole
Contraction and relaxation of a chamber. Unqualified, they refer to the ventricles. At 72 beats·min⁻¹ the cycle lasts 0.83 s, of which systole is about 0.3 s — so roughly 64 % of the cycle is diastole, and that is when the ventricles fill and when the left coronary artery is perfused.
Ejection fraction
EF = SV/EDV. Normal 55–70 %. It is the index of contractility, because it asks what fraction of the available blood the ventricle managed to expel.
Preload / afterload
Preload is the stretch on the myocardium at end-diastole — essentially EDV, and hence venous return. Afterload is the pressure the ventricle must exceed to open the semilunar valve — essentially arterial pressure.
Autorhythmicity
Cardiac muscle generates its own rhythm; a denervated heart still beats. Nerves and hormones modulate an intrinsic rhythm, they do not create it. A transplanted heart runs at about 100 min⁻¹ because it has lost vagal tone.

Following the pressure round one beat

Take the left side. In late diastole the mitral valve is open because atrial pressure exceeds ventricular, and the ventricle is filling passively — most of it is already in before the atrium does anything. Atrial systole then tops it up. Here is the first commonly examined number: at rest, atrial contraction contributes only about 20–25 % of ventricular filling, which is why people in atrial fibrillation are not in immediate danger at rest but become breathless on exertion, when the shortened diastole makes that last 25 % matter.

Ventricular systole begins. Ventricular pressure immediately exceeds atrial, the mitral valve slams shut — that is the first heart sound (S1) — but it is still far below aortic pressure, so the aortic valve stays shut too. For a brief interval all four valves are closed and the ventricle contracts without changing volume: isovolumetric contraction. When ventricular pressure finally exceeds aortic (~80 mmHg), the aortic valve opens and ejection begins. As the ventricle relaxes, pressure falls back below aortic and the aortic valve closes — the second heart sound (S2), and the small notch on the aortic pressure trace (the dicrotic notch) is the elastic recoil of the aorta against the closed valve. Then another all-valves-closed interval, isovolumetric relaxation, until ventricular pressure drops below atrial and the mitral valve opens for the next filling phase.

atrial systole→S1 · isovolumetric contraction→ ejection→S2 · isovolumetric relaxation→filling

What the heart sounds actually are

S1 and S2 are produced by valve closure and the turbulence that follows it, not by muscle contracting and not by valves opening — healthy valves open silently. A useful consequence: S1 marks the start of ventricular systole and S2 the end, so the interval between S1 and S2 is systole and the longer gap from S2 to the next S1 is diastole. A murmur between S1 and S2 is a systolic murmur, and the two commonest causes are a leaking mitral valve or a narrowed aortic valve — which is exactly what you would predict from asking which valves ought to be shut and which ought to be wide open at that moment.

There is no 口訣 for the cardiac cycle, and you must not build one

Five phases, four valves, two sounds and two isovolumetric intervals look like a list to memorise, and a memorised list is exactly what fails under time pressure, because the exam asks it from the middle ("during isovolumetric relaxation, which valves are open?") rather than from the start. Run the note's own rule instead: a valve is opened and closed by the pressure difference across it, and by nothing else. Given any moment, ask which chamber currently has the higher pressure; that fixes which valves are open, and everything else follows — no flow across a shut valve, so volume is constant, so you are in an isovolumetric phase; a valve shutting makes a sound, a valve opening makes none. Two anchors are worth having cold because they orient the whole cycle: S1 opens systole, S2 closes it, so S1→S2 is systole and the longer S2→S1 gap is diastole. Everything else is derived on the spot, and an option that has a nerve, a muscle or an opening valve producing a heart sound is wrong before you have thought about the phase at all.

The conduction system, and why every part of it is where it is

The sinoatrial node sits in the wall of the right atrium near the entry of the superior vena cava. Its cells have no stable resting potential: a slow inward "funny" current (If, carried mainly by Na⁺ through HCN channels) drifts the membrane upward, T-type then L-type Ca²⁺ channels take over, and threshold is reached. The upstroke of a nodal action potential is carried by Ca²⁺, not Na⁺ — this is examined, and it is why calcium-channel blockers such as verapamil slow the heart while having little effect on ventricular muscle's fast Na⁺ upstroke.

The impulse spreads through atrial muscle to the atrioventricular node. The atria and ventricles are electrically insulated from one another by the fibrous skeleton of the heart, so the AV node is the only electrical connection between them. Its conduction is deliberately slow — about 0.05 m·s⁻¹, giving a delay near 0.1 s — and that delay is what allows the atria to finish emptying before the ventricles contract. From there the bundle of His descends the interventricular septum, divides into left and right bundle branches, and terminates in the Purkinje fibres, the fastest-conducting tissue in the heart at 2–4 m·s⁻¹.

The Purkinje network delivers the impulse to the apex first, so the ventricles contract from the bottom upward, wringing blood toward the outflow valves at the top. A pump that squeezed top-first would trap blood at the apex. Every feature of the conduction system is therefore doing one of two jobs: imposing the atria-then-ventricles sequence, or making ventricular contraction near-simultaneous and directional.

Three things the ECG is not

It is not a record of contraction. It records the spread of electrical excitation. Mechanical events follow each electrical one by a short delay, and in some catastrophes (pulseless electrical activity) the trace looks nearly normal while nothing is pumping at all. So "the T wave is ventricular relaxation" is wrong twice: it is repolarisation, and repolarisation is not relaxation.

There is no atrial T wave on the trace. Atrial repolarisation is small and coincides with the QRS, which swamps it. An option offering "the wave between QRS and T is atrial repolarisation" is describing something that does not exist.

It does not show the pacemaker firing. The nodal action potential involves too little tissue to register; the P wave is the atrial muscle responding to it.

The normal ECG durations, without memorising four decimals

Small square 0.04 s — P 2½, PR 3 to 5, QRS under 3

The small-square rule itself is the standard clinical one and you will meet it everywhere; counting the three waves off it in one breath — two and a half, three to five, under three — is ours. ECG paper runs at 25 mm·s⁻¹, so one small square is 0.04 s and the whole table becomes small integers. P 2½ — up to 2½ squares, 0.08–0.10 s. PR 3 to 5 — 3 to 5 squares, 0.12–0.20 s; longer than five is first-degree AV block, and note that five small squares is one large box, so "PR must fit inside one big box" is the same rule read off the paper. QRS under 3 — under 3 squares, < 0.12 s; three or more means the impulse has bypassed the Purkinje system. For QT, the bedside rule is that it should be under half the R–R interval at a normal rate: at 72 min⁻¹ the R–R is 0.83 s, half of it 0.42 s, which lands inside the quoted 0.35–0.44 s. Notice the coincidence you can exploit: 3 small squares is both the lower bound of PR and the upper bound of QRS, so one number anchors two rows. The hook covers durations only — it says nothing about what a segment means, and the ST segment and T wave have no normal duration to encode. It also does not encode the QT bound as squares, because that bound moves with heart rate.

Worked example 1 — cardiac output, ejection fraction, and what a fast heart actually costs

A resting adult has EDV 120 mL, ESV 50 mL and HR 72 min⁻¹. (a) Find SV, CO and EF. (b) The heart rate rises to 180 min⁻¹ during exercise and systole shortens from 0.30 s to 0.20 s. How much time is left for diastole per beat, and what fraction of the cycle is that? (c) Why does this matter for the heart muscle itself?

  1. (a) Stroke volume. SV = EDV − ESV = 120 − 50 = 70 mL.
  2. Cardiac output. CO = 72 × 70 = 5040 mL·min⁻¹ = 5.04 L·min⁻¹. With a blood volume near 5 L, the entire circulation turns over about once a minute.
  3. Ejection fraction. EF = 70/120 = 0.583 = 58 % — normal. Note it is not 70/50; dividing by ESV is the standard slip and gives an impossible 140 %.
  4. (b) At rest. Cycle = 60/72 = 0.833 s; diastole = 0.833 − 0.30 = 0.533 s, which is 0.533/0.833 = 64 % of the cycle.
  5. At 180 min⁻¹. Cycle = 60/180 = 0.333 s; diastole = 0.333 − 0.20 = 0.133 s, only 40 % of the cycle. Diastole has been cut to a quarter of its resting duration while systole has fallen by only a third.
  6. (c) The consequence. Filling time collapses, so beyond roughly 180 min⁻¹ stroke volume starts to fall and cardiac output stops rising. Worse, the left ventricular myocardium is perfused almost entirely during diastole — during systole the contracting wall squeezes its own coronary vessels shut. So a very fast heart simultaneously demands more oxygen and gives itself less time to receive it. That is the mechanism of exertional angina, and the reason β-blockers, which slow the heart, relieve it.

Exam technique · EDV, ESV and a heart rate — the 40-second recipe

  1. Recognise it instantly — if the stem hands you two ventricular volumes and a rate, nothing conceptual is being tested. Do not re-read the physiology; run the chain.
  2. Always the same three lines — SV = EDV − ESV, then CO = SV × HR (in mL·min⁻¹, divide by 1000 at the end), then EF = SV ÷ EDV. Write SV down before you look at the options; the options are built to make you skip it.
  3. Kill the EF decoy first — SV/ESV is the single commonest slip and there is always an option carrying it. Test it for free: that quotient can exceed 100 %, and a fraction ejected cannot, so the definition is impossible. With EDV 140 and ESV 84 it gives 67 % against the correct 40 % — and both numbers will be on the page.
  4. Kill on magnitude before you finish — a resting adult CO lands near 5 L·min⁻¹. An option near 12 L·min⁻¹ has used EDV as the stroke volume (140 × 90 = 12 600 mL); one near 7.6 L·min⁻¹ has used ESV. Both are strikeable without computing anything, purely on physiological scale.
  5. Read which quantity was asked — "cardiac output" and "stroke volume" appear in the same option set, and so do "% " and "fraction". A CO that looks normal can sit on a badly reduced EF, which is precisely why a stem asks for both.
  6. If the stem gives a time instead — systole is roughly fixed while diastole is what a rising rate eats. Cycle length is 60/HR; subtract systole to get diastole. At 72 min⁻¹ that is 0.833 − 0.30 = 0.533 s (64 % of the cycle); at 180 it is 0.333 − 0.20 = 0.133 s (40 %). Any option claiming systole and diastole shorten proportionally is wrong.
Go deeper — Frank–Starling, and why the two ventricles never drift apart

Stretch a strip of cardiac muscle further before you stimulate it and it contracts harder. That is the Frank–Starling law: within limits, stroke volume rises with end-diastolic volume. Two mechanisms contribute. The familiar one is sarcomere geometry — a resting cardiac sarcomere sits below its optimum length, so stretching improves thick–thin filament overlap. The larger contribution, and the one textbooks often omit, is length-dependent activation: stretch increases the Ca²⁺ sensitivity of troponin C, so the same calcium transient produces more force.

The system consequence is elegant. Suppose the right ventricle transiently ejects 1 mL more than the left. That extra millilitre arrives at the left atrium, raises left ventricular EDV, and by Frank–Starling the left ventricle ejects more on the next beat. The two outputs are matched automatically, beat by beat, with no nervous control at all. This is also why a sudden increase in venous return — standing up from a squat, or a rapid infusion — raises cardiac output without any change in heart rate.

The pressure–volume loop puts the whole cycle on one diagram: volume on the x-axis, ventricular pressure on the y-axis. It is traced anticlockwise as a rectangle with rounded corners. The bottom edge is filling (volume rises, pressure barely does), the right edge is isovolumetric contraction (pressure rises, volume fixed), the top edge is ejection (volume falls at high pressure), and the left edge is isovolumetric relaxation. Its width is the stroke volume and its enclosed area is the stroke work. Increasing preload widens the loop to the right; increasing afterload raises the top edge and narrows the loop; increasing contractility raises the end-systolic pressure–volume relationship so the loop empties further left. Being able to say which edge moves in each case is a much stronger form of knowing Frank–Starling than a sentence about sarcomere length.

Autonomic control, in the form the exam uses it

Sympathetic (noradrenaline → β₁ receptors → cAMP) increases rate (chronotropy, by steepening the pacemaker drift), conduction velocity through the AV node (dromotropy) and force (inotropy, by loading the sarcoplasmic reticulum with more Ca²⁺). Parasympathetic (vagus nerve → acetylcholine → M₂ receptors) does the opposite, but supplies the nodes and atria far more densely than the ventricles — so vagal stimulation slows the heart much more than it weakens it. At rest, vagal tone dominates: the intrinsic SA rate is about 100 min⁻¹ and a resting pulse of 70 is a heart being held back. Cut both nerves and the rate rises. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch close the loop: a rise in arterial pressure increases their firing, which inhibits sympathetic outflow and raises vagal tone, dropping rate and pressure. The sign of that reflex is worth checking carefully — more stretch means more firing means less drive.

§ 04

Vessels: pressure, velocity, area — and how capillaries exchange

The ideaTwo relations run this whole section and they pull in opposite directions, which is why they are so often confused. The first is about pressure: flow through a tube is the pressure difference divided by the resistance, and resistance depends ferociously on radius. The second is about speed: the same total flow squeezed through a bigger total cross-section must move more slowly. A single capillary is narrow, so it is a big resistance — but there are billions of them in parallel, so the total area is enormous and the blood crawls. Narrow vessel, slow blood. That is not a contradiction; it is the point.

Definition & key facts

Vascular resistance
The opposition a vessel offers to flow. For steady laminar flow of a Newtonian fluid through a rigid cylinder it is given by Poiseuille's relation, and the striking feature is the fourth power of the radius — a vessel that dilates by 19 % doubles its flow.
Q = ΔPR  ·  R = 8ηLπr⁴  ·  Q = A · v flow, resistance, and the continuity relation that sets velocity
Full definition, symbols and units
Arteriole
The resistance vessel. A thick ring of smooth muscle on a narrow lumen, densely innervated. Arterioles contribute roughly half of total peripheral resistance and are where the biggest pressure drop in the circulation occurs. Changing their radius is how the body redirects flow between organs.
Capillary
The exchange vessel: a single layer of endothelium on a basement membrane, wall ~0.5 µm thick, lumen 5–10 µm so red cells must deform to pass. Three types — continuous (muscle, brain), fenestrated (gut, glomerulus, endocrine), sinusoidal/discontinuous (liver, spleen, marrow), in order of increasing leakiness.
Vein
The capacitance vessel. Thin-walled, distensible, valved; the venous side holds about 60–65 % of total blood volume at any instant. Venoconstriction is therefore a way of transfusing yourself from your own reservoir.

The capillary bed has a combined cross-section near 2500 cm² — a thousand times greater — so the same flow moves at about 0.033 cm·s⁻¹ = 0.33 mm·s⁻¹. A red cell therefore spends roughly 1.5 s traversing a half-millimetre capillary, and that dwell time is not an accident: it is what makes exchange possible at all. Speed up the blood and you starve the tissue even though you are delivering more of it per minute.

The single most-missed relationship in this topic

"Blood is slowest in capillaries because they are the narrowest vessels." The conclusion is right, the reason is wrong, and the wrong reason produces wrong answers elsewhere. Velocity depends on total cross-sectional area of the whole class of vessels in parallel, not on the bore of one of them. If you doubt it, note that a venule is wider than a capillary yet blood in venules moves faster, because there are far fewer of them and the total area has fallen. Resistance, by contrast, does depend on individual radius — which is why the same capillaries are simultaneously a place of low velocity and, individually, high resistance. Two different questions.

Exam technique · decide which of the three relations the stem is using, then run it

  1. Triage on the noun in the question — the word flow or resistance means Q = ΔP/R with R ∝ 1/r⁴. The word velocity, speed or how long a red cell spends means v = Q/A with A the total area of the class. The words filtration, oedema or fluid leaving mean Starling. Three relations, three trigger words, and picking the wrong one is how this section is failed.
  2. Radius questions: raise the factor to the fourth power — and expect the decoy ladder, because it is always there. A radius rise of 50 % gives 1.5⁴ = 5.06 ×, and the other options will be 1.5 (treating Q ∝ r), 2.25 (r², the cross-sectional-area answer) and 3.4 (r³). Three of the five options are pre-eliminated the moment you recognise the ladder. Two more worth having ready: halving r multiplies resistance by 16, and a 19 % dilation almost exactly doubles flow (1.19⁴ = 2.01).
  3. Velocity questions: never answer from the bore of one vessel — the killer test is the venule. A venule is wider than a capillary and blood in it moves faster, so any option that explains slow capillary flow by "capillaries are the narrowest vessels" has the right conclusion for a reason that fails on the next question. The correct clause is always "greatest total cross-sectional area".
  4. Starling questions: one term has changed, name it — swelling means ↑Pc, ↓πp, ↑permeability, or a blocked lymphatic, and the stem always tells you which. Signs are the whole difficulty: hydrostatic pushes out, oncotic pulls in, so NFP = (Pc − Pif) − (πp − πif) and a positive NFP is filtration. Sanity check: at the venous end the answer must come out negative with normal numbers, so if you get a positive NFP at both ends you have dropped a sign.
  5. Use MAP as a free arithmetic check — from "120 over 80" you can always produce pulse pressure 40 and MAP = 80 + ⅓(40) = 93 mmHg. MAP must sit nearer the diastolic value; any option offering the arithmetic mean of 100 has used the wrong formula and can be struck immediately.

Capillary exchange: three mechanisms, only one of which moves much solute

Substances cross a capillary wall three ways. Diffusion down concentration gradients does essentially all of the useful work: lipid-soluble molecules including O₂ and CO₂ straight through the endothelial membranes, water-soluble ones through intercellular clefts and fenestrations. Transcytosis in vesicles handles a few large molecules. Bulk flow — the filtration and reabsorption of fluid driven by pressure — moves a great deal of water but relatively little net solute, because the fluid leaving and returning carries roughly the same small solutes. Bulk flow is about fluid balance, not nutrition. An exam option claiming that glucose reaches cells "by bulk flow through the capillary wall" is wrong: it diffuses.

The direction of bulk flow at any point is set by four pressures, two pushing water out and two pulling it back — the Starling forces. Capillary hydrostatic pressure Pc pushes out; plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure πp, generated almost entirely by albumin, pulls in; the interstitial values do the reverse and are small. The key asymmetry is that Pc falls along the capillary as pressure is consumed, while πp stays essentially constant — proteins do not leave. So the net force reverses somewhere along the way.

Worked example 2 — Starling forces, and where the "missing" fluid goes

A systemic capillary has Pc = 35 mmHg at its arterial end and 15 mmHg at its venous end. Plasma oncotic pressure is 25 mmHg; take interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures as approximately zero. (a) Find the net filtration pressure at each end. (b) Roughly 20 L of fluid is filtered per day across the whole body and about 17 L is reabsorbed. Account for the difference. (c) Predict what happens in severe liver disease or nephrotic syndrome.

  1. (a) Arterial end. NFP = (35 − 0) − (25 − 0) = +10 mmHg — positive, so filtration out.
  2. Venous end. NFP = (15 − 0) − (25 − 0) = −10 mmHg — negative, so reabsorption in. The reversal happens where Pc falls through 25 mmHg, near the middle.
  3. (b) The balance. 20 − 17 = 3 L·day⁻¹, i.e. 3/20 = 15 % of what is filtered, does not come back through the capillary wall. It is collected by the lymphatic system and returned to the venous circulation at the subclavian veins. Lymphatics also retrieve the small quantity of plasma protein that escapes — which matters, because if protein accumulated interstitially, πif would rise and filtration would run away.
  4. (c) Low albumin. A failing liver cannot synthesise albumin; a leaking glomerulus loses it in urine. Either way πp falls, say to 15 mmHg. Now the venous end gives (15 − 0) − (15 − 0) = 0 mmHg — no reabsorption at all — and the arterial end gives +20 mmHg. Filtration doubles, reabsorption stops, and the lymphatics are overwhelmed: oedema. This is why children with severe protein malnutrition (kwashiorkor) have swollen abdomens, and it is the cleanest demonstration that oncotic pressure is a real force and not a bookkeeping device.
+10 mmHg out at the arterial end · −10 mmHg in at the venous end · ≈3 L·day⁻¹ returned as lymph
Go deeper — the revised Starling principle, and why the classic picture is only half right

The textbook diagram of filtration at the arterial end and equal reabsorption at the venous end is a teaching model, and it is the model the exam expects. It is also, in most tissues, not what happens in the steady state. Two corrections matter.

First, the osmotic barrier is not the endothelial cell layer but the endothelial glycocalyx, a gel of proteoglycans coating the luminal surface. The oncotic pressure that opposes filtration is the difference across that glycocalyx — between plasma and the tiny protein-poor space immediately beneath it — not between plasma and the bulk interstitium. Because that sub-glycocalyx space is kept nearly protein-free by the outward flow itself, the effective oncotic gradient stays high.

Second, and following from it, filtration is low but continuous along the whole length of most capillaries, and sustained venular reabsorption does not occur. Fluid returns to the circulation via the lymphatics, not by re-entering the venous end. The classic reversal does occur transiently — after haemorrhage, for instance, when Pc falls sharply and the capillary bed autotransfuses interstitial fluid into the plasma — and sustained reabsorption is genuinely the normal state in two special beds: the renal peritubular capillaries and the intestinal mucosa, which is exactly where you would want it.

Why bother knowing this? Because it is a good example of the thing this paper likes to punish. A quoted textbook sentence — "fluid filtered at the arterial end is reabsorbed at the venous end" — is a simplification with a known boundary. On this paper you should answer with the classic model unless the stem signals otherwise, but you should know it is a model.

Oedema: four mechanisms, one equation

Every cause of oedema is one term of the Starling equation, or the lymphatic drain. ↑Pc — heart failure, venous obstruction, prolonged standing. ↓πp — liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition. ↑capillary permeability — inflammation and burns, where protein leaks out and raises πif as well. Lymphatic obstruction — surgical node clearance, or the filarial worms that cause elephantiasis. If a question describes swelling, ask which of those four has changed; you will never need to memorise a list of causes.

Getting blood back: the venous side has no ventricle of its own

By the time blood reaches the venules the pressure is around 10–15 mmHg and at the right atrium it is essentially zero, so almost none of the driving force from the left ventricle survives. Venous return therefore depends on three auxiliary pumps. The skeletal muscle pump squeezes deep veins during contraction, and one-way valves ensure the squeeze moves blood only toward the heart — which is why soldiers standing rigidly to attention faint and marching soldiers do not. The respiratory pump lowers intrathoracic pressure on inspiration, sucking blood into the great veins. And venoconstriction shifts blood out of the capacitance reservoir. Failure of the valves gives varicose veins; failure of the whole system on standing gives orthostatic hypotension.

§ 05

Blood: what is in it, and how a hole gets plugged

The ideaBlood is a tissue whose matrix happens to be liquid. Spin it and it separates into a straw-coloured fluid carrying dissolved proteins and a packed red sediment of cells; almost every property of blood belongs clearly to one half or the other. Stopping a leak is then a three-stage job — narrow the pipe, stick a temporary plug of platelets over the hole, and finally weave a protein mesh through the plug to make it permanent. Each stage buys time for the next.

Definition & key facts

Haematocrit
The fraction of blood volume occupied by red cells — about 45 % (men 40–52 %, women 36–48 %). It is the main determinant of blood viscosity, and since resistance is proportional to viscosity, a very high haematocrit makes the heart work considerably harder.

Stage by stage: how a cut in an arteriole is closed

Damage exposes subendothelial collagen. Local myogenic reflexes and endothelin narrow the vessel, cutting the flow that would wash away any plug. Von Willebrand factor binds the exposed collagen and, through platelet glycoprotein Ib, tethers platelets to it — a step that must happen first, because platelets cannot stick to fast-flowing arterial shear on their own. Adhered platelets change shape, degranulate, and release ADP and thromboxane A₂, which recruit and activate more platelets, positive-feedback style. Activation flips glycoprotein IIb/IIIa into its high-affinity form, and fibrinogen bridges adjacent platelets through it, aggregating them into a plug. That is primary haemostasis: fast, but fragile.

Coagulation then converts the plug into something permanent. The cascade is a series of proteolytic activations — each factor, once cut, cuts the next — which delivers enormous amplification: a few molecules of tissue factor generate thrombin by the millions. Thrombin is the hinge of the whole system. It converts soluble fibrinogen to fibrin monomers that polymerise into a mesh; it activates factor XIII, which covalently cross-links that mesh; and it feeds back to activate factors V, VIII and XI and to activate more platelets. Days later, plasmin — generated from plasminogen by tissue plasminogen activator released from healthy endothelium — digests the clot away.

Which clotting factors need vitamin K

1972 — X, IX, VII, II

This is the standard, widely circulating one and the reason to use it is that you will meet it in textbooks and USMLE material rather than anything of ours. Read the factors down in descending order — X, IX, VII, II, that is 10, 9, 7, 2 — and the digits fall out as the year 1972. Learn it in Roman numerals, since that is how the paper will name them. Those four are the γ-carboxylated factors: the liver cannot finish them without vitamin K, which is exactly what warfarin blocks, and why warfarin takes days to work (existing factors must first be cleared) and does nothing at all to blood already in a tube. Two omissions, and both are examined. (1) The natural anticoagulants protein C and protein S are vitamin-K-dependent too, and 1972 does not contain them — bolt them on by hand. (2) The hook says nothing about which arm each factor sits in, and that is a separate question: VII is the extrinsic one (with tissue factor, measured by PT/INR), IX is intrinsic (measured by aPTT), and II and X are common. Nor does it tell you the pathway's shape, which needs no mnemonic at all — everything converges on X, and after X the route is common: X → thrombin → fibrin → XIII.

Go deeper — the cascade in full, why the "two pathways" are a laboratory artefact, and the drugs

Extrinsic pathway. Tissue factor (III), exposed when the vessel wall is breached, binds factor VII and activates it. The TF·VIIa complex activates factor X. Two steps, seconds, and this is the route that actually initiates clotting in the body. Measured by the prothrombin time (PT/INR).

Intrinsic pathway. Factor XII is activated by contact with a negatively charged surface (collagen in vivo, glass in a tube), then XI → IX; IXa with its cofactor VIIIa activates X. Slower, more steps. Measured by the aPTT.

Common pathway. Xa, with cofactor Va, Ca²⁺ (factor IV) and a platelet phospholipid surface, forms prothrombinase, which converts prothrombin (II) to thrombin (IIa). Thrombin makes fibrin from fibrinogen (I) and activates XIII.

The correction that matters. The neat split into two independent pathways is a description of two laboratory tests, not of physiology. In vivo, coagulation is initiated almost entirely by tissue factor, and the "intrinsic" factors are then recruited by thrombin itself (thrombin activates XI) to sustain the burst. The clinical proof is decisive: factor XII deficiency causes no bleeding at all, despite a grossly prolonged aPTT — whereas deficiency of factor VIII or IX gives haemophilia A or B. If the classic cascade were literally true, XII deficiency ought to be catastrophic.

Where the drugs act, because each one tells you a mechanism. Aspirin irreversibly acetylates cyclo-oxygenase, blocking thromboxane A₂ — platelet aggregation, and irreversibly, because a platelet has no nucleus to make new enzyme. Clopidogrel blocks the platelet ADP receptor P2Y12. Heparin accelerates antithrombin III, inactivating thrombin and Xa immediately. Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase, so the liver cannot γ-carboxylate factors II, VII, IX and X — which means it takes days to work and none at all in vitro. Alteplase is recombinant tPA and dissolves formed clot. Note the natural anticoagulants using the same vitamin: protein C and protein S inactivate factors Va and VIIIa, and healthy endothelium continuously releases prostacyclin, nitric oxide and thrombomodulin so that clotting stays where the damage is.

Anaemia is not one disease, and the exam knows it

Approach any anaemia through MCV. Microcytic (MCV < 80): iron deficiency, thalassaemia — the cell cannot fill itself with haemoglobin, so it divides once more and ends up small. Macrocytic (MCV > 100): B₁₂ or folate deficiency, where DNA synthesis fails while cytoplasmic growth continues, so the cell is large and its nucleus immature — and B₁₂ deficiency additionally causes neurological damage that folate does not, which is why treating with folate alone is dangerous. Normocytic: acute blood loss, haemolysis, chronic kidney disease (no EPO). Two mechanisms worth holding: pernicious anaemia is autoimmune destruction of the gastric parietal cells, so the missing molecule is intrinsic factor, not B₁₂ itself; and sickle-cell disease is a single Glu→Val substitution at position 6 of β-globin that lets deoxygenated haemoglobin polymerise — which is why the crisis is precipitated by anything that promotes deoxygenation. The haemoglobin chemistry behind that sits in biochemistry note 02.

§ 06

Respiratory surfaces and the mechanics of ventilation

The ideaA gas exchange surface has to be thin, huge and wet, and the wetness is the catch — a wet surface loses water to air, which is why land animals tuck their lungs inside. But a tucked-in lung is a dead end, and a dead end can only be flushed in and out, never rinsed through. That single geometric fact separates the mammalian lung, which is a tidal bag whose air is never fully replaced, from the fish gill and the bird lung, both of which are through-flow systems. Almost every difference between them follows from it.

Definition & key facts

Ventilation
Bulk movement of the respiratory medium (air or water) past the exchange surface, so that the gradient driving diffusion is continually renewed. Without it, the medium in contact with the surface equilibrates and exchange stops. Ventilation is the "maintained gradient" leg of the triad from § 01.

Negative-pressure breathing
The mammalian method: the thoracic cavity is enlarged, intrapleural pressure falls, the lung is pulled open and air flows in. Water is a difficult medium, and gills are the response to that difficulty

Air-saturated fresh water holds about 6–7 mL of O₂ per litre; air holds about 210. Water is also a thousand times denser and fifty times more viscous, and the oxygen it does hold falls further as temperature or salinity rises. A fish therefore has to move a great deal of a heavy, oxygen-poor medium: ventilating the gills can consume 10–20 % of a fish's oxygen budget, against 1–2 % for a resting mammal. Two adaptations answer this. Flow is made unidirectional — water enters the mouth and leaves under the operculum, never reversing — which avoids the cost of accelerating a dense medium back and forth. And blood in the lamellae flows opposite to the water.

The bird lung is not counter-current, and this is examined

Textbooks and lecture slides routinely call the avian arrangement "counter-current", because unidirectional airflow feels like the same idea. It is not. The blood capillaries around the air capillaries run across the direction of airflow, not against it, and the correct name is cross-current. The distinction has a testable consequence: in a counter-current exchanger blood leaving can approach the partial pressure of the incoming medium, whereas in a cross-current exchanger it lands somewhere between the incoming and outgoing values — higher than expired gas, lower than inspired. Two things about the bird lung are exactly as advertised, and they are what make it superb at altitude: airflow through the parabronchi is genuinely one-way, and gas exchange continues during both inhalation and exhalation. The air sacs, note, do no gas exchange at all — they are bellows.

Go deeper — the avian breathing cycle, step by step, and why it takes two breaths

Follow one bolus of air. Inhalation 1: the sternum swings forward, all air sacs expand, and the bolus travels down the trachea and primary bronchus into the posterior air sacs. Exhalation 1: the posterior sacs compress and push that air forward through the parabronchi of the lung — this is where exchange happens. Inhalation 2: the sacs expand again; the now-partly-deoxygenated air is drawn out of the parabronchi into the anterior air sacs, while a fresh bolus enters the posterior sacs behind it. Exhalation 2: the anterior sacs empty out through the trachea, while the second bolus is pushed through the parabronchi. Two full cycles, four steps, for one packet of air — and because a fresh packet is always one step behind, air flows through the parabronchi in the same direction during every phase, so exchange never pauses.

Compare this with a mammal, where flow reverses twice per cycle and exchange is discontinuous, and where the incoming fresh air must mix with the 2.3 L already resident. The pay-off is measurable: bar-headed geese migrate over the Himalaya at altitudes where a mammal of the same size is unconscious, and a bird's arterial PO₂ at altitude exceeds the PO₂ of the air it exhales — an outcome that is geometrically impossible in a tidal lung.

One more comparison worth holding. Insects solve the same problem by abolishing the carrier entirely: spiracles admit air to a branching tracheal system whose finest tracheoles end directly on the cells, often fluid-filled at the tip. Because oxygen is delivered as a gas — where it diffuses about 10⁴ times faster than in water — the delivery distances can be long by cellular standards. The cost is that the system scales badly, which is one of several reasons insects are small, and that haemolymph carries little or no oxygen.

How a mammal moves air, and what can go wrong with it

Inspiration is active. The diaphragm contracts and flattens; the external intercostals swing the ribs up and out. Thoracic volume rises, so by Boyle's law alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric and air flows in. Quiet expiration is passive: the muscles relax and the elastic recoil of the lung and chest wall does the work. Forced expiration recruits the internal intercostals and the abdominal muscles, which is why a chest-wall injury impairs coughing more than breathing.

The lung does not attach to the chest wall; it is held against it by the pleural fluid, and intrapleural pressure is always subatmospheric — about −5 cmH₂O at rest and −8 at end-inspiration. Two opposing elastic forces create that suction: the lung always tries to collapse inward, the chest wall always tries to spring outward. Puncture the pleural space and the coupling is lost: the lung collapses to its own resting volume, the chest wall expands to its own, and that is a pneumothorax.

Surface tension inside the alveoli is the other force to reckon with, and the number is startling: it accounts for roughly two thirds of lung recoil, elastin and collagen for only the remaining third. By Laplace's law P = 2T/r, a small alveolus at the same surface tension would have a higher internal pressure than a large one and would empty into it. Surfactant prevents that, and it does so in an elegantly self-correcting way: as an alveolus shrinks, its surfactant molecules are concentrated on a smaller area and lower the tension further. Surfactant therefore raises compliance, reduces the work of breathing, keeps alveoli of different sizes stable together, and — by reducing the tendency of the alveolus to collapse — reduces the suction pulling fluid out of pulmonary capillaries. Premature infants born before about 28–32 weeks have not yet made enough of it, hence neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

Worked example 3 — deep and slow versus shallow and fast, at identical minute ventilation

Dead space is 150 mL. Compare three patterns, all with the same minute ventilation of 6000 mL·min⁻¹: (A) 500 mL × 12, (B) 250 mL × 24, (C) 1000 mL × 6. Which delivers the most fresh air to the alveoli? What would 150 mL × 40 achieve?

  2. Pattern A. V̇A = (500 − 150) × 12 = 350 × 12 = 4200 mL·min⁻¹.
  3. Pattern B. (250 − 150) × 24 = 100 × 24 = 2400 mL·min⁻¹.

Exam technique · any breathing-pattern question, in three lines

  1. The cue is a dead space in the stem — the moment a number like "dead space 150 mL" appears, the examiner is testing V̇A = (VT − VD) × f and nothing else. Subtract before you multiply. Multiplying first gives minute ventilation, which is the trap, not the answer.
  2. If the two patterns have equal minute ventilation, answer without computing — dead space is paid per breath, so the pattern with the larger tidal volume always wins. Deep and slow beats shallow and fast, every time, whenever V̇E is held constant. That recognition alone answers the question; compute only if the stem wants the size of the gap.
  3. The option that says "identical, because minute ventilation is identical" is the built-in trap — it is on the page precisely because it is a true sentence answering a question that was not asked. On this paper that pattern is common: an option can be textbook-correct in isolation and still wrong for the stem.
  4. Two free sanity checks — V̇A must always be smaller than V̇E, so any option equal to or above VT × f is dead on arrival. And the limiting case fixes the principle: at VT = VD, V̇A is exactly zero however fast the patient breathes.
  5. Lung volumes need no mnemonic — read the names — a capacity is a sum of volumes, and each name states its own definition: vital capacity is everything you can move (IRV + VT + ERV), total lung capacity adds the volume you cannot (VC + RV), functional residual capacity is what is left sitting there after a quiet breath, which is what you could still blow out plus what you could not (ERV + RV). The only fact not derivable from a name is that RV, and therefore TLC and FRC, cannot be measured by spirometry — and that is what gets asked.

Ventilation and perfusion must be matched, not merely present

Gas exchange needs air and blood at the same alveolus. An alveolus that is ventilated but not perfused is alveolar dead space (a pulmonary embolus); one that is perfused but not ventilated is a shunt (consolidated pneumonia, collapse). The two behave quite differently under treatment: raising inspired oxygen fixes a low-V/Q area handsomely and does almost nothing for a true shunt, because shunted blood never meets the enriched gas at all. The lung matches the two locally by a mechanism unique in the body — hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Systemic arterioles dilate when oxygen falls, to bring more blood to a struggling tissue; pulmonary arterioles constrict, diverting blood away from a poorly ventilated region toward one that is working. Getting the sign of that reversal right is a favourite discriminator.

§ 07

Oxygen transport and the haemoglobin dissociation curve

The ideaOxygen barely dissolves in water, so blood plasma alone could not supply a mouse. Haemoglobin fixes that by binding oxygen — but a carrier that binds tightly enough to fill at the lung would be too reluctant to let go at the tissue. The solution is a molecule that changes its own mind: binding one oxygen makes the next easier, so the carrier is nearly empty at low partial pressure and nearly full at high, with a very steep switch in between. Put the lung above the steep bit and the tissues inside it, and you have a molecule that fills completely and empties on demand.

Definition & key facts

Cooperative binding
Haemoglobin is a tetramer (α₂β₂), each subunit carrying one haem with one Fe²⁺. Oxygenation of one subunit shifts the whole molecule from the low-affinity T (tense) state toward the high-affinity R (relaxed) state, raising the affinity of the remaining sites. The result is a sigmoid curve — the visual signature of cooperativity. Monomeric myoglobin, with nothing to cooperate with, gives a hyperbola.
CaO₂ = 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂ ≈ 19.5 + 0.3 ≈ 20 mL·dL⁻¹ bound oxygen plus dissolved oxygen — and dissolved is only 1.5 % of it
Full definition, symbols and units
P50
The partial pressure at which the carrier is half saturated — the standard index of affinity, and the number every "shift" question is really about. Adult HbA ≈ 26–27 mmHg; fetal HbF ≈ 19 mmHg; myoglobin ≈ 2.8 mmHg. A higher P50 means lower affinity and a curve shifted right.
Bohr effect
A fall in pH (or a rise in PCO₂) lowers haemoglobin's oxygen affinity and shifts the curve right. Mechanism: H⁺ protonates specific residues — chiefly the C-terminal histidines of the β chains — stabilising salt bridges that hold the T state. CO₂ acts both indirectly (through carbonic acid) and directly, by forming carbamino groups on N-terminal amines that also stabilise T.
2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG)
A glycolytic side-product present in red cells at roughly equimolar concentration with haemoglobin tetramers (~5 mmol·L⁻¹). It binds in the central cavity between the two β chains, which is open only in the T state — so it locks T and shifts the curve right. It rises in chronic hypoxia, anaemia and at altitude, and falls in stored blood.

Why the curve is the shape it is, in three observations

The flat top is a safety margin. Above about 60 mmHg the curve is nearly horizontal, so saturation stays above 90 % even if alveolar PO₂ falls substantially — at moderate altitude, or in mild lung disease. It also means that giving supplemental oxygen to someone already at PaO₂ 100 adds almost nothing: the haemoglobin is full, and all you can add is dissolved gas, which is 1.5 % of the total.

The steep middle is where the work is done. Tissue PO₂ sits between roughly 20 and 40 mmHg, exactly on the steepest part. A small further fall in tissue PO₂ — a muscle starting to work — releases a large amount of oxygen with no change anywhere else in the system. The carrier is, in effect, self-regulating.

Myoglobin's curve tells you its job. With a P50 near 2.8 mmHg, myoglobin is still 94 % saturated at the venous PO₂ of 40 — so it does not hand oxygen over under ordinary conditions. It is a store and a facilitator of intracellular diffusion that unloads only when muscle PO₂ collapses during intense contraction. An option describing myoglobin as "transferring oxygen from haemoglobin to the mitochondria in normal conditions" is describing something that barely happens.

What shifts the curve right

CADET, face Right!

The standard mnemonic, in the form it circulates, and worth using for that reason alone. A cadet facing right is a curve shifted right — lower affinity, higher P50, more oxygen released at the tissue. C — ↑CO₂. A — ↑Acid, i.e. falling pH, the Bohr effect proper. D — 2,3-DPG (the older name for 2,3-BPG), raised in chronic hypoxia, anaemia and at altitude. E — Exercise. T — ↑Temperature. Every item is a signature of a tissue that needs oxygen now, which is why they all point the same way and why you can rebuild the list from that idea if the word deserts you. Three cautions. (1) E is not an independent mechanism — exercise is C, A and T arriving together, so do not offer it as a separate cause in an explanation. (2) It encodes only the right-shift set. Most of the left-shift set is simply these reversed (alkalosis, hypothermia, stored blood in which 2,3-BPG has fallen), but the two that are not in CADET at all are the two most examined: carbon monoxide and HbF. (3) Direction of benefit: a right shift is nearly free, because the flat top means arterial saturation barely moves (97.5 % → 93 %) while venous saturation drops hard (75 % → 50 %).

The three P50 values, and the ordering they imply

Mum 27, bump 19, muscle 3

Ours rather than a standard hook, but the rhyme holds three values and the route oxygen takes between them. Mum 27 — maternal adult HbA, P50 ≈ 26–27 mmHg. Bump 19 — fetal HbF, ≈ 19. Muscle 3 — myoglobin, ≈ 2.8, rounded up only to keep the chant. Then remember the one rule that turns the numbers into answers: higher P50 = lower affinity = curve further right. The three numbers fall as you go along the chain, so affinity rises along it — each station pulls harder than the one before, which is why oxygen moves mum → bump → muscle and never back. Affinity therefore runs myoglobin ≫ HbF > HbA, and at the placenta the fetal curve lies left of the mother's, which is the whole of placental oxygen transfer in one line. The numbers do not carry the mechanism, and the mechanism is what the exam callout below is about: HbF is left-shifted in the red cell not because its intrinsic affinity is higher but because the γ chain binds 2,3-BPG weakly. Stripped of BPG the two haemoglobins are similar, so an option saying "HbF has a higher intrinsic affinity for oxygen" is the plausible wrong answer. Myoglobin's 2.8 also carries a trap: it is so far left that myoglobin is still ~94 % saturated at a venous PO₂ of 40, so it is a store, not a courier.

Exam technique · every oxygen question is one of three questions — decide which first

  1. Sort the stem in five seconds — anaemia, haemorrhage, carbon monoxide, polycythaemia, transfusion ⇒ this is a content question, use CaO₂ = 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂.   2. Content questions are almost pure proportionality — the dissolved term is only about 1.5 % of a normal content, so estimate 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ first and add ≈0.3 at the end.   3. The free elimination: neither monitor measures content — a pulse oximeter reports the percentage of the haemoglobin present that is saturated, and PaO₂ reports dissolved gas. Any option that detects anaemia or CO poisoning from a saturation or a PaO₂ is dead without arithmetic — and that single rule answers a whole family of stems.
  4. Shift questions: work the venous point, not the arterial one — read the curve at PO₂ 40 and ask what saturation does. A right shift takes venous saturation from about 75 % to about 50 % — twice the oxygen delivered — while arterial saturation slips only from 97.5 % to 93 %. If an option claims a right shift meaningfully impairs loading at the lung, the flat top of the curve refutes it.

Fetal haemoglobin, and the mechanism candidates usually miss

HbF (α₂γ₂) has a P50 near 19 mmHg against the mother's 27, so at the placenta — where the fetal circulation faces maternal blood at a PO₂ around 40 mmHg — fetal haemoglobin binds oxygen that maternal haemoglobin is releasing. The usual explanation, "HbF has a higher intrinsic affinity for oxygen", is not the main mechanism. Stripped of 2,3-BPG, HbA and HbF have very similar affinities. The difference is that the γ chain carries serine where the β chain carries histidine at position 143, removing two of the positive charges that grip 2,3-BPG in the central cavity — so HbF binds BPG weakly and is therefore less right-shifted in the red cell. The Bohr effect helps too, and in the right direction: fetal blood gives up CO₂ to the mother, so fetal pH rises and its curve shifts further left, while maternal pH falls and hers shifts right. Both changes move oxygen the same way. This is called the double Bohr effect, and it is a good example of a mechanism that only makes sense once you stop treating a shift as a fact to memorise.

§ 08

Carbon dioxide transport, the chloride shift, and the control of breathing

The ideaCarbon dioxide has an easier journey than oxygen because it can turn into something else. Most of it is converted inside the red cell into bicarbonate, an ion so soluble that plasma can carry it in bulk, and the conversion is undone at the lung. Two consequences follow and both are heavily examined: an anion has to leave the red cell, so a different anion must come in to replace it; and the reaction generates acid, which means breathing is the body's fastest acid–base control. That is why the drive to breathe is built around CO₂ and not around oxygen.

Definition & key facts

Chloride shift (Hamburger shift)
The exchange of HCO₃⁻ leaving the red cell for Cl⁻ entering it, through the anion exchanger band 3 (AE1). It happens in the systemic capillaries and runs in reverse at the lung. It exists for one reason: to preserve electroneutrality while exporting the bicarbonate the cell has just manufactured.

Haldane effect
Deoxygenated haemoglobin carries more CO₂ than oxygenated haemoglobin, for two reasons: it is a better proton buffer (so it soaks up the H⁺ released by the carbonic anhydrase reaction, pulling the equilibrium toward more bicarbonate) and it forms carbamino compounds more readily. The Bohr and Haldane effects are the same allosteric coupling read in opposite directions.

Neurons on the ventral surface of the medulla, bathed in CSF. They respond to H⁺, not to CO₂ directly — but CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier freely while H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ do not, so a rise in arterial PCO₂ acidifies CSF sharply and they respond briskly. They account for roughly 70–80 % of the ventilatory response to CO₂.

Carotid bodies (afferents in the glossopharyngeal nerve, IX) and aortic bodies (vagus, X). They sense PaO₂, PaCO₂ and pH, and they are the only receptors that sense hypoxaemia. Their oxygen response is negligible until PaO₂ falls below about 60 mmHg.

Following one CO₂ molecule from a working muscle to the alveolus

CO₂ diffuses out of the cell into plasma and most of it keeps going, into a red cell. Plasma contains essentially no carbonic anhydrase, so the hydration reaction there is far too slow to matter; inside the red cell the enzyme makes it almost instantaneous. The carbonic acid dissociates. The H⁺ is mopped up by haemoglobin — and here is where the two effects lock together, because haemoglobin that has just released its oxygen is a better buffer, so the very act of unloading oxygen makes the cell better at loading CO₂. The HCO₃⁻ is exported through band 3 in exchange for Cl⁻ entering. Because the red cell has gained an osmotically active chloride ion (and, through it, water), venous red cells are slightly swollen and venous haematocrit is marginally higher than arterial — a detail that is a good marker of whether someone has actually followed the mechanism.

At the lung, every arrow reverses. Oxygen binds haemoglobin, which promptly becomes a worse proton buffer and releases H⁺; the H⁺ combines with the HCO₃⁻ flowing back into the cell (Cl⁻ leaving in exchange); carbonic anhydrase runs the dehydration; CO₂ diffuses out into the alveolus. Nothing new has to be learned for the lung end — it is the same four steps taken backwards, driven by oxygen binding instead of oxygen release.

Four sentences about CO₂ carriage that look correct and are not

"CO₂ binds to the iron of haem, at a site different from oxygen." No. Carbamino CO₂ binds the N-terminal α-amino groups of the globin chains. Haem iron binds O₂, CO, NO and cyanide — never CO₂.

"Carbonic anhydrase in the plasma converts CO₂ to bicarbonate." The enzyme is inside the erythrocyte (and in renal tubule cells, gastric parietal cells and elsewhere), not free in plasma. That localisation is the reason the chloride shift has to exist at all — if the reaction happened in plasma, nothing would need to cross a membrane.

"In the tissues, chloride leaves the red cell." Backwards. In the tissues bicarbonate leaves and chloride enters. Fix the direction by asking what the cell is trying to get rid of: it has just manufactured bicarbonate, so bicarbonate is the export.

"Most CO₂ is carried bound to haemoglobin." Only about 23 % is, and about 70 % travels as bicarbonate — most of it dissolved in plasma, having been made in the red cell. Both halves of that sentence are usually needed for the mark.

(1) Where the bicarbonate is: made inside the red cell, where the carbonic anhydrase is, then carried mostly in plasma — which is the entire reason a chloride shift must exist. (2) The direction of that shift, which you should never memorise: ask what the cell has just manufactured and wants rid of. It made bicarbonate, so in the tissues bicarbonate goes out and chloride comes in; at the lung every arrow reverses. Control of breathing: why you cannot hold your breath to death

The rhythm originates in the medulla — the pre-Bötzinger complex of the ventral respiratory group is the pacemaker, and the dorsal respiratory group drives inspiration; pontine centres smooth the transition between phases. Onto that rhythm the chemoreceptors write a set-point, and the set-point is essentially PaCO₂. Arterial CO₂ is normally held within a few mmHg of 40, and a rise of just 2–3 mmHg roughly doubles ventilation over the linear range. Arterial oxygen, by contrast, has to fall below about 60 mmHg before it stimulates breathing at all — and that threshold is not arbitrary: it is the shoulder of the dissociation curve, the point below which saturation (and therefore content) really does start to collapse. Regulating oxygen at any higher threshold would be regulating a variable that is not yet changing.

This is why a breath-hold ends the way it does. The urge to breathe is driven by rising CO₂ and falling pH, and it becomes irresistible long before oxygen becomes dangerous — a protective ordering. It is also why hyperventilating before a dive is hazardous: blowing off CO₂ removes the alarm without adding meaningful oxygen (haemoglobin was already 97 % full), so a swimmer can reach hypoxic unconsciousness before feeling any urge to surface. Every year people drown this way.

Metabolic acidosis: watch which direction the compensation runs

Read Henderson–Hasselbalch as a ratio, and every acid–base question becomes a one-line argument. In diabetic ketoacidosis the numerator [HCO₃⁻] is consumed by ketoacids, the ratio falls and pH falls. The peripheral chemoreceptors detect the arterial H⁺ directly and ventilation rises, driving PaCO₂ down — the denominator falls too, and the ratio is partly restored. That is Kussmaul breathing: deep and fast, and it is compensation, not distress. Note the ordering that examiners probe: respiratory compensation for a metabolic problem begins within minutes because it only requires a change in ventilation, whereas renal compensation for a respiratory problem — retaining bicarbonate — takes 2–5 days because it requires a change in transporter expression. Fast lungs, slow kidneys. The buffering chemistry itself is worked through in biochemistry note 01.

§ 09

Digestion: the canal region by region, and the hormones that time it

The ideaA gut is a tube through your body, and its contents are technically still outside you until something crosses an epithelium. The design problem is that food arrives as polymers too large to cross anything, so the tube has to run a controlled demolition — but the same enzymes that dismantle food would dismantle the tube. Almost every feature of the gut is a solution to one of those two problems: how to break polymers down in stages, and how to avoid digesting yourself while doing it.

Definition & key facts

The four processes
Ingestion → digestion → absorption → elimination. Digestion is both mechanical (chewing, churning, emulsification) and chemical (hydrolysis). Mechanical processing does not break chemical bonds — it increases surface area so that the enzymes can work faster, which is the same Fick argument as § 01.

Region by region, asking at each stage what has changed

Mouth. Mechanical breakdown by teeth; saliva supplies mucin for lubrication, lysozyme for defence, bicarbonate as a buffer, and salivary amylase, which begins hydrolysing starch to maltose, maltotriose and α-limit dextrins. Its optimum is near pH 7, so it is inactivated in the stomach — though it keeps working for a while inside the bolus before acid penetrates. Note what saliva does not contain: no protease, and no meaningful lipid digestion beyond a modest lingual lipase.

Stomach. Three secretory cell types do the work, and it is worth knowing which is which because the exam asks. Parietal (oxyntic) cells secrete HCl, using an H⁺/K⁺-ATPase — the proton pump that omeprazole blocks — and also secrete intrinsic factor, which is the only truly indispensable gastric product, because without it vitamin B₁₂ cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum. Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, which acid converts to pepsin, and pepsin then activates more pepsinogen autocatalytically. G cells in the antrum secrete gastrin. The acid itself denatures proteins (exposing bonds for pepsin), kills most ingested microbes and solubilises dietary iron and calcium. Very little is absorbed here — ethanol, aspirin, and some water. A person can survive gastrectomy with B₁₂ injections, which tells you how much of gastric digestion is convenience rather than necessity.

Small intestine. This is where digestion is finished and essentially all absorption occurs. Acidic chyme entering the duodenum triggers secretin release, and pancreatic bicarbonate neutralises it — not as housekeeping but because the pancreatic enzymes have an alkaline optimum (trypsin near pH 8) and the brush-border enzymes a near-neutral one, and none of them work at gastric pH. Pancreatic juice delivers amylase, lipase (with colipase), nucleases and a set of protease zymogens; bile delivers bile salts and phospholipids. Brush-border enzymes finish the job at the membrane.

Large intestine. No digestive enzymes of its own. It absorbs water and electrolytes, houses the microbiota — which ferment undigested fibre to short-chain fatty acids that the colonocytes themselves use as fuel, and synthesise vitamin K and several B vitamins — and forms and stores faeces.

The trypsinogen chain, and why it is the answer to two different questions

The pancreas secretes every protease as a zymogen, so none of them can start work until something activates the first one — and that something is deliberately placed outside the pancreas. Enteropeptidase (enterokinase) is a brush-border enzyme of the duodenum; it converts trypsinogen to trypsin, and trypsin then activates chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidase, prophospholipase — and more trypsinogen. One localised trigger, one amplifying cascade, all of it downstream of the gland. When that containment fails and trypsin is activated prematurely inside the pancreas, the organ digests itself: acute pancreatitis. Two exam points fall out. First, the enzyme that starts the cascade is intestinal, not pancreatic. Second, an option saying "pepsin activates trypsinogen" is wrong — pepsin is long since destroyed by the alkaline duodenum.

Absorption: four different mechanisms for four kinds of molecule

Carbohydrate. Only monosaccharides cross. Glucose and galactose enter the enterocyte apically on SGLT1, a Na⁺-coupled symporter — this is secondary active transport, powered not by ATP directly but by the sodium gradient that the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase maintains. Fructose enters by facilitated diffusion on GLUT5. All three leave basolaterally on GLUT2. The clinical corollary is the finest piece of applied physiology in this section: because SGLT1 needs both sodium and glucose, oral rehydration solution must contain both salt and sugar — glucose alone or salt alone is far less effective, and the water follows the absorbed solute osmotically.

Protein. Amino acids are absorbed by several Na⁺-coupled carriers with different side-chain specificities. But the textbook line that "proteins are absorbed as amino acids" is incomplete: di- and tripeptides are absorbed intact and rather efficiently on PepT1, an H⁺-coupled transporter, and are hydrolysed inside the cell. Peptide uptake is quantitatively more important than free-amino-acid uptake for several residues.

Fat. This is the one route with a different destination, and it is examined most years. Lipase products — 2-monoacylglycerol and free fatty acids — are carried through the unstirred water layer to the brush border inside micelles of bile salts, and diffuse across the membrane. Inside the enterocyte they are re-esterified to triacylglycerol in the smooth ER, packaged with cholesterol, phospholipid and apolipoprotein B-48 into a chylomicron, and exported by exocytosis into the lacteal — a lymphatic, not a blood capillary. Chylomicrons therefore travel up the thoracic duct and enter the blood at the left subclavian vein, bypassing the hepatic portal vein and the liver's first pass entirely. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids are the exception: water-soluble enough to enter the portal blood directly, which is why medium-chain triglycerides are used clinically when lymphatic transport or bile is compromised. Note also that the bile salts themselves are not absorbed with the fat — they are reclaimed further along, in the terminal ileum, and returned to the liver in the enterohepatic circulation, roughly 95 % of them being recycled several times per meal.

Water and ions. Water absorption is entirely passive and osmotic — it follows absorbed solute, principally sodium. There is no water pump. Iron is absorbed in the duodenum (DMT1 in, ferroportin out, both under the control of hepcidin), calcium under the control of calcitriol, and vitamin B₁₂ only in the terminal ileum and only as the intrinsic-factor complex.

Three localisations that carry a disease each

Terminal ileum absorbs vitamin B₁₂ and reclaims bile salts — so ileal resection or Crohn's disease produces both B₁₂ deficiency and fat malabsorption, and the two arrive together. Duodenum and proximal jejunum absorb iron, calcium and folate — so coeliac disease, which flattens villi from the proximal end, presents with iron-deficiency anaemia. Brush border lactase declines after weaning in most of the world's adults; undigested lactose is osmotically active and is then fermented by colonic bacteria, giving the characteristic combination of osmotic diarrhoea plus gas. Note the logic: the symptom follows from the molecule's fate, not from a memorised list.

Worked example 5 — the gut's fluid budget, and why cholera kills

An adult ingests about 2 L of fluid daily. Salivary, gastric, biliary, pancreatic and intestinal secretions add about 7 L. Faecal water is about 0.1 L. (a) How much is absorbed, and where? (b) Cholera toxin locks intestinal crypt cells into maximal Cl⁻ secretion. If small-intestinal absorption falls by a third while secretion rises by 3 L, what reaches the colon and what is the outcome? (c) Why does oral rehydration solution still work?

  1. (a) Total presented. 2 + 7 = 9 L·day⁻¹ enters the small intestine. It absorbs about 7.5 L, so 9 − 7.5 = 1.5 L passes the ileocaecal valve. The colon absorbs about 1.4 L, leaving 0.1 L in faeces — over 98 % of the load reclaimed.
  2. (b) In cholera. Secretion becomes 7 + 3 = 10 L, so 2 + 10 = 12 L is presented; small-intestinal absorption falls to 7.5 × ⅔ = 5.0 L. Delivered to the colon: 12 − 5.0 = 7.0 L. The colon's maximum absorptive capacity is only around 4–5 L·day⁻¹, so at least 2–3 L·day⁻¹ is lost as stool — and in severe cases far more. Against a plasma volume of about 3 L and an extracellular volume of 14 L, losses on that scale cause hypovolaemic shock within hours. Cholera does not poison anyone; it dehydrates them.
  3. (c) Why ORS works. The toxin disables Cl⁻-driven secretion control but leaves SGLT1 completely intact. Give glucose and sodium together and the cotransporter still pulls both across, water follows osmotically, and net absorption is restored even while secretion continues. Salt alone or sugar alone does far less — the transporter needs both. A therapy costing almost nothing, derived directly from knowing the mechanism of one membrane protein, and estimated to have saved tens of millions of lives.

Cellulose, and the honest version of "herbivores digest it"

No vertebrate makes cellulase. Herbivores do not digest cellulose; their symbiotic microbes do, and the animal absorbs the volatile fatty acids the microbes produce. Foregut fermenters (ruminants: rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum) ferment before the true stomach, so they also digest the microbes themselves and recover their protein — efficient, but slow. Hindgut fermenters (horses, rabbits) ferment in an enlarged caecum after the absorptive small intestine, which is why rabbits practise coprophagy: eating the first, soft faeces is the only way to pass those nutrients through the absorptive surface at all. Note also the general correlation the exam likes — herbivores have long guts relative to body length, carnivores short ones, because plant material is difficult and meat is already close to the composition of the consumer.

§ 10

Osmoregulation and nitrogenous waste: the water bill decides everything

The ideaTwo problems that look separate are really one. An animal has to keep the water and salt content of its body fluids within narrow limits, and it has to get rid of the nitrogen left over when it burns protein — and every way of disposing of nitrogen costs water, energy, or both. Which nitrogen molecule an animal excretes is therefore not a taxonomic quirk. It is a direct read-out of how much water the animal can afford to throw away, and you can predict it from habitat almost every time.

Definition & key facts

Osmoconformer vs osmoregulator
An osmoconformer keeps its internal osmolarity equal to its surroundings — cheap, but it confines the animal to a stable environment, and almost all are marine invertebrates. An osmoregulator holds an internal osmolarity different from its surroundings, which costs continuous energy but permits life in fresh water, on land, and across a salinity gradient.

Deriving the nitrogen choice instead of memorising it

Ask one question: how much water can this animal afford to spend on excretion? A bony fish in a lake is surrounded by an infinite dilute sink, so it can afford to dump the cheapest, most toxic molecule straight out across its gills — ammonia, and no synthetic cost at all. A mammal must carry its own water, so it converts nitrogen to urea, which is safe enough to concentrate in a bladder, paying 4 ~P per molecule for the privilege. A bird in flight must carry water and minimise mass, and — decisively — its embryo develops inside a sealed shell with no way to void anything, so waste must be rendered harmless and stored dry: uric acid, precipitated as crystals in the allantois. The shelled egg is the single strongest predictor of uricotelism, which is why birds, most reptiles and insects converge on it despite their distance from each other.

The clean test of that logic is the animals that change. A tadpole lives in water and is ammonotelic; at metamorphosis the frog switches on the urea cycle and becomes ureotelic. Nothing about its taxonomy changed — only its water budget. Similarly some fish, when air-exposed or crowded in stagnant water, upregulate urea synthesis. And the human clinical mirror: liver failure removes the ability to make urea, blood ammonia rises, and the result is hepatic encephalopathy. The whole pathway sits in biochemistry note 08; here the point is only that ammonia detoxification is a hepatic function and the kidney merely excretes the product.

The comparative osmoregulation question, solved in one step

Ask only: is the animal hyper- or hypo-osmotic to its surroundings? Water moves toward the more concentrated side, so that answer fixes the direction of the problem, and the responses follow mechanically.
Freshwater fish (≈300 inside, ≈5 outside) — hyperosmotic, so water floods in and salts leak out. Response: never drink, produce copious dilute urine, actively take up salt across the gills.
Marine bony fish (≈350 inside, ≈1000 outside) — hypoosmotic, so water is drawn out. Response: drink seawater constantly, excrete the swallowed salt through gill chloride cells and the gut, and produce a small volume of urine roughly isosmotic to the blood.
Sharks and rays — retain urea and TMAO until they are very slightly hyperosmotic to seawater. So water flows gently in, they do not need to drink, they excrete the surplus as urine, and they dump excess NaCl through a dedicated rectal gland. This is the option people get wrong: a shark is not fighting dehydration.
Marine birds and reptiles — drink seawater and excrete concentrated NaCl from salt glands near the eye or nostril, because their kidneys cannot make urine saltier than the sea. Note the pattern: whenever a kidney is not up to the job, a supplementary salt-exporting epithelium appears somewhere else.

"Marine fish drink and freshwater fish don't" — make sure you can say why

It is stated as a fact so often that candidates reverse it under pressure. Anchor it to the osmotic gradient rather than the habitat: a marine teleost is losing water to the sea across every permeable surface, so it drinks to replace what it loses and then faces the separate problem of exporting the salt that came with the drink. A freshwater fish is gaining water and would burst if it drank; its kidney is essentially a bilge pump. And note the corollary that is asked just as often: the glomerulus is large and numerous in freshwater fish and reduced or even absent in some marine species, because filtration capacity tracks how much urine the animal needs to make. Structure following the water bill again.

The excretory organs, in one sweep

Protonephridia (flatworms) are blind-ended tubules capped by ciliated flame cells that drive fluid outward; they are chiefly osmoregulatory, since ammonia diffuses away across the body wall anyway. Metanephridia (annelids) open at both ends: a ciliated funnel collects coelomic fluid from one segment and the tubule reabsorbs valuable solutes before discharging through a nephridiopore — the first appearance of the filter-then-reclaim design. Malpighian tubules (insects) invert the design because an open circulation offers no filtration pressure: they secrete first and reclaim later. Vertebrate kidneys return to filtration, and take it further than anything else, which is the subject of the next section.

§ 11

The nephron: filter everything, then take back what you want

The ideaThe kidney's strategy looks wasteful and is actually brilliant. Rather than identifying and removing each unwanted substance, it dumps a huge volume of protein-free plasma into a tubule and then reclaims, selectively and in stages, everything worth keeping. Anything the body has no transporter for is simply left behind and excreted — including molecules the animal has never encountered before. That is why a filter-and-reclaim kidney copes with novel drugs and toxins that no dedicated pump could have anticipated.

Definition & key facts

The three renal processes
Filtration at the glomerulus (bulk, non-selective, driven by hydrostatic pressure); reabsorption from tubule back to blood (selective, mostly active, the great majority of the work); secretion from blood into tubule (for H⁺, K⁺, organic acids and bases, and many drugs). Excretion = filtration − reabsorption + secretion.

Filtration: three pressures, and why the kidney can defend its own flow

Glomerular filtration obeys the same Starling arithmetic as any capillary, with one difference: the numbers are set up so that filtration never reverses. Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure is high — about 55 mmHg rather than the 35 of a systemic capillary — because the glomerulus sits between two arterioles rather than between an arteriole and a venule. Against it push Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure (~15 mmHg) and glomerular oncotic pressure (~30 mmHg), giving a net filtration pressure near 55 − 15 − 30 = +10 mmHg — modest, but positive along the whole length.

Because the efferent arteriole lies downstream of the glomerulus, the kidney has two independent dials. Constricting the afferent arteriole reduces both flow and glomerular pressure, so GFR falls. Constricting the efferent arteriole reduces flow but raises glomerular pressure, so GFR is maintained or even rises while renal plasma flow falls — and filtration fraction therefore rises. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, which is exactly how the kidney preserves filtration when perfusion pressure drops. It also explains a clinical fact worth carrying: ACE inhibitors dilate the efferent arteriole, so in a patient whose GFR is being propped up by angiotensin II — bilateral renal artery stenosis — they can precipitate acute renal failure.

On top of this sits autoregulation. Over a mean arterial pressure range of roughly 80–180 mmHg, renal blood flow and GFR are held nearly constant by two mechanisms: a myogenic response (a stretched afferent arteriole constricts) and tubuloglomerular feedback, in which the macula densa of the thick ascending limb senses the NaCl concentration of fluid arriving in its own distal tubule and, if it is high — implying GFR is too high — signals the afferent arteriole to constrict. It is a negative feedback loop wired within a single nephron.

Where ADH acts, and what it actually does

ADH (vasopressin) does not act on the loop of Henle, and it does not "pump water". It is released from the posterior pituitary — synthesised in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus and transported down axons, so the posterior pituitary stores rather than makes it — in response to a rise in plasma osmolality of as little as 1 %, or to a large fall in blood volume. It binds V₂ receptors on collecting duct principal cells, raises cAMP, and causes vesicles carrying aquaporin-2 to fuse with the apical membrane. That is all it does: it opens a door. The force that then drives water out of the duct is the medullary osmotic gradient built by the loop. Hence the two forms of diabetes insipidus — central (no ADH made) and nephrogenic (ADH present, receptor or AQP2 unresponsive) — and hence the fact that a patient given a loop diuretic cannot concentrate urine no matter how much ADH is circulating: the gradient has been destroyed, so opening the door achieves nothing.

Worked example 6 — renal arithmetic: filtered load, clearance, and the appearance of glycosuria

Take GFR 125 mL·min⁻¹, RPF 625 mL·min⁻¹, Tm for glucose 375 mg·min⁻¹. (a) Find the filtration fraction and the daily filtered volume. (b) At a normal plasma glucose of 100 mg·dL⁻¹, what is the filtered load, and how much appears in urine? (c) In uncontrolled diabetes the plasma glucose is 400 mg·dL⁻¹. Now how much is excreted, and what does that do to urine volume? (d) A substance has urine concentration 60 mg·mL⁻¹, urine flow 1 mL·min⁻¹ and plasma concentration 0.5 mg·mL⁻¹. Is it being secreted or reabsorbed?

  1. (a) Filtration fraction. FF = 125/625 = 0.20 — a fifth of the plasma is filtered on each pass. Daily volume: 125 × 1440 = 180 000 mL = 180 L·day⁻¹. Against a urine output of 1.5 L that is (180 − 1.5)/180 = 99.2 % reabsorbed.
  2. (b) Normal glucose. Convert first: 100 mg·dL⁻¹ = 1 mg·mL⁻¹. Filtered load = GFR × P = 125 × 1 = 125 mg·min⁻¹ — comfortably under the Tm of 375, so reabsorption is complete and none appears in urine. Over a day that is 180 g of glucose filtered and 180 g recovered.
  3. (c) Diabetes. 400 mg·dL⁻¹ = 4 mg·mL⁻¹; filtered load = 125 × 4 = 500 mg·min⁻¹. The carriers saturate at 375, so 500 − 375 = 125 mg·min⁻¹ is excreted — about 180 g·day⁻¹ of glucose lost. And because that glucose stays in the tubule it holds water with it: an osmotic diuresis. This is the mechanism behind the two classic presenting symptoms — polyuria, then polydipsia as the patient tries to keep up. Note the ordering: the thirst is a consequence of the water loss, not an independent symptom.
  4. (d) Clearance. C = U·V/P = (60 × 1)/0.5 = 120 mL·min⁻¹. That is just below GFR of 125, so the substance is filtered and then very slightly reabsorbed — net. The rule is worth stating cleanly: C < GFR ⇒ net reabsorption; C = GFR ⇒ neither (inulin); C > GFR ⇒ net secretion (PAH, at about 625 mL·min⁻¹, approaches total clearance of the plasma reaching the kidney). Creatinine sits just above GFR because a little is secreted — which is why creatinine clearance mildly overestimates true GFR.

Exam technique · renal arithmetic — convert the units, then answer the right question

  1. Convert before you do anything else — plasma concentrations arrive in mg·dL⁻¹ and GFR arrives in mL·min⁻¹. Divide the concentration by 100 to get mg·mL⁻¹ and the two now multiply cleanly. Nearly every wrong answer on this material is a factor of 100, not a misunderstanding.
  2. Filtered load, then the transport maximum — filtered = GFR × P; excreted = filtered − Tm, and if that is negative the answer is zero. At P = 400 mg·dL⁻¹: 125 × 4 = 500, minus 375, gives 125 mg·min⁻¹.
  3. The option set is always the same three decoys — the filtered load (500), the reabsorbed amount (Tm itself, 375), and "none, because reabsorption is active". The third is beatable on principle alone: reabsorption is carrier-mediated, and carriers saturate. Recognising the trio kills three of five options before you compute, which is the whole game under negative marking.
  4. Clearance questions: compute C, then compare it with 125 — C = U·V/P, then C < GFR ⇒ net reabsorption; C = GFR ⇒ neither, this is inulin; C > GFR ⇒ net secretion. The two anchors to carry are inulin at 125 (so C = GFR) and PAH at ≈625 (so C = RPF). Creatinine sits just above 125 because a little is secreted, which is why creatinine clearance mildly overestimates GFR — a favourite one-line question.
  5. Sanity-check on scale — GFR 125 mL·min⁻¹ is 180 L·day⁻¹, and urine is about 1.5 L, so 99 % of filtrate comes back. Any answer implying you excrete more than a few per cent of what you filter is wrong. Similarly FF = 125/625 = 0.20: a filtration fraction above ~0.25 or below ~0.15 in a normal stem means you have swapped RPF for renal blood flow (1100 mL·min⁻¹), which is the standard slip.
  6. Read whether the stem wants a rate or a daily total — 125 mg·min⁻¹ and 180 g·day⁻¹ are the same answer (125 × 1440 = 180 000 mg), and both will appear. Same trap as "filtered" versus "excreted": check which noun the question used before you commit.
Go deeper — urea recycling, the vasa recta, and how the medulla stays salty without washing out

Two problems remain once you accept that the thick ascending limb builds the gradient. First, NaCl alone does not account for all 1200 mOsm at the papilla — roughly half of the inner medullary gradient is urea. Here is the circuit. Most of the tubule is impermeable to urea, so as water is reabsorbed elsewhere the urea in the tubular fluid becomes steadily more concentrated. In the inner medullary collecting duct, and only there, ADH-stimulated urea transporters (UT-A1, UT-A3) let that concentrated urea move into the interstitium, where it contributes osmotically. Some of it then re-enters the thin limbs of the loop and goes round again — urea recycling. The elegant consequence is that a well-fed animal, producing plenty of urea, can concentrate its urine better than a protein-starved one, which is why severe malnutrition impairs urinary concentration.

Second: why does the blood supply not simply wash the gradient away? Any ordinary capillary bed perfusing a hyperosmotic tissue would equilibrate with it and carry the solute off. The medulla is instead supplied by the vasa recta, long hairpin vessels running parallel to the loops. As blood descends, it loses water and gains solute; as it ascends through progressively less concentrated interstitium, it gives the solute back and takes the water up. Net solute removal is therefore minimal. Note the term carefully, because it is a favourite distractor: the vasa recta is a counter-current exchanger — entirely passive, it preserves a gradient it did not make. The loop of Henle is the counter-current multiplier — it spends ATP and creates the gradient. Multiplier makes, exchanger keeps. Blood flow through the vasa recta is also kept deliberately low — medullary perfusion is only a few per cent of renal blood flow — for the same reason.

Put the pieces together and the ceiling on urine concentration is explained by one anatomical variable: relative loop length. Humans, with 15 % of nephrons juxtamedullary and a modest medulla, reach about 1200 mOsm·kg⁻¹. A beaver, never short of water, manages about 500. A kangaroo rat, with exceptionally long loops and a thick renal papilla, reaches around 6000 mOsm·kg⁻¹ and can live on metabolic water alone, never drinking. Structure predicting physiology, and the reverse — if a question tells you an animal has an unusually long loop of Henle, it is telling you about its habitat.

ADH and aldosterone are answers to different questions

Candidates conflate them because both cause sodium and water retention. Separate them by asking what is being defended. ADH defends osmolality. Its trigger is a rise in plasma osmolality sensed by hypothalamic osmoreceptors; its action is to reabsorb water without solute, so it dilutes the plasma back down. Aldosterone defends volume and pressure. Its triggers are angiotensin II (via renin, released by juxtaglomerular cells when perfusion pressure falls, when the macula densa reports low NaCl delivery, or under β₁ sympathetic drive) and, independently, a rise in plasma K⁺; its action is to reabsorb Na⁺ through ENaC while secreting K⁺ and H⁺ — so water follows the sodium and volume is restored, with osmolality roughly unchanged. Hence the pattern to recognise: hyperaldosteronism gives hypertension with hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis, while Addison's disease gives hypotension with hyperkalaemia and acidosis. The counter-regulator is ANP, released by atrial myocytes when the atria are stretched by a high volume: it raises GFR, inhibits Na⁺ reabsorption, and suppresses renin, aldosterone and ADH. One hormone system says "hold on", the other says "let go", and the atrium is where volume is actually measured.

The one-line test for every nephron question

Ask "is this segment permeable to water?" first, and the rest follows. Proximal tubule: yes, so everything stays isosmotic. Descending limb: yes, so contents concentrate. Ascending limb: no, so contents dilute and the interstitium concentrates. Collecting duct: only if ADH says so — which is the whole of urine concentration in one clause. Then keep the two counter-current devices apart: the loop of Henle is the ATP-spending multiplier that creates the medullary gradient, while the vasa recta is a passive exchanger that only preserves it. Multiplier makes, exchanger keeps.
