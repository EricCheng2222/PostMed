Biology · Note 05

Evolution and Population Genetics

This block is about 4.5 % of the general-biology half — two questions in a typical year — and it is wildly out of proportion as a source of the paper's beyond-textbook items, because it is the one topic where a sentence can be fluent, familiar and completely wrong. One idea organises all of it: evolution is a statement about populations across generations, and nothing else. An individual does not evolve. Nothing is trying to become anything. There is no need, no goal and no ladder. What there is, is a pool of alleles whose proportions are pushed around by exactly five processes — and one of those five, natural selection, is the only one that reliably builds things that work. Hardy–Weinberg is how you write down what the pool looks like when none of the five is acting, which is why it is worth so much: it is the ruler against which every real process is measured.

§ 01

What natural selection actually says

The ideaA population is not a thing that wants anything. It is a collection of individuals who differ from one another, some of whom happen to leave more offspring than others. If the reason they leave more offspring is something their children can inherit, then next year's collection will look slightly different from this year's — not because anyone tried, but because the arithmetic of who had children forces it. That is the whole of natural selection. Almost every wrong answer on this topic comes from quietly smuggling intention back in.

Definition & key facts

Evolution
A change in the frequency of alleles in a population from one generation to the next. That is the operational definition, and it is the one the arithmetic on this page uses. Note what it excludes: an individual changing during its lifetime is not evolution, and a population that stays put genetically is not evolving no matter how much its members are struggling.
variation + heritability + differential reproduction → adaptation all three are necessary; none of them alone is sufficient
Full definition, symbols and units
Natural selection, stated as four conditions
(1) Overproduction — more offspring are produced than the environment can support. (2) Variation — individuals within a population differ. (3) Heritability — at least part of that variation is passed to offspring. (4) Non-random survival and reproduction — the variation correlates with reproductive success. Given all four, allele frequencies must change. Selection is a consequence, not an agent.
Fitness (W)
The expected number of offspring a genotype contributes to the next generation, in a stated environment. Relative fitness w divides by the best genotype's value, so the fittest genotype has w = 1 and the selection coefficient is s = 1 − w. Fitness is not strength, health, size or longevity: a long-lived, powerful, sterile animal has fitness zero.
Adaptation
A heritable trait whose present form is explained by past natural selection for its current function. Distinguish sharply from acclimatisation (a reversible physiological adjustment within one individual — a highland trek raising your haematocrit is not evolution).

Where the marks are actually lost

The exam does not usually ask you to state Darwin's postulates. It gives you a sentence — often lifted more or less verbatim from a textbook paragraph, which is what makes it dangerous — and asks whether it is correct. So the useful skill is not recall but diagnosis: what kind of error is this sentence making? There are only about six kinds, and once you can name them the distractors stop working.

Common incorrect statements about natural selection, the error each makes, and the correct version
The sentence you will meetWhat is wrong with itThe precise version
"Giraffes evolved long necks in order to reach high leaves." Teleology. Puts the outcome before the cause. Nothing was aiming at anything. Giraffes with alleles for longer necks left more offspring; those alleles rose in frequency. The function explains the spread, never the origin, of the variant.
"Individuals evolve to become better adapted." Wrong level. Individuals develop, acclimatise, learn and die with the genotype they were born with. Populations evolve. The unit of selection is the individual (or the gene); the unit that evolves is the population.
"Exposure to antibiotic induces resistance mutations." Directed mutation. Mutation is blind to what would be useful. Resistant mutants pre-exist exposure; the drug only reveals them. The Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test (1943) and Lederberg replica plating (1952) were designed precisely to prove this.
"Organisms develop traits because they need them." Lamarckism. Need does not generate variation, and use does not modify gametes. Selection can only edit variation that already exists. A population needing a trait it has no alleles for simply goes extinct — which is the usual outcome.
"Natural selection produces perfectly adapted organisms." Ignores constraints. Four constraints, all examinable: selection works on available variation; history limits the starting point (the vertebrate retina is wired backwards); every trait trades off against others; and drift and a changing environment interfere continuously.
"Traits evolve for the good of the species." Naive group selection. An allele that sacrifices its bearer's reproduction for the species is out-competed by one that does not. Apparent altruism is explained by inclusive fitness — Hamilton's rule, r·b > c — or by reciprocity, not by species-level benefit.
"Evolution is progress from simple to complex." Ladder thinking. There is no direction. Tapeworms lost their gut; cave fish lost their eyes; the most successful lineages on Earth by every measure are prokaryotes. Loss is as common as gain.
"Natural selection is the mechanism of evolution." Too strong. It is one of several. Selection, drift, mutation, gene flow and non-random mating all change a population. Selection is the only one that consistently produces adaptation — that is the precise claim.

Why adaptation is never perfect — the four constraints

Selection can only CHAT — Chance · History · Available variation · Trade-offs

Chance — drift and a changing environment interfere continuously, so the target moves while selection is still aiming at it. History — ancestry fixes the starting point, which is why the vertebrate retina is wired backwards. Available variation — selection can only edit variation that already exists; a population needing an allele it does not have simply goes extinct. Trade-offs — every trait is paid for out of another, so nothing is optimised on its own. My own hook, not a circulating one. Use it the instant an option contains perfect, optimal, ideal or invariably: you now have four separate reasons to strike it and can name one in three seconds. The four letters are a set, not a sequence — nothing turns on chance coming first. It does not cover the developmental and physical limits some textbooks add as a fifth constraint. And note where a hook is not wanted: Darwin's four conditions — overproduction, variation, heritability, non-random reproduction — are just the idea paragraph above, told in order. If more are born than can live, and they differ, and the difference is inherited, and it correlates with who breeds, then allele frequencies must move. Rebuild that one sentence and all four fall out in the right order, with nothing to forget.

Exam technique · diagnosing a "which statement is correct" sentence

  1. Find the word "evolve" and look at its subject — if it is an individual, an organism, a giraffe or a bacterium, the option is dead on the spot. Only populations evolve. This single check disposes of an option on most stems in this section.
  2. Scan for purpose words — in order to, so that it could, in response to the need for, for the purpose of, wanted, tries to, learned to. Any one of them is teleology, and the option is wrong however textbook-fluent it sounds. Function explains the spread of a variant, never its origin.
  3. Check which way causation runs on mutation — if the environment, the drug or the need induces, causes, triggers or directs the useful mutation, strike it. The legitimate verbs are reveals and selects: Luria–Delbrück and replica plating showed the mutants are already there before the agent arrives.
  4. "For the good of the species / the colony / the population" — wrong every time it is offered as a mechanism. The replacement is inclusive fitness (r·b > c) or reciprocity.
  5. Give absolutes one extra beat, because exactly one "only" on this topic is true. "Selection is the only process that changes allele frequencies" is false — four others do. "Selection is the only process that consistently produces adaptation" is true. Read which of those two claims the option is actually making before you strike it; the pair is a standard trap and the wrong half is written to sound like praise for selection.
  6. "Advantageous" with no environment named — fitness is a property of a genotype in a stated environment, so an option grading an allele in the abstract is either incomplete or is the thing being tested. Same for "fittest" used to mean strongest, largest or longest-lived: fitness is counted in descendants.
  7. Budget ten to twenty seconds, not sixty. This whole family is recognition, and the six filters above usually leave one option standing. Even when they leave two, you have eliminated three of five — which under −0.25 / −0.5 marking makes answering strictly better than a blank. Never leave one of these blank, and bank the saved time for § 04 and § 06.

"Survival of the fittest" is the phrase that costs the most marks

Two problems with it. First, survival is only the means: an organism that survives a hundred years and reproduces once is less fit than one that dies at two having raised twenty young. Fitness is counted in descendants, not in years. Second, fitness is not a property of an organism — it is a property of a genotype in a particular environment. The sickle-cell heterozygote is the fittest genotype in a malarial region and an ordinary one elsewhere. A stem that describes an allele as "advantageous" without naming an environment is either incomplete or is testing whether you noticed. The phrase is also close to circular as usually stated ("the fittest are those that survive"); it becomes non-circular only when fitness is predicted from a measured trait and then checked against realised reproduction, which is exactly what the Grants did on Daphne Major.

Selection acts on phenotypes; only heredity converts that into evolution

This distinction is worth a paragraph of its own because it explains an entire family of exam answers. A predator eats the phenotype it can catch — it has no access to genotypes. So selection is always phenotypic. But the phenotype has two components, genetic and environmental, and only the genetic component is transmitted. That is the content of the breeder's equation R = h²S: if you select the top individuals so that the selected parents exceed the population mean by S, the offspring generation moves by h²S, where h² is the narrow-sense heritability. If h² = 0 — the variation is entirely environmental — then no matter how ferociously you select, R = 0 and nothing evolves. Body size in a population of well-fed and starved animals may vary enormously and be perfectly non-heritable.

Two consequences the paper likes. (1) Sustained artificial selection eventually plateaus, because the additive genetic variance for the trait is used up; further progress waits on new mutation. (2) A trait can be highly heritable within each of two populations and the difference between the populations still be entirely environmental. Heritability is a within-population statistic and says nothing about between-population differences.

Directly observed evolution — the four cases worth being able to cite

Antibiotic and pesticide resistance — the fastest and clinically the most important; also the clean demonstration that mutation precedes selection. Peppered moth (Biston betularia): the melanic carbonaria morph rose from under 2 % in 1848 Manchester to over 95 % by 1895 and collapsed again after the Clean Air Acts; the causal mutation has since been identified as a transposable-element insertion in the cortex gene, dated to about 1819 — i.e. the variant existed before the soot. Darwin's finches: after the 1977 drought on Daphne Major left only large hard seeds, mean beak depth in the surviving Geospiza fortis and their offspring rose by roughly 4 %, and the 1983 El Niño reversed it — selection is not a ratchet. HIV: drug resistance evolves inside a single patient within weeks, which is why combination therapy exists.

§ 02

The evidence for common descent

The ideaNobody disputes that living things change. The interesting claim is stronger: that all of them go back to one starting point. The evidence for that is a particular kind of clue. Not that two animals are similar — similarity can happen for all sorts of reasons, including that the same job has the same best answer. What counts is that they share the same odd, arbitrary, pointless details. Two people can invent a wheel independently. Two people do not make the same typing mistake in the same word by accident.

Definition & key facts

Common descent
The hypothesis that any two living species share an ancestor, and that the similarities and differences between them were produced by descent with modification from that ancestor. Its central testable prediction is a nested hierarchy: characters should sort organisms into groups within groups, and independent characters should agree on the same grouping.
shared arbitrary detail > shared useful detail function can be reinvented; an error cannot be reinvented identically
Full definition, symbols and units
Homologous structure
A structure shared by two species because it was present in their common ancestor. Recognised by correspondence of parts and position, and by shared embryological origin — never by similarity of function. The tetrapod forelimb is the standard case: one humerus, two forearm bones, a block of carpals, five digit rays, in a whale flipper, a bat wing, a horse leg and your arm.
Vestigial structure
A structure that is reduced and has lost its ancestral function. Vestigial does not mean useless. The human coccyx anchors pelvic-floor muscles; the appendix carries gut-associated lymphoid tissue. What makes them vestigial is that they no longer do what the homologous structure does in relatives — a tail, a caecal fermentation chamber.
Nested hierarchy
The pattern in which every group is wholly contained within a larger group and never straddles two. All birds are amniotes; all amniotes are tetrapods. Convergence produces exceptions for single characters; it does not produce a consistent alternative hierarchy across thousands of independent characters, which is why congruence between independent data sets is the strongest single argument.

Three textbook sentences about this evidence that are wrong or incomplete

"Ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny." Haeckel's slogan, and false as stated: a human embryo does not pass through a fish stage, an amphibian stage and a reptile stage. What is true is the much weaker and more interesting claim that embryos of related species resemble each other's embryos more than the adults do, because early development is strongly constrained. If a stem offers you recapitulation as the correct account of embryological evidence, it is wrong. "Vestigial organs are functionless." Vestigial refers to loss of the ancestral function; a vestigial structure may be co-opted for something else, and the appendix and coccyx both were. "Analogous structures are evidence of common ancestry." They are evidence of common selective pressure and of nothing else. This inversion appears as a distractor almost every time homology is examined.

Go deeper · shared errors: pseudogenes, retroviruses and human chromosome 2

A shared working feature has a rival explanation — both lineages need it. A shared broken feature, at the same position, broken in the same way, does not. Three cases, in ascending order of how hard they are to explain any other way.

The GULO pseudogene. Most mammals synthesise their own vitamin C, using L-gulonolactone oxidase. Haplorrhine primates — including us — cannot, and that is why scurvy exists. The gene is still in our genome, inactivated by the same exon deletions found in every other haplorrhine, at the same positions. Guinea pigs also lost the gene, but by a different set of lesions: an independent loss, exactly as expected if the shared pattern in primates comes from a shared ancestor.

Endogenous retroviruses. Roughly 8 % of the human genome is retroviral wreckage. A retrovirus integrates essentially at random among millions of possible sites; if it lands in a germ-line cell the insertion is inherited. Humans and chimpanzees share thousands of ERV insertions at the identical nucleotide position, flanked by the identical target-site duplications. Independent insertion at the same base, thousands of times over, is not an available explanation.

Human chromosome 2. Humans have 2n = 46; chimpanzee, gorilla and orangutan all have 2n = 48. Common descent therefore requires that two ancestral chromosomes fused in our lineage — and predicts what the product should look like. Human chromosome 2 carries an internal block of telomeric TTAGGG repeats in head-to-head orientation at 2q13, exactly where two chromosome ends would have met, plus a second, inactive centromere at 2q21. Its two arms match great-ape chromosomes 2A and 2B along their whole length. This is a prediction that could have failed and did not.

The general form of the argument matters more than any one case, because it is the reasoning the exam tests: evidence discriminates between hypotheses only when the hypotheses predict different things. Functional similarity is predicted by everything. Shared arbitrary damage is predicted by descent alone.

§ 03

Hardy–Weinberg: a null model, not a law

The ideaSuppose absolutely nothing is happening to a population — nobody dies young for a reason connected to their genes, nobody moves in or out, everyone pairs off at random, and there are far too many individuals for luck to matter. What would the mix of genotypes look like next generation? Hardy and Weinberg worked that out, and the answer is deliberately boring. The point is not the answer. The point is that once you know exactly what "nothing happening" looks like, any departure from it is a measurement of something happening.

Definition & key facts

Hardy–Weinberg principle
In a population meeting five conditions, allele frequencies do not change from generation to generation, and genotype frequencies settle at p² : 2pq : q² after a single generation of random mating. It is not a claim about real populations; it is the baseline against which real populations are compared.
(p + q)² = p² + 2pq + q² = 1 p² = AA · 2pq = Aa · q² = aa, with p + q = 1
Full definition, symbols and units
The derivation, in one line
If mating is random, gametes fuse at random. The chance of drawing an A egg is p and an A sperm is p, so AA zygotes are p²; heterozygotes can be made two ways (A egg × a sperm, or the reverse), hence the factor of 2. The binomial expansion is the biology.
Three alleles
The same expansion generalises: with p, q, r summing to 1, genotype frequencies are p² + q² + r² + 2pq + 2pr + 2qr. This is how ABO blood groups are handled — the A and B alleles are codominant to each other and dominant to O.
Allele frequency from genotype counts
Count alleles, not people. Each homozygote contributes two copies and each heterozygote one, over a total of 2N copies.

The five assumptions — and § 05's five forces are the same five

Dr MSG breaks equilibrium — Drift · non-Random mating · Mutation · Selection · Gene flow

Read the five letters as forces and you have § 05: Drift, non-Random mating, Mutation, Selection, Gene flow. Negate each one — a population large enough that drift is nil, mating at random, no mutation, no selection, no gene flow — and you have this section's five assumptions; the same letter R carries the mating condition either way round. It is one list, learnt once, doing two jobs. Three riders settle most of the conceptual questions on their own: only Mutation makes new alleles; only Drift has no direction; and only non-Random mating leaves p and q exactly where they were. The five-item list itself is the standard one you will meet everywhere; the five-letter compression is mine. The order carries no meaning — this is a set, not a sequence — so read nothing into drift coming first. What the hook cannot encode is the magnitudes: mutation is on the list as a force, but at 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁶ per locus per generation it is a rounding error, which is exactly why "mutation drives evolution" is a wrong answer (§ 05).

Four precise points that separate a good answer from a plausible one

(1) One generation. Whatever the starting genotype frequencies, one round of random mating at an autosomal locus puts the population into p² : 2pq : q². Start with 50 % AA and 50 % aa and no heterozygotes at all, and the very next generation is 25 : 50 : 25. Stems that say "after many generations of random mating" are testing this. (2) X-linked loci are the exception — because males carry one copy, the male and female allele frequencies converge only gradually, the difference between them halving and changing sign each generation. (3) "Random mating" means random with respect to this locus. A population can mate strongly assortatively for height and still be in perfect Hardy–Weinberg proportions at a blood-group locus. (4) Being in Hardy–Weinberg proportions is weak evidence of anything. One generation of random mating restores the proportions no matter what selection did last generation, so a locus under selection can test perfectly at the zygote stage. Fit to Hardy–Weinberg does not demonstrate that a population is not evolving.

Exam technique · "what changed?" — alleles or genotypes

  1. Classify the violation before you read a single option. Inbreeding, consanguinity, assortative mating, "individuals prefer to mate with their own type", or a sample that turns out to be two populations pooled (Wahlund) — every one of those is the mating assumption and none of the other four.
  2. Then apply the one-line rule and delete. Non-random mating rearranges alleles into genotypes; the other four consume or import alleles. So on a non-random-mating stem, every option asserting that p and q have changed is gone — typically two or three of the five, before any thinking at all.
  3. Reading it backwards from data. Both homozygote classes in excess and heterozygotes deficient, symmetrically, is the signature of non-random mating or of pooled subpopulations; selection normally distorts one class, not both evenly. Quantify with F = 1 − Hobs/Hexp, and for scale remember that the offspring of first cousins sit at F = 0.0625, so an F of 0.24 is very large.
  4. Two impossibility filters that cost no arithmetic. 2pq ≤ 0.5 always, so an option offering more than 50 % heterozygotes "at equilibrium" is dead on its face. And p² + 2pq + q² must sum to 1 — add the option's own three numbers.
  5. The χ² default. Two alleles, three genotype classes: df = 3 − 1 (the total is fixed) − 1 (you estimated p from these same data) = 1, and the 5 % critical value is 3.84. Reaching for df = 2 out of habit is the standard slip. You never have to memorise the general case either — the same subtraction gives df = k(k−1)/2, so three alleles give 3.
  6. Two stock red herrings. "After many generations of random mating" is planted to see whether you know that one generation is enough at an autosomal locus; the genuine exception is an X-linked locus, which converges over several. And "the population fits Hardy–Weinberg, therefore it is not evolving" is always wrong, because one round of random mating restores the proportions whatever selection did last generation.
§ 04

Working the numbers: carriers, incidence, X-linked loci

The ideaThis is the part the exam actually makes you do. You are told how many people have a disease and asked how many carry it silently; or you are told how many boys are affected and asked about girls. Both are one square root and one multiplication. The only thing that goes wrong is losing track of which quantity the question handed you — the frequency of a genotype or the frequency of an allele. Nearly every mark lost here is that confusion, and it costs nothing to write the two on separate lines.

Definition & key facts

Carrier frequency from disease incidence
For an autosomal recessive disease in a population assumed to be at equilibrium, the incidence at birth is q². So q is its square root, p = 1 − q, and the carrier frequency is 2pq. Because p ≈ 1 for a rare allele, 2pq ≈ 2q: the carrier frequency is about twice the square root of the incidence.
carriers ≈ 2incidence 1 in 10 000 → q = 0.01 → carriers ≈ 2 % · 1 in 2500 → q = 0.02 → carriers ≈ 4 %
Full definition, symbols and units
The carrier-to-affected ratio
2pq / q² = 2p/q ≈ 2/q. This grows without limit as the disease gets rarer, and it is the number that makes carrier screening worth doing. At q = 0.02 there are 98 carriers for every affected individual; at q = 0.01, 198.
Where the recessive alleles actually are
The proportion of all a alleles sitting in heterozygotes is 2pq / (2pq + 2q²) = p — exactly p. When q = 0.01, 99 % of the disease alleles in the population are in healthy carriers, which is why selection against affected homozygotes is so nearly useless (§ 05).
X-linked loci
Males are hemizygous, so the frequency of affected males is the allele frequency itself, q. Females need two copies: affected q², carriers 2pq ≈ 2q. Hence two rules worth memorising: affected males exceed affected females by a factor of 1/q, and female carriers outnumber affected males by a factor of about 2.

Worked example 1 — cystic fibrosis: carriers, ratios and a couple's risk

Cystic fibrosis affects about 1 in 2500 newborns of northern European ancestry. Assume Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. (a) What is the frequency of the CF allele? (b) What fraction of the population are carriers? (c) How many carriers per affected person? (d) What fraction of all CF alleles are in carriers rather than patients? (e) A phenotypically normal woman whose brother has CF marries a man from the general population. What is the chance their first child is affected?

  1. (a) The incidence is q², so q² = 1/2500 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ and q = 0.02, giving p = 0.98. Write both lines out; answering "1/2500" when asked for q is the commonest single error in this topic.
  2. (b) 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.0392 — 3.92 %, or 1 in 25.5. This is where the clinical rule of thumb "about 1 in 25 northern Europeans carries a CF mutation" comes from. The shortcut 2q = 0.04 is within 2 %, which is fine under time pressure.
  3. (c) 2pq / q² = 2p/q = 2 × 0.98 / 0.02 = 98 carriers per patient.
  4. (d) Exactly p = 0.98. Ninety-eight per cent of CF alleles are carried by people who will never have a symptom — which is why carrier screening, not treating patients, is what changes population incidence.
  5. (e) Two independent steps. Her brother is aa, so both parents are Aa and her own prior possibilities were 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. Observing that she is unaffected removes the aa branch, leaving P(carrier) = 2/3. Her husband is a random member of the population, so P(carrier) = 0.0392.
  6. If both are carriers, one quarter of their children are affected: (2/3) × 0.0392 × 0.25 = 6.53 × 10⁻³ — about 1 in 153. Against a population risk of 1 in 2500 that is a sixteen-fold increase from one affected relative. Dropping the 2/3 and using 1/2 gives 1 in 204, which is exactly the sort of near-miss a distractor is built from.

Worked example 2 — X-linked loci: haemophilia and colour blindness

(a) Haemophilia A (X-linked recessive) affects about 1 in 5000 males. What proportion of females are affected, and what proportion are carriers? (b) Red–green colour blindness affects 8 % of males in a population; find the same two quantities. (c) Why is the male incidence the quantity you start from?

  1. (a) Males have one X, so the frequency of affected males equals the allele frequency: q = 1/5000 = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴. No square root at this step — that is the whole point of hemizygosity.
  2. Affected females: q² = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸, about 1 in 25 million. Carrier females: 2pq = 2 × 0.9998 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴, about 1 in 2500 — twice the male incidence, as always.
  3. (b) q = 0.08, p = 0.92. Affected females q² = 0.0064 = 0.64 %; carrier females 2pq = 2 × 0.92 × 0.08 = 0.1472 = 14.7 %. So roughly one woman in seven carries the allele while fewer than one in 150 is affected, and affected males outnumber affected females 0.08 : 0.0064 = 12.5 : 1.
  4. (c) Because for an X-linked locus the male phenotype is a direct readout of the allele frequency, with no dominance in the way. Starting from the female incidence would also work (√0.0064 = 0.08) but you are almost never given it. Taking a square root of the male incidence is the classic error: it would give √0.08 = 0.283, a wrong answer that is generously supplied as an option.
  5. Sanity check on the scheme as a whole: females carry two-thirds of the X chromosomes in a population and males one-third, so the overall allele frequency is (2/3)q♀ + (1/3)q♂. At equilibrium these are equal and it collapses to q. Out of equilibrium they are not, which is why an X-linked locus needs several generations to settle (§ 03).

X-linked recessive: where the square root goes, and where it must not

Boys read it · girls square it · carriers double it

Boys read it — males are hemizygous, so the frequency of affected males is q: read the male figure straight off the stem and never take its square root, which is the error the paper rewards most reliably. Girls square it — females need two copies, so affected females are q², i.e. square the q you just read (and conversely, the female incidence is the one you would square-root to recover q). Carriers double it — carriers are 2pq ≈ 2q, about twice the male incidence, always. My own hook. It earns its place because the wrong root is generously supplied as an option: with 8 % of males colour-blind, √0.08 = 0.283 and 2pq would come out at 41 % — and "about 28 % affected, about 41 % carriers" is exactly the distractor on the list. The right answers are 0.64 % of females affected and 14.7 % carriers. Scope: X-linked recessive only. For an autosomal recessive the affected frequency is q² in both sexes and you do take the root; for a dominant condition the logic inverts again, and the callout at the end of this section says how.

Worked example 3 — testing a population: MN blood groups, χ² and F

The MN blood group is codominant, so all three genotypes are distinguishable. A sample of 1000 people gives MM 540 · MN 320 · NN 140. (a) Estimate the allele frequencies. (b) Are the genotype frequencies consistent with Hardy–Weinberg? (c) If not, what is the most likely explanation, and how large is the effect?

  1. (a) Count alleles, not people. There are 2 × 1000 = 2000 copies. p(M) = (2 × 540 + 320)/2000 = 1400/2000 = 0.70, so q(N) = 0.30. No equilibrium assumption was needed — allele counting is always valid, which is why codominant markers are preferred for this kind of test.
  2. (b) Expected numbers under equilibrium: p²N = 0.49 × 1000 = 490, 2pqN = 0.42 × 1000 = 420, q²N = 0.09 × 1000 = 90.
  3. χ² = (540−490)²/490 + (320−420)²/420 + (140−90)²/90 = 5.10 + 23.81 + 27.78 = 56.7.
  4. Degrees of freedom: three classes, minus one for the fixed total, minus one for the allele frequency estimated from these same data, leaves df = 1. The 5 % critical value is 3.84, so 56.7 is a decisive rejection. Using df = 2 out of habit is a standard slip — you spent a degree of freedom estimating p.
  5. (c) Both homozygote classes are in excess and heterozygotes deficient. That symmetrical pattern is diagnostic: selection would usually distort one class. It points to non-random mating (inbreeding or assortative mating), or to the sample being a mixture of two differentiated subpopulations — the Wahlund effect. Quantify with the fixation index: F = 1 − Hobs/Hexp = 1 − 0.320/0.420 = 0.238.
  6. An F of 0.24 is a large heterozygote deficit; for comparison the offspring of first cousins have F = 0.0625. Note what has not happened: the allele frequencies are still 0.70 and 0.30 and would stay there indefinitely. Non-random mating rearranges alleles into genotypes; it does not consume them.
p = 0.70, q = 0.30 · χ² = 56.7 on 1 df — reject · F = 0.238, heterozygote deficit

When you may take the square root — and when you may not

The step q = √(frequency of affected) assumes Hardy–Weinberg, and is therefore illegitimate in precisely the situations that make a question interesting. It fails if the population is inbred (homozygotes are in excess, so the square root overestimates q), if the sample mixes populations, or if selection against the homozygote is strong within a generation. It also fails silently for a dominant condition: there the affected class is p² + 2pq = 1 − q², so you take the square root of the frequency of the unaffected and get p by subtraction. Reaching for a square root the moment a disease frequency appears is the most reliable way to be caught. And if all three genotypes are distinguishable — codominance, or any molecular assay — count alleles instead and skip the assumption entirely.

Exam technique · the Hardy–Weinberg numbers, in the order that stops you slipping a line

  1. Before anything else, write two labelled lines — "allele: p, q" and "genotype: p², 2pq, q²" — and put the number the stem handed you on the correct one. Almost every mark lost in this section is an answer that is one line out, and the two lines cost four seconds.
  2. Branch on the locus, not on the disease name. Autosomal recessive → the incidence you were given is q², so q = √incidence. X-linked, and the figure is a male frequency → that figure is q, no root. Autosomal dominant disease → the affected class is 1 − q², so root the frequency of the unaffected to get the normal allele and subtract to get the disease allele. All three genotypes distinguishable (codominance, or any molecular assay) → count alleles, p = (2nAA + nAa)/2N, and assume nothing at all.
  3. Then the fast route through the arithmetic. For an incidence of 1 in D, q = 1/√D and carriers ≈ 2/√D — that is, roughly 1 in (√D)/2. So 1 in 10 000 → 1 in 50; 1 in 2500 → 1 in 25; 1 in 40 000 → 1 in 100. One square root and one halving, and it runs high by under 2 % because it drops the factor p.
  4. Fence the answer before computing, and delete. For any q < 0.5 the carrier frequency lies strictly between q and 2q. So an option equal to the incidence you were handed, and an option equal to q itself, are both dead with no arithmetic — and those two are precisely what the standard version of this question is built from.
  5. One-line cross-check. Carriers : affected must come out at 2p/q ≈ 2/q — 198 : 1 at q = 0.01, 98 : 1 at q = 0.02. If your carrier figure does not reproduce that ratio against the incidence in the stem, the slip is yours and you have found it in five seconds instead of losing the mark.
  6. If a relative is mentioned, a conditional-probability step has been bolted on. An unaffected sibling of an affected child is a carrier with probability 2/3, not 1/2, because observing that she is unaffected removes the aa branch of 1 : 2 : 1. Using 1/2 gives 1 in 204 where the answer is 1 in 153, and both numbers will be on the list.
  7. Spend the full 66 seconds here. This is the most reliably examinable arithmetic in the note and the working is genuinely short; the time comes from the ten-second recognition items in §§ 01–02.
§ 05

The five forces that break equilibrium

The ideaFive things can move a population away from the do-nothing baseline, and they are nothing like equal partners. One creates the raw material but by itself barely moves the numbers. One mixes populations together and makes them more alike. One is pure luck, and gets stronger the fewer individuals there are. One rearranges who pairs with whom without touching the underlying pool. And only one of the five reliably builds things that work. Knowing which is which answers most of the conceptual questions here without any calculation at all.

Definition & key facts

The five processes
Mutation — the only source of new alleles. Gene flow (migration) — movement of alleles between populations. Genetic drift — random change from finite sampling. Non-random mating — assortative mating and inbreeding. Natural selection — differential reproduction correlated with genotype. Four of the five change allele frequencies; non-random mating does not.
mutation makes it · flow spreads it · drift scatters it · selection sorts it and non-random mating only reshuffles it into genotypes
Full definition, symbols and units
Directional versus non-directional
Mutation, migration and selection are directional: repeat the experiment and the allele moves the same way. Drift is non-directional: repeat it and the allele is as likely to go the other way. That difference is why the two behave so differently over long runs, and it is the reasoning behind the effectively-neutral criterion in § 06.
Mutation rate
Per-locus rates are about 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁵ per gamete per generation for a typical gene; the human per-base rate is about 1.2 × 10⁻⁸ per generation, which over 2 × 3.1 × 10⁹ bases gives roughly 70 new point mutations per zygote. Enormous as a genome-wide source of variation, negligible as a force on one locus.
Selection against a recessive genotype
Fitnesses 1 : 1 : 1−s for AA : Aa : aa. The change per generation contains a factor of q², so it collapses to almost nothing once the allele is rare — selection can only act on what is expressed.

Why "mutation drives evolution" is a wrong answer

Take a locus with µ = 10⁻⁶ and no back mutation. Starting from p = 1, the new allele reaches a frequency of about µt after t generations, so getting to even 1 % takes ten thousand generations with nothing else acting. As a force, mutation is a rounding error. What it is, indispensably, is the source: no mutation, no variation, nothing for the other four to work on. The correct sentence is that mutation supplies the variation while selection and drift change the frequencies. A stem in which mutation "drives" a rapid change is testing whether you have the orders of magnitude.

The one place mutation genuinely sets the answer is mutation–selection balance, where a deleterious allele is recreated as fast as selection removes it. For a fully recessive lethal (s = 1) the equilibrium is q̂ = √(µ/s): with µ = 10⁻⁶ that is q̂ = 10⁻³, and a disease incidence of 10⁻⁶. For a dominant lethal, selection sees every copy and the balance is q̂ = µ/s — a thousand times lower. That square root is the entire reason serious recessive disease is common and dominant lethal disease is not; where a dominant severe disorder does persist, as in achondroplasia, it is because the mutation rate at that site is unusually high and fitness is only partly reduced.

Worked example 4 — how slowly can you purge a recessive allele?

A recessive allele is lethal before reproduction, so waa = 0 and s = 1. Its current frequency is q₀ = 0.10. (a) Derive the recursion. (b) How many generations to reach q = 0.01? (c) And from 0.01 to 0.001? (d) What does this say about eliminating a recessive disease by preventing affected people from reproducing?

  1. (a) After selection the aa individuals are gone, so the survivors amount to 1 − q² of the original, and every remaining a allele is in a heterozygote — a total of pq. Hence q′ = pq/(1 − q²) = q(1−q)/[(1−q)(1+q)] = q/(1 + q).
  2. Take reciprocals: 1/q′ = (1 + q)/q = 1/q + 1. The reciprocal of the allele frequency rises by exactly one per generation, giving the exact solution 1/qt = 1/q₀ + t. Worth remembering — it turns an ugly recursion into arithmetic you can do in your head.
  3. (b) t = 1/0.01 − 1/0.10 = 100 − 10 = 90 generations. At 25 years per human generation, about 2250 years to take a disease from 1 % of births to 0.01 %.
  4. (c) t = 1000 − 100 = 900 generations — ten times as long for the same tenfold reduction, and it gets ten times worse again at each further decade.
  5. (d) It is essentially futile, and the arithmetic says exactly why: at q = 0.01 the fraction of a alleles hidden in unaffected carriers is p = 0.99. Selection can only see the 1 % that is exposed. This is the population-genetic refutation of eugenic sterilisation programmes, and it is much more persuasive stated as a number than as an opinion.
  6. Contrast a dominant lethal, where both AA and Aa have w = 0: the allele vanishes in one generation apart from new mutations. What sets the speed is dominance, not the strength of selection.
q′ = q/(1+q) · 1/qt = 1/q₀ + t · 0.10→0.01 in 90 generations · 0.01→0.001 in 900
Go deeper · the arithmetic of gene flow, and the one-migrant-per-generation rule

Under one-way migration at rate m, the recipient's deviation from the source shrinks geometrically: qt − qsource = (q₀ − qsource)(1 − m)t. With m = 0.01, half the difference is gone in about 69 generations. Gene flow is a far stronger homogenising force than mutation is a diversifying one, which is why populations connected even weakly tend not to diverge.

The tidiest result in the area is the balance between drift pushing populations apart and migration pulling them together. In the island model at equilibrium, FST ≈ 1/(4Nem + 1). What matters is Nem — the absolute number of migrants per generation, not the fraction. One migrant per generation gives FST = 0.20; ten migrants give 0.024. A single individual moving between populations each generation is enough to stop them differentiating much, regardless of how large the populations are. That is the "one migrant per generation" rule used in conservation genetics, and it explains why species with any long-distance dispersal show shallow geographic structure.

Two cautions the paper can exploit. First, gene flow increases variation within a population and decreases it between populations; an option reading simply "gene flow increases genetic variation" is incomplete, and is wrong outright if the question is about differentiation. Second, gene flow can be maladaptive: immigrants carrying alleles suited to somewhere else can swamp local adaptation in a small peripheral population — migration load. That is one reason allopatric speciation needs the barrier in the first place, and it is the mechanism behind the idea of a species' range limit being set by gene flow from the centre.

§ 06

Genetic drift, bottlenecks and founders

The ideaToss a coin ten times and you will often not get five heads. Passing alleles to the next generation is the same kind of sampling, and when the group is small the sample is small, so the proportions wobble about for no reason at all. Wobble long enough and a variant hits nought or a hundred per cent — and then the wobbling stops for good. Nothing in this process cares whether the variant was any use, which is why small populations lose their variety and can even get worse at surviving.

Definition & key facts

Genetic drift（遺傳漂變）
Change in allele frequency caused by random sampling of gametes in a finite population. It has no expected direction — next generation's expected frequency equals this one's — but it has a variance, pq/2Ne, and the variance is what does the damage.
P(a neutral allele is eventually fixed) = its current frequency so a brand-new mutation, present in one copy, fixes with probability 1/2Ne
Full definition, symbols and units
Effective population size Ne
The size of an idealised population — equal sex ratio, random mating, Poisson family sizes, constant size — that would drift as fast as the real one. It is almost always smaller than the census size, often by an order of magnitude, and it is Ne, never N, that belongs in every formula on this page.
Bottleneck effect（瓶頸效應）
A population crashes; the survivors are a small random sample, so allele frequencies jump and rare alleles are lost outright. Recovering in numbers does not restore the variation — that waits on new mutation, and heterozygosity climbs back toward its mutation–drift equilibrium only over a timescale of order 2Ne generations, against the handful of generations needed to recover in numbers.
Founder effect（建立者效應）
A few individuals colonise a new area, and their allele frequencies, not the source population's, become the new population's. Mechanistically identical to a bottleneck; the difference is demographic — a new population is created rather than an old one reduced — and examiners do expect the two words used correctly.

Why Ne is nearly always much smaller than N

Every drift formula takes the effective size, and the gap between it and the head count is where the real biology lives. Four things shrink it: an unequal breeding sex ratio, variance in family size, fluctuation in size over time, and non-random mating. The first and third get examined, because they are calculable.

Worked example 5 — effective size, a bottleneck, and how much variation is lost

A captive herd holds 100 adults, but a harem structure means only 10 males and 90 females breed. (a) Find Ne. (c) Back to the herd: starting from a heterozygosity of 0.60, how much is left after 10 generations at the Ne of part (a)? Bottleneck or founder effect — the only thing that differs is the demography

The bottleneck stays · the founders stray

Stays — a bottleneck happens where the population already is: an existing population crashes where it stands, and the survivors are a small random sample of it. Stray — in a founder effect a few individuals stray off and a new population is born elsewhere, carrying their allele frequencies rather than the source population's. My own hook, and it deliberately encodes only the demography, because that is the only thing that differs: the genetic mechanism is identical (random sampling, i.e. drift), both lose heterozygosity, both lose rare alleles, and neither gets its variation back when numbers recover — that waits on new mutation. Examiners do expect the two words used correctly, which is the whole reason to carry the hook. What it does not do is separate either of them from inbreeding, which is a different mechanism (non-random mating), leaves allele frequencies untouched, and is reversed at once if random mating resumes.

The founder-effect calculation examiners like

Ellis–van Creveld syndrome — autosomal recessive short-limbed dwarfism with polydactyly, nail and tooth defects and, in about half of cases, an atrial septal defect — occurs in roughly 1 in 200 births in Lancaster County. So q = √0.005 = 0.0707 and 2pq = 2 × 0.929 × 0.0707 = 0.131: about one carrier in eight. Worldwide, at 1 in 60 000, q = 0.0041 and carriers are about 1 in 123. The allele is not doing anything different in Pennsylvania. It was simply present, by chance, in a couple who arrived in 1744 and left a great many descendants. That is the whole content of the founder effect, and it is drift, not selection — an option describing it as "selection for a locally advantageous allele" turns up reliably, and is wrong because nothing about the environment favours the allele.

Drift versus selection: when does an allele's fitness stop mattering?

Both act at once, and which wins is decided by one comparison. Drift moves an allele by an amount of order 1/(2Ne) per generation; selection moves it by an amount of order s. So selection dominates when |Nes| ≫ 1 and drift dominates when |Nes| ≪ 1. Alleles in the second class are called effectively neutral: they may be genuinely harmful and still drift to fixation, because the population is too small for selection to see them.

Put numbers on it. With Ne = 10 000, any allele with |s| < 5 × 10⁻⁵ is invisible to selection. With Ne = 100 — a captive breeding programme, an island endemic — the threshold rises to |s| < 0.005, and a whole class of mildly harmful mutations now fixes at random. This is Ohta's nearly neutral theory, and it is why small isolated populations accumulate genetic load and why conservation genetics cares about Ne rather than head count. It is also a reliable source of beyond-textbook items.

The mirror-image result is just as striking and just as examinable. A beneficial mutation is guaranteed nothing: its fixation probability is only about 2s. An allele conferring a 1 % advantage is lost 98 times out of 100, because while it exists in a single copy one unlucky generation destroys it. Adaptation is not the smooth ascent it is usually drawn as; it is a lottery that beneficial mutations enter over and over until one wins.

Exam technique · drift arithmetic, and the bounds that kill options for free

  1. Recognise the cue words. "Effective size", "breeding individuals", "harem", "only x males breed", "crashed to", "colonised by", "captive herd" — every one of them means Ne, and any option that has plainly used the census head count instead is a free elimination.
  2. Unequal sexes give you two bounds before you compute. Ne = 4NmNf/(Nm+Nf) is always ≤ the census total, with equality only at a 1 : 1 breeding ratio — so strike any option that is ≥ N at once. And when the ratio is badly skewed it collapses to about 4 × the rarer sex: 10 males and 90 females give 36 against an estimate of 40; 2 and 98 give 7.84 against 8. That estimate alone usually identifies the answer.
  3. Across generations it is the harmonic mean. Bound it first: the answer must lie between the smallest and the largest N in the run, and it sits near the smallest. For sizes 1000, 1000, 10, 1000, 1000 the answer is 48.1 — while the arithmetic mean, 802, is on the option list and is about 17 times too big. One crash dominates the whole average.
  4. Fixation probabilities come in only three forms. A neutral allele already at frequency p fixes with probability p. A brand-new neutral mutation is one copy among 2Ne, so 1/(2Ne) — the trap option is 1/Ne and it appears every time, because a diploid carries two copies of every autosomal locus. A beneficial mutation with small s fixes with probability only about 2s, so "it will certainly spread" is wrong. Neutral fixation, when it does happen, takes of order 4Ne generations.
  5. Heterozygosity has a shape you can check by eye. Ht = H₀(1 − 1/2Ne)t — the bracket is just under 1, so H falls monotonically, never below 0 and never above H₀. Any option larger than the starting heterozygosity is dead without a calculation.
  6. The vocabulary tell that separates drift from selection. A correct drift option contains randomly, by chance, non-selectively, regardless of fitness. An option containing consistent, advantageous, improves survival or better adapted is describing selection — and in an EXCEPT-style drift question it is usually a perfectly true sentence dropped into the wrong stem. Strike it on the wording, without re-arguing the biology.
  7. Effectively neutral, in one step. The threshold is |s| < 1/(2Ne): Ne = 100 → 0.005; Ne = 500 → 0.001; Ne = 10 000 → 5 × 10⁻⁵. If the stem's s is below the line, the allele's fitness is irrelevant and drift decides — which is the whole of the nearly neutral theory, and the reason a deleterious allele fixing is not a contradiction.
§ 07

Modes of selection — and sexual selection

The ideaSelection is not one thing. Once you accept that individuals vary along some measurable scale — height, beak depth, birth weight — there are only three interesting shapes the survival curve can take: it can favour one end, favour the middle, or favour both ends and dislike the middle. Those three do different things to the average and to the spread, and telling them apart from a pair of before-and-after graphs is a standard question. Then there is a fourth kind that is not about survival at all — it is about getting a mate, and it can push a trait in a direction that makes survival worse.

Definition & key facts

The three modes, by what they do to the distribution
Directional — one extreme is favoured; the mean shifts, and variance usually falls. Stabilising — intermediates are favoured; the mean is unchanged and variance falls. Disruptive (diversifying) — both extremes are favoured; the mean may be unchanged while variance rises, often producing a bimodal distribution. Read the mean and the spread separately; that pair of readings identifies the mode uniquely.
mean shifts → directional · spread shrinks → stabilising · spread grows → disruptive the two-question test that settles every graph question on this topic
Full definition, symbols and units
Balancing selection
Any regime that actively maintains two or more alleles rather than driving one to fixation. Three routes: heterozygote advantage (overdominance), negative frequency-dependent selection (rare morphs do better), and selection that varies in space or time. Contrast with directional selection, which removes variation.
Sexual selection
Differential reproduction arising from differences in mating success rather than in survival. Intrasexual — competition within one sex, usually males, giving weapons and size dimorphism. Intersexual — mate choice, usually by females, giving ornaments and displays. It is a component of natural selection in the broad sense, but is treated separately because it can oppose survival.
Why females usually choose
Anisogamy and the parental investment that follows it. Eggs are few and expensive, sperm many and cheap, so male reproductive success is limited by the number of mates and female success by resources and time (Bateman's principle). The sex investing less competes; the sex investing more chooses — and in species where males invest more, such as pipefish and some phalaropes, the roles and the ornaments reverse. That reversal is the evidence that the explanation is investment, not sex.

Four things routinely got wrong about the modes

(1) "Disruptive selection increases the mean." It usually does not — a symmetrical disruptive regime leaves the mean exactly where it was and increases the variance. Judge from the spread. (2) "Stabilising selection eliminates genetic variation." It reduces it; it does not eliminate it, because mutation replenishes variation and because heterozygotes carry alleles invisibly. (3) "Directional selection is the commonest mode." It is the most discussed. Most traits in most populations most of the time are under stabilising selection — which is precisely why species look stable for long stretches (§ 08). (4) "The birth-weight example proves stabilising selection is acting today." The classic data are from the 1930s–50s. Neonatal intensive care and routine caesarean section have substantially weakened selection at the low-weight end, which is a good example of a textbook case whose conclusion is sound but whose tense is not.

Sexual selection: why a trait that shortens your life can still spread

Darwin introduced sexual selection in 1871 precisely because natural selection alone could not explain the peacock's train, which is metabolically expensive, conspicuous to predators and aerodynamically useless. The resolution is that fitness is measured in offspring, not in survival, and a trait that halves your survival while tripling your mating success is a net gain. The formal statement: sexual selection can oppose survival selection, and the trait settles where the two balance. Everything else in this area is detail about why females should prefer such a trait at all.

  • Good genes / indicator models. The ornament is costly, so only a male in good condition can afford a big one. Preference for the ornament is therefore preference for condition, which may be partly heritable. Zahavi's handicap principle is the strong version: the signal is honest because it is costly, since a poor-quality male could not fake it.
  • Fisherian runaway. If a preference and the preferred trait become genetically correlated, each reinforces the other — sons inherit the trait, daughters the preference — and the pair can escalate rapidly until survival costs stop it. Note this needs no benefit at all beyond the sons' attractiveness.
  • Sensory bias. The preference may pre-date the trait, having evolved for some other reason (a fondness for red because ripe fruit is red), and the trait then exploits it.
  • Intrasexual competition produces a different signature: weapons, body-size dimorphism, territoriality and, less visibly, sperm competition and large testes in species where females mate multiply.

Two exam-relevant consequences. First, sexual selection is a major cause of speciation where mate choice itself becomes the isolating mechanism — the haplochromine cichlids of Lake Victoria are the standard case, and the fact that turbidity from eutrophication, by wrecking colour discrimination, has caused previously distinct species to hybridise is a satisfying demonstration that the barrier really was female preference. Second, sexual dimorphism is the observable signature: where males are much larger, expect male–male combat; where males are gaudy but not larger, expect female choice.

Intrasexual or intersexual — read it off the animal

Weapons within · ornaments between

Weapons within = intrasexual, competition within one sex: antlers, horns, enlarged canines, body-size dimorphism, territory-holding — males markedly larger than females. Ornaments between = intersexual, mate choice between the sexes: trains, colour, song, display grounds — males conspicuous but not necessarily bigger. The dimorphism a stem describes therefore tells you which mechanism it means before you have read the options. My own hook. The Latin prefixes already give you intra = within and inter = between for nothing, and the hook borrows those two words so that the only thing you have to add is the phenotypic signature — weapons against ornaments — which is what stems describe. One real gap: sperm competition and enlarged testes in multiply-mating species are also intrasexual and leave no visible weapon at all. And note where no hook is wanted — the three modes of selection. Do not memorise directional / stabilising / disruptive; read the graph twice, once for the mean and once for the spread. Mean moved → directional. Mean fixed, spread shrunk → stabilising. Mean fixed, spread grown → disruptive. Two readings generate all three every time, and the height of the curve carries no information whatever, because every density encloses the same area.

Go deeper · balancing selection, and the arithmetic of the sickle-cell polymorphism

The sickle-cell case is the one every syllabus uses, and it is worth doing numerically because the numbers come out right. Let A be the normal β-globin allele and S the sickle allele (a single Glu6Val substitution, GAG→GTG). In a region of intense Plasmodium falciparum transmission, assign wAA = 0.85 (malaria kills some AA children), wAS = 1 (protected, and not sickling), and wSS = 0 (sickle-cell disease, historically lethal before reproduction). Then sAA = 0.15 and sSS = 1.0.

The equilibrium is q̂ = sAA/(sAA + sSS) = 0.15/1.15 = 0.130. That predicts 2pq = 2 × 0.870 × 0.130 = 0.227 — about 23 % of the population carrying sickle trait — and q² = 0.017, so 1.7 % of births with sickle-cell disease. Both figures are close to what is observed in the highest-transmission belts of West and Central Africa, which is a real quantitative success for a two-line model.

Note two things the model also tells you. The equilibrium is stable: push q up and selection pushes it back down, and vice versa, which is what distinguishes balancing from directional selection. And the population pays for it — mean fitness at equilibrium is w̄ = 1 − sAAsSS/(sAA + sSS) = 0.870, a 13 % segregational load. The polymorphism is not an optimum; it is the best available compromise given that a heterozygote cannot breed true.

Frequency-dependent selection is the other main route to a stable polymorphism, and it behaves differently: fitness depends on how common the genotype is, not on the genotype alone. The scale-eating cichlid Perissodus microlepis comes in left-mouthed and right-mouthed forms that attack prey from opposite sides; whichever is rarer catches more, because prey watch the side they are usually attacked from, and the ratio oscillates around 0.5 over the decades. Plant self-incompatibility alleles are the extreme case — a rare S-allele can mate with everyone, so dozens of alleles are maintained at a single locus. And the same logic drives host–parasite Red Queen dynamics, which is one of the standard explanations for why sex exists at all.

One caution about a commonly over-stated case: the CCR5-Δ32 deletion, at about 10 % in northern Europeans, does protect homozygotes against CCR5-tropic HIV-1, but HIV is far too recent to have driven its frequency. The popular attribution to plague or smallpox is contested and the honest answer is that the selective history is unresolved. A stem asserting that Δ32 "was selected by the Black Death" is reproducing a textbook simplification.

§ 08

Speciation and reproductive isolation

The ideaSpecies do not come with labels attached. What actually happens is that one interbreeding group stops exchanging genes with another, and once the exchange stops the two halves drift and adapt apart until they could not interbreed even if you put them back together. So the question "how do new species form?" is really the question "what stops gene flow?" — and the answers split cleanly into things that prevent a mating happening at all and things that let the mating happen but spoil the result.

Definition & key facts

Biological species concept (Mayr)
Groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Its virtue is that it names a mechanism; its limits are that it cannot be applied to asexual organisms, to fossils, or to populations that never meet, and that many good species hybridise occasionally without merging.
speciation = the evolution of reproductive isolation not "becoming different" — differences without isolation are just variation
Full definition, symbols and units
Other species concepts, and when each is used
Morphological — defined by form; the only option for most fossils and for museum work, but arbitrary where variation is continuous. Ecological — defined by the niche occupied; handles asexual organisms. Phylogenetic — the smallest monophyletic group sharing a derived character; operational for sequence data, but tends to recognise many more species. None is universally correct, and a stem asking which concept a given piece of evidence supports is asking you to notice which one the evidence can actually test.
Prezygotic barriers
Anything that prevents fertilisation: habitat, temporal, behavioural, mechanical and gametic isolation. Cheap, in the sense that no gametes are wasted.
Postzygotic barriers
Anything that reduces the fitness of a hybrid that was formed: reduced hybrid viability, reduced hybrid fertility, and hybrid breakdown (the F₁ is fine but the F₂ or backcross generation is feeble or sterile). Expensive, which is why selection tends to build prezygotic barriers on top of them.

Allopatric and sympatric — and why the distinction is about gene flow, not maps

Allopatric speciation（異域種化）begins with a physical barrier: a river changes course, a mountain rises, sea level falls, a few individuals raft to an island. Gene flow stops, and drift and divergent selection then act independently on the two halves. It is the majority route in animals, and the evidence is geographic: sister species pairs sitting on opposite sides of a barrier of known age. The Isthmus of Panama, complete by about 3 Ma, left fifteen pairs of Alpheus snapping shrimp facing each other across it; antelope squirrels differ between the two rims of the Grand Canyon.

Sympatric speciation（同域種化）happens without any geographic barrier, which is much harder, because gene flow has to be blocked by something intrinsic while the two forms are living side by side. Three mechanisms are accepted:

  1. Polyploidy — instantaneous, and by far the commonest genuine case. Discussed below.
  2. Habitat or host shift with assortative mating. The apple-maggot fly Rhagoletis pomonella fed on hawthorn until apples were introduced to North America; a race that feeds on apples appeared in the Hudson Valley in the 1860s. Because these flies mate on the fruit and apples ripen earlier, host choice and mating are automatically linked, and the two races now differ in emergence time and allele frequencies with only a few per cent gene flow between them.
  3. Sexual selection. If female preference and male signal diverge, mating becomes assortative without any ecological difference at all — the Lake Victoria cichlids again.

Polyploidy: the one mechanism that makes a new species in a single generation

This is the highest-yield mechanism in the section because it is mechanistic and therefore easy to build a question around. In autopolyploidy a failure of cell division doubles one species' own chromosome set — either a mitotic failure in a growing shoot, whose flowers are then 4n, or the fusion of two unreduced (2n) gametes from 2n parents. Either way a 4n individual appears (one unreduced gamete on its own would only give a 3n zygote). It can self-fertilise or cross with another 4n plant and produce fertile 4n offspring. But crossed back to its 2n parents it gives 3n offspring — and at meiosis, three sets cannot pair two by two, so segregation is chaotic, the gametes are aneuploid and the triploid is sterile. Reproductive isolation is complete in one generation, in the same field, with no barrier of any kind. That is why polyploidy is the textbook proof that sympatric speciation is possible. In allopolyploidy two species hybridise — the F₁ is sterile because the two parental chromosome sets have no partners — and then chromosome doubling gives every chromosome a partner and restores fertility at a stroke. Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum, 2n = 6x = 42, genomes AABBDD) is a double allopolyploid; Spartina anglica arose in southern England in the 1890s; Tragopogon miscellus arose in the north-western United States in the 1920s and can be dated because the parent species were introduced from Europe only decades earlier. Polyploidy is common in plants — every flowering-plant lineage examined carries at least one whole-genome duplication in its history, and roughly 15 % of angiosperm and 31 % of fern speciation events are accompanied by an increase in ploidy — and rare in animals, largely because chromosomal sex determination does not survive the change.

What happens where two diverging populations meet again

Secondary contact is the interesting test, because it reveals how far isolation has got. Three outcomes:

  • Fusion. If hybrids are as fit as the parents, gene flow re-homogenises the two forms and the incipient species disappears. This is the usual outcome, and it is why speciation needs time.
  • Reinforcement. If hybrids are unfit, any allele that makes an individual avoid mating across the boundary is favoured, so prezygotic barriers strengthen where the ranges overlap. The signature is reproductive character displacement: courtship signals differ more in the zone of overlap than they do in allopatry. Note the logic — reinforcement is selection acting against hybridisation, which is a form of natural selection completing a job that geography started.
  • Stability. A narrow hybrid zone persists indefinitely, maintained by a balance between dispersal into it and selection against hybrids within it.

The tempo question is separate from the mechanism question, and stems conflate them. Gradualism pictures divergence accumulating steadily; punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould, 1972) is the observation that the fossil record more often shows long stasis interrupted by geologically abrupt change, generally at speciation events in small peripheral populations.

Punctuated equilibrium is not what the distractors say it is

Three misreadings, all of which appear as options. It does not propose that new species appear in one generation. "Geologically abrupt" means tens of thousands of years — invisible in a rock sequence, thousands of generations to a biologist. It is not an alternative to natural selection, and it is not saltationism; the mechanism during the punctuation is ordinary selection in a small, peripheral population. It is not a claim that gradualism never happens — both patterns are documented, and the debate is about relative frequency. What punctuated equilibrium genuinely adds is that stasis is data: species staying the same for millions of years is something that needs explaining, and the explanation is stabilising selection plus gene flow across a wide range, not an absence of variation.

Exam technique · isolation and speciation — recipes, and one list you should not memorise

  1. Pre- or postzygotic is a single question: did a zygote form? Anything preventing fertilisation is prezygotic; anything damaging a hybrid that already exists is postzygotic. A sterile hybrid — mule, liger, tigon — is postzygotic every time, so an option filing it under prezygotic is wrong on the definition alone.
  2. There is no mnemonic for the five prezygotic barriers and you do not need one — the list is the timeline of one mating attempt, in order. Same place? (habitat) → same season, same hour? (temporal) → does the courtship match? (behavioural) → do the bodies fit? (mechanical) → do the gametes recognise each other? (gametic). Walk the timeline and you regenerate all five in the right order with nothing to forget. The three postzygotic barriers are the same trick run later: does the zygote live (viability) → can the adult breed (fertility) → does the F₂ hold up (breakdown). Learn the two timelines and the eight names look after themselves.
  3. On a polyploidy stem, do the ploidy arithmetic before reading the options. 2n × 2n → 2n, fertile. 4n × 4n → 4n, fertile. 4n × 2n → 3n, and three sets cannot pair two by two at meiosis I, so the gametes are aneuploid and the triploid is sterile. Whichever option does not turn on that odd number is not stating the mechanism, however true it sounds — "polyploids are vigorous" and "polyploids outcompete their diploid parents" are the classic pair, and vigour is neither necessary nor sufficient for speciation.
  4. Allopatric versus sympatric is about gene flow, not distance on a map. If the stem has already told you there is no geographic barrier, any option explaining the isolation by geographic separation contradicts the stem's own premise — strike it without doing any biology.
  5. Punctuated equilibrium has three fixed distractor shapes. "New species appear in one generation" (no — geologically abrupt means tens of thousands of years), "it is an alternative to natural selection" (no — the mechanism during a punctuation is ordinary selection in a small peripheral population), and "it shows gradualism never occurs" (no — both patterns are documented and the argument is about relative frequency). Its actual content is that stasis is data.
  6. Species-concept questions: ask what the evidence could physically test. You cannot test a fossil, or an asexual organism, or two populations that never meet, for interbreeding — so the biological species concept cannot be the answer there, whatever else the option says.
  7. Where the time goes. Barrier-naming and vocabulary items are ten-second recognition — answer and move on. Only the ploidy arithmetic and the secondary-contact outcomes (fusion / reinforcement / stable hybrid zone) are worth the full 66 seconds.
§ 09

Radiation, convergence, homology and analogy

The ideaTwo organisms can look alike for exactly two reasons: they inherited the resemblance from a shared ancestor, or they arrived at it separately because the same job has the same best answer. Telling these apart is the single most useful skill in the whole topic, because the first kind is evidence of relationship and the second kind is evidence of nothing except that the physics of swimming has not changed. And the same test decides the other half of this section: when a lineage suddenly fills a lot of new roles, it is the opportunity that is new, not the mechanism.

Definition & key facts

Homology versus analogy
Homologous（同源）structures are similar because of common ancestry — judged by correspondence of parts, position and embryonic origin, never by function. Analogous（同功）structures are similar because of convergent evolution — judged by the absence of that correspondence and by the fact that the shared feature is absent in the intervening ancestors. The general word for a similarity not due to common ancestry is homoplasy.
same structure, different job → homology · same job, different structure → analogy the test is the structure and its position, never what it is used for
Full definition, symbols and units
Adaptive radiation（適應輻射）
Rapid diversification of one lineage into many ecologically distinct descendants. It requires ecological opportunity, which arrives in three ways: colonisation of an empty habitat (island archipelagos, a new lake), a mass extinction that vacates niches (placental mammals after the K–Pg boundary, 66 Ma), or a key innovation that opens a way of life the lineage could not previously exploit (flowers, wings, the amniote egg).
Convergent evolution
Independent evolution of similar features in unrelated lineages under similar selection. The strongest evidence that selection, not chance, is responsible is repeated convergence: the same solution appearing many times independently under the same conditions.
Orthologues and paralogues
The molecular version of the same distinction, and a favourite of examiners. Orthologues are copies of a gene in two species that diverged when the species diverged — human and mouse β-globin. Paralogues arose by gene duplication within a genome — human α-globin and human β-globin, or the whole set of Hox genes. Both are homologous; only orthologues track the species tree, which is why phylogenies built on the wrong one go wrong.

Homologous or analogous — one couplet

Same source, different course · same course, different source

Same source, different course = homologous — one origin, and the jobs have since diverged: humerus, radius and ulna, carpals and digits, doing duty as an arm, a flipper, a wing and a horse's leg. Same course, different source = analogous — one job, arrived at from different origins: a bird's wing (a modified tetrapod forelimb) and an insect's wing (an outgrowth of the body wall, with no bones in it at all). The operative word in both halves is source: you classify on origin, never on the course the structure has been put to. The general word for the second kind of similarity is homoplasy. The couplet is mine; the rule underneath it — judge by origin, never by function — is the standard one and is what the exam actually tests. Two things the couplet must not be allowed to become. First, a different course is not a requirement: homologues frequently share a function — a bird's forelimb and a bat's are both used to fly — and the real test is always correspondence of parts, position and embryonic origin, never what the structure is used for. Second, the hook cannot resolve the case examiners like best: bird wing versus bat wing is both, homologous as forelimbs and analogous as flight surfaces. For that you need the method in the figure above — map the character onto the tree and count origins. One origin → homology. Two or more → homoplasy.

Adaptive radiation: the pattern, and what actually causes it

The standard cases are worth carrying as a short list, because a stem will name one and expect you to know what it illustrates. Darwin's finches on the Galápagos: one South American colonist, fourteen to eighteen species differing mainly in beak form and hence in diet. Hawaiian silverswords: about thirty species of tree, shrub, vine, mat and rosette plant, all descended from a single Californian tarweed that arrived roughly five million years ago — as morphologically diverse a radiation as exists, and confirmed as a single clade by molecular data. Lake Victoria cichlids: several hundred endemic haplochromine species in a lake that in its present form may be under 15 000 years old. Placental mammals after the K–Pg extinction, moving into the roles vacated by non-avian dinosaurs.

What unites them is not a special evolutionary mechanism — it is ordinary selection acting where competition is weak and unfilled niches are numerous. That is the exam point: adaptive radiation is a description of circumstances, not of a process. When a question asks what caused a radiation, the answer is always ecological opportunity plus some means of dividing the resource, never a change in how evolution works.

Go deeper · Anolis, molecular convergence and the limits of "deep homology"

The Anolis lizards of the Greater Antilles are the most instructive single case in this section, because they combine radiation and convergence in one system. On Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica and Puerto Rico the lizards independently radiated into the same set of ecomorphs — trunk-ground, trunk-crown, twig, grass-bush, crown-giant — each with its characteristic limb proportions, tail length and toe-pad size. Molecular phylogenies show that the ecomorphs on each island are more closely related to the other lizards on their own island than to the same ecomorph elsewhere. The similarity between two twig anoles from different islands is therefore analogy, and the resemblance is repeatable enough to argue that the outcome of adaptive radiation is substantially predictable.

Convergence reaches down to sequence. Echolocating bats and toothed whales, separated by 90 million years, show convergent amino-acid substitutions in Prestin, the motor protein of outer hair cells — so much so that a tree built on that gene alone incorrectly groups the echolocators together. That is a cautionary result: molecular characters are not automatically immune to homoplasy, and this is why phylogenies use many independent loci. Lactase persistence in humans is another: it arose independently in European and in East African pastoralist populations, through different mutations in the same regulatory region — convergent evolution observable within our own species in the last ten thousand years.

Deep homology is the subtlest idea here and the easiest to overstate. Pax6 specifies eye development in vertebrates, in cephalopods and in Drosophila, and the mouse gene will induce ectopic eyes when expressed in a fly — fly eyes, not mouse eyes. The correct reading is that the genetic control circuit is homologous while the organs are not: the shared ancestor had a Pax6-controlled photoreceptive patch, and each lineage independently elaborated its own kind of eye around it. An option claiming that this makes vertebrate and cephalopod eyes homologous overreaches; an option claiming the two eyes have nothing in common misses the genetics. The precise statement — homologous regulator, analogous organ — is the answer worth writing.

§ 10

Neutral theory and the molecular clock

The ideaMost changes in DNA make no difference to the organism. Those changes still accumulate — they just accumulate at a steady rate set by how often they happen, not by how useful they are. A steady rate of anything is a clock, so a gene can be used to date a split. The catch, and it is the whole subject, is that the rate is only steady for the parts of the sequence where nothing is at stake.

Definition & key facts

Neutral theory（中性理論，Kimura 1968）
The claim that the great majority of substitutions that become fixed at the molecular level are selectively neutral, and that most molecular polymorphism within a species is transient neutral variation on its way to fixation or loss. It is a theory about the molecular level and about fixed differences; it says nothing against selection shaping morphology.
k = 2Neµ × 12Ne = µ new neutral mutations per generation × probability each fixes — Ne cancels exactly

Why the cancellation matters
A large population generates more mutations but fixes each with lower probability; a small one generates fewer but fixes each more readily. The two effects cancel exactly, so the neutral substitution rate equals the neutral mutation rate and is independent of population size. That independence is the theoretical justification for a molecular clock — without it, a gene's rate would vary with a species' demography and no clock would be possible.
Molecular clock
Sequence divergence between two lineages accumulates roughly linearly with time, so a divergence measured today can be converted into a date once the rate has been calibrated against an independently dated event — a fossil, or the closure of a seaway.
dN/dS (Ka/Ks)
The ratio of nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site to synonymous substitutions per synonymous site. Synonymous changes are the internal neutral control. < 1 means purifying selection (the usual finding); = 1 means neutrality; > 1 means positive, diversifying selection — rare, and always worth noticing.

Rate reflects constraint, not importance

Different proteins tick at wildly different speeds, and the ordering is the single most useful thing to understand here. It is not that fast proteins are evolving harder; it is that they are constrained less, so a larger fraction of their mutations are neutral and survive to fix. A protein in which almost every residue matters has almost no neutral mutations available, so almost nothing fixes.

Worked example 6 — calibrating a clock, and the factor of two

Lineages A and B are known from the fossil record to have separated 400 million years ago, and they differ at 40 % of the aligned amino-acid positions of a protein. Lineage C differs from A at 15 % of the same positions. (a) Find the substitution rate. (b) When did C separate from A? (c) A different protein of 400 residues differs at 24 positions between two species; its per-lineage rate is 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ substitutions per site per year. How long ago did they diverge? (d) What would make all of these answers too young?

  1. (a) The 40 % divergence accumulated in both lineages over 400 My, so the total path is 2 × 400 = 800 lineage-million-years: r = 0.40 / (2 × 400) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ substitutions per site per million years, per lineage.
  2. (b) t = D/(2r) = 0.15 / (2 × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.15/10⁻³ = 150 million years.
  3. Watch the trap. Dividing by r instead of 2r gives 300 My — exactly twice too old, and it will be on the option list. The shortcut that avoids it entirely is to work in proportions: 400 × (15/40) = 150 My, because the factor of two cancels between calibration and query.
  4. (c) D = 24/400 = 0.060, so t = 0.060 / (2 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁹) = 3.0 × 10⁷ years = 30 million years. Note the units: the rate was given per year, so the answer is in years — converting at the end is safer than converting at the start.
  5. (d) Saturation. Once a site has changed twice, you see one difference or none, so observed divergence understates true divergence and every date comes out too recent. Corrections such as Jukes–Cantor exist precisely to undo this, and the effect is why a deep split should never be dated from a fast-evolving sequence.
  6. Two further caveats worth a sentence in any written answer: the rate is per generation at bottom, so species with short generations tick faster in absolute time; and any site under selection breaks the assumption, which is why clocks are calibrated on synonymous sites or pseudogenes where possible.

What neutral theory does not say

Every one of these appears as a distractor, and all four are false. (1) "Neutral theory denies natural selection." It does not. Kimura's own position was that purifying selection is ubiquitous — most mutations that matter are deleterious and are removed — and that what is left to fix is mostly neutral. Adaptive evolution of morphology is untouched by the claim. (2) "Neutral means the mutation has no effect." Neutral means no effect on fitness, in that environment, at that population size; a synonymous change can alter codon usage or splicing and stop being neutral. (3) "Substitution rates should be higher in large populations." They are not, for neutral sites, and that exact cancellation is the theory's central result. (4) "The molecular clock runs at one universal rate." It does not — it runs at a different rate for every class of site, and it is influenced by generation time, metabolic rate and repair efficiency. The honest formulation is that a clock is approximately constant for a given gene across related lineages, which is why every serious estimate quotes a confidence interval and uses several calibration points.

Where dN/dS > 1 actually turns up

Positive selection at the sequence level is rare enough that the standard examples are worth knowing, and they share one feature: an arms race or a recognition problem where being different is itself the point. The peptide-binding groove of MHC class I and II — the residues that contact antigen, and only those, show dN/dS above 1, while the rest of the molecule is conserved. Surface antigens of rapidly evolving pathogens: influenza haemagglutinin, HIV-1 env. Reproductive proteins such as sea-urchin bindin, which links directly back to the gametic isolation of § 08. Notice how the statistic is used: because the synonymous rate is an internal neutral control measured in the same gene, dN/dS is a test for selection that needs no external calibration at all.

Exam technique · clock questions, and never losing the factor of two

  1. Decide which of two shapes the stem has, because there are only two. Given a calibrated pair and asked about a second pair, do not compute a rate at all — scale directly: t = tcal × D/Dcal. The factor of two cancels between calibration and query and the trap cannot reach you: 400 × (15/40) = 150 Ma, in one line. Given a rate quoted per lineage instead, use t = D/(2r).
  2. If two options differ by exactly a factor of two, that is the question. 150 and 300 on the same list is not a coincidence; 300 is what you get by dividing by r instead of 2r. With a per-lineage rate, take the smaller — the 2 is there because both lineages have been changing since the split.
  3. Turn differences into a proportion first. 24 differences over 400 residues is D = 0.060, not 24. A stem that hands you both a count and a sequence length is instructing you to divide; a stem that gives a count with no length is not asking for a date at all.
  4. Unit filter. Rate per year → answer in years; rate per million years → answer in My. Convert at the end, never at the start, and check the exponent against the option list before committing: a factor of 10⁶ is easier to drop than a factor of 2 and just as fatal.
  5. Saturation biases in one direction only. A site that has changed twice shows one difference or none, so observed divergence understates the truth and every date comes out too young. An option claiming saturation makes an estimate too old is wrong on direction alone — no calculation needed.
  6. Rate measures freedom, not importance — so do not memorise the rate table. Ask how much of the molecule has a job: fibrinopeptides (cleaved off and discarded) > haemoglobin (free surface, constrained haem pocket) > cytochrome c (must dock on both faces) > histone H4 (nearly every residue contacts DNA or another histone). The same generator gives synonymous > nonsynonymous, intron and pseudogene > exon, and third codon position > first and second. Any stem inverting one of these is testing that one idea, and the answer is always "the faster sequence is the freer one".
  7. dN/dS > 1 has a recognition cue: being different is itself the point. MHC peptide-binding groove, influenza haemagglutinin, HIV-1 env, sea-urchin bindin — arms races and recognition systems. Everything else defaults to < 1, purifying. And match the marker to the depth: a recent split wants a fast sequence, a deep split a slow one, so an option dating a deep divergence from the mitochondrial control region is offering you a saturated clock.
§ 11

The major evolutionary transitions, in outline

The ideaStand far enough back from the history of life and it is not a smooth ramp. It is a small number of moments where the rules changed — where things that used to reproduce on their own could afterwards only reproduce as part of something bigger. Each of those moments needed the same two things: a new way of storing information, and some way of stopping the parts from cheating on the whole.

Definition & key facts

A major evolutionary transition
In the sense of Maynard Smith and Szathmáry (1995): a change after which entities that could previously replicate independently can only replicate as part of a larger whole, usually accompanied by a new way of storing or transmitting information and by a division of labour among the parts.
independent replicators → obligate parts of a larger replicator the common signature of every transition on the list
Full definition, symbols and units
Endosymbiotic theory
Mitochondria descend from an engulfed alphaproteobacterium and plastids from an engulfed cyanobacterium. The evidence is a checklist: a double membrane whose inner layer resembles a bacterial plasma membrane, a circular genome with no histones, 70S ribosomes, division by binary fission rather than by the cell's own machinery, sensitivity to antibacterial antibiotics, and rRNA sequences that place them squarely inside the bacterial tree.
Conflict suppression
Every transition creates an opportunity to cheat, so every transition requires a mechanism that removes it. Fair meiosis stops one chromosome from over-transmitting; uniparental (usually maternal) inheritance of mitochondria removes competition between organelle lineages; worker policing removes the incentive for individual workers to lay eggs. If a stem asks what a transition requires, this is the answer that is usually being looked for.

Go deeper · kin selection, eusociality, and a textbook simplification that has not survived

The transition to sterile castes is the hardest one to explain, because it requires an allele that makes its bearer not reproduce at all. Hamilton's answer is that fitness should be counted inclusively: an allele can spread by helping copies of itself in relatives. Formally, r·b > c. Count in expected allele copies, and the diploid arithmetic is stark: your own offspring carry any given allele of yours with probability 0.5, and so does a full sibling — r = 0.5 both ways — so raising one extra sibling is worth exactly one offspring of your own, and no more. Sacrificing yourself is a different sum, because r to yourself is 1: two brothers (2 × 0.5) or eight first cousins (8 × 0.125) just repay it, which is Haldane's remark about jumping into a river.

The standard textbook argument then goes further. In the haplodiploid Hymenoptera — ants, bees, wasps — males are haploid, so a female's full sisters share all of their father's genome plus half their mother's: r = 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.75, higher than the 0.5 she would share with her own daughters. A worker therefore propagates more copies of her alleles by raising sisters than by breeding. This is elegant and it is not sufficient, and the exam has been known to reward knowing why. Relatedness to brothers is only 0.25, so unless the colony's sex ratio is biased toward females the average relatedness to siblings is back to 0.5. Eusociality has also evolved in fully diploid groups — termites, naked mole rats, some shrimps and aphids — where haplodiploidy cannot be the explanation. The current view puts most weight on lifetime monogamy (which guarantees r = 0.5 to siblings) plus ecological factors: a defensible, valuable nest, and high costs of dispersing to breed alone.

The general lesson is the one that closes § 01. Apparent altruism is never explained by benefit to the species. It is explained by inclusive fitness among relatives, by reciprocity among non-relatives, or by manipulation — and an option offering "for the survival of the colony" as the mechanism is offering the one answer that population genetics rules out.
