Glycolysis: The Ten Steps, One Diagram Each

Note 04 treats glycolysis as one component of the carbohydrate picture and gives you the pathway as a balance sheet. This page does the opposite: it stops at every one of the ten reactions and asks what the chemistry is actually doing there, and why it has to be that reaction and not another. Every step gets its own diagram — substrate on the left, product on the right, the group that changes picked out in colour, the cosubstrates on curved arrows, the enzyme and its ΔG°′ on the arrow, and a single-headed arrow wherever the reaction cannot be run backwards. The carbon numbers run unbroken through all ten pictures, so you can put a finger on glucose C1 in the first diagram and follow it to the methyl carbon of pyruvate in the last. Memory hooks for the eleven intermediates, the ten enzymes, the three irreversible steps and the ATP ledger are gathered at the end, in English, because the paper is in English.

Start here

  1. Understand first — § 02 and § 04 read as one argument: the carbonyl has to move from C1 to C2 before the sugar can be cut into two three-carbon pieces, because a retro-aldol cleavage always breaks the bond two carbons away from the carbonyl. Get that and the whole preparatory phase stops being arbitrary. Then § 06: one active site performs an oxidation and a phosphorylation together, and the energy of the first pays for the second.   2. § 01

Step 1 · Hexokinase — trapping the sugar

The ideaThe very first thing a cell does to a sugar is bolt a phosphate onto its far end. It costs one unit of the cell's energy currency, and the cell pays it before it has earned anything, because the charged molecule that results can no longer leave through the door it came in by. Nothing else about the sugar changes here: five of its six carbons are untouched, and the carbon that was the aldehyde is still the aldehyde.

How to read the ten diagrams

The drawing convention
Every sugar is drawn open-chain as a vertical backbone, one dot per carbon, numbered down the left. In solution the hexoses are almost entirely cyclic — glucose 6-phosphate is a pyranose ring, fructose 6-phosphate a furanose — and the enzymes of steps 2 and 4 open the ring as part of their mechanism. The open chain is drawn here because it is the only representation in which a carbon keeps the same position on the page — with one deliberate exception, step 5, where the upper half is drawn end-for-end because that is precisely what triose phosphate isomerase does to its numbering.
The colour code
Teal marks the group that changes, on both sides of the arrow, so the eye can find the reaction without reading. Grey is everything that does not change. Green flags a phosphate whose transfer potential exceeds that of ATP — the acyl phosphate of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and the enol phosphate of phosphoenolpyruvate, and nothing else. Amber is ATP/ADP traffic and, deliberately, the three irreversible arrows. Indigo is everything else that enters or leaves: NAD⁺, Pi, water.
The two kinds of arrow
A single thick amber arrow means the reaction runs one way in a living cell; the seven thin double arrows mean the reaction sits close enough to equilibrium that it will run either way depending on which side is being drained. Under each arrow are two numbers: ΔG°′, the standard free-energy change, and the sign of ΔG at real cellular concentrations. They frequently disagree, and which one you are being asked about is the single commonest hinge in this topic.
glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADP ΔG°′ = −16.7 kJ·mol⁻¹ hexokinase (EC 2.7.1.1), a transferase; Mg²⁺·ATP is the true substrate
Full definition, symbols and units
ΔG°′ versus ΔG
ΔG°′ is measured with every species at 1 mol·L⁻¹, pH 7, 25 °C. ΔG is what the reaction actually does at the concentrations present, and it is the only one that decides direction: ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q. A step can have a strongly positive ΔG°′ and still run forward all day, which is exactly what step 4 does.
Phosphoryl-transfer potential
The ΔG°′ of hydrolysis of a phosphorylated compound. Anything more negative than ATP's −30.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ can phosphorylate ADP; anything less negative cannot. Two glycolytic intermediates qualify, and they are the two that make ATP.
Condensed chain notation
Interior carbons are written CHOH without their stereochemistry, because no glycolytic step inverts a stereocentre — steps 2, 4 and 5 destroy stereocentres, turning them into carbonyl carbons or plain CH₂ groups, and the reverse reactions rebuild them in the original configuration, but nothing in the pathway epimerises anything. Where a bond genuinely changes — a carbonyl appearing, a double bond forming — the diagram shows it explicitly.

The chemistry: a phosphoryl transfer that must not become a hydrolysis

A kinase moves the terminal phosphoryl group of onto a nucleophile — here the C6 hydroxyl oxygen of glucose, which attacks the γ-phosphorus. The obvious hazard is that water is also a nucleophile, is present at 55 mol·L⁻¹, and would give ADP and Pi with nothing gained. Hexokinase solves this with the textbook case of induced fit: the two lobes of the enzyme close around glucose like a hand closing on a ball, and the closure is what completes the active site and expels water from it. Glucose is not merely bound; glucose is the trigger that builds the catalytic machine. This is why hexokinase appears in the enzymology chapter as often as in the metabolism chapter.

The reason a cell bothers is threefold, and all three matter. A phosphate ester carries two negative charges, so no GLUT transporter will carry it — the sugar is trapped. Free intracellular glucose therefore stays low, so the inward gradient that drives uptake never collapses. And glucose 6-phosphate is the substrate the next four pathways all recognise: nothing downstream of here works on unphosphorylated sugar.

Energetics and regulation

ΔG°′ = −16.7 kJ·mol⁻¹, which is a K′eq of about 8 × 10². That alone would not make the step irreversible; what does is the cellular ΔG of roughly −33 kJ·mol⁻¹, which is the number to quote if a stem asks why the reaction cannot be reversed. Gluconeogenesis does not reverse it. It hydrolyses the ester with a different enzyme, glucose 6-phosphatase, in a different compartment, in only three tissues.

Hexokinases I–III are inhibited by their own product, glucose 6-phosphate — local housekeeping. Glucokinase (hexokinase IV) is not, and its [S]0.5 of about 10 mM makes the liver a glucose sensor rather than a constant consumer; note 04 § 01 does that comparison in full and it is not repeated here.

The trap on step 1 · irreversible is not the same as committed

Candidates who have learned "hexokinase is irreversible" reach for it when asked for the committed step, and lose the mark. Committed means the intermediate has nowhere else to go, and glucose 6-phosphate has four exits — glycolysis, glycogen, the pentose phosphate pathway and, in liver, kidney and intestine, back out as free glucose. The committed step of glycolysis is PFK-1, two reactions later. A second trap in the same family: this step is irreversible, but it is not the rate-limiting one either. Irreversible, committed and rate-limiting are three different claims, and only PFK-1 satisfies all three.

§ 02

Step 2 · Phosphohexose isomerase — moving the carbonyl

The ideaThe second reaction looks like the cell shuffling a molecule for no reason. Nothing is added, nothing is removed, nothing is oxidised, and the free-energy change is almost zero. It is nevertheless one of the most consequential reactions in the pathway, because moving the double-bonded oxygen one place along the chain is what makes it possible to cut the sugar cleanly down the middle two steps later — and it opens up the far end so that the second phosphate has somewhere to go.

glucose 6-phosphate ⇌ fructose 6-phosphate ΔG°′ = +1.7 kJ·mol⁻¹ phosphohexose isomerase (phosphoglucose isomerase, EC 5.3.1.9); no cofactor, no metal

Why this reaction has to happen before the cut

An aldol cleavage is not a general-purpose pair of scissors. It breaks one specific bond: the one between the carbon α to the carbonyl and the carbon β to it, where the β carbon carries a hydroxyl. Count that out on each of the two molecules and the whole preparatory phase explains itself.

On glucose 6-phosphate the carbonyl is at C1, so α is C2 and β is C3. A retro-aldol would break C2–C3, giving a two-carbon fragment and a four-carbon fragment. Neither is a triose, the two-carbon piece carries no phosphate at all and would diffuse straight out of the cell, and the pathway would need two entirely separate sets of downstream enzymes.

On fructose 1,6-bisphosphate the carbonyl is at C2, so α is C3 and β is C4. The retro-aldol breaks C3–C4 — dead centre — giving two three-carbon fragments, each carrying one of the two phosphates. That is the entire justification for step 2, and it also tells you why step 3 phosphorylates C1 rather than anywhere else: C1 has to be charged before the cut, because after the cut it is the exposed end of the upper half and would otherwise leak away.

α β γ · phosphates in a nucleotide

Counted outward from the sugar: α is the phosphate bonded to the 5′ carbon, then β, then γ at the far tip. A kinase hands over the γ phosphate, which is the sense used in § 01 — the C6 hydroxyl of glucose attacks the γ-phosphorus of ATP. Click the ATP button there for the diagram.

α β · the anomeric carbon of a sugar

Fixed when the ring closes, by which face the new C1 hydroxyl ends up on: α- or β-D-glucopyranose, β-D-fructofuranose 6-phosphate. Nothing to do with phosphates, and there is no γ. This is the sense in the "Go deeper" panel below.

α β γ · position along a carbon chain

Counted from the functional group: the α carbon is the one next to the carbonyl or carboxyl, then β, then γ. This is the sense used in the paragraphs just above, where the retro-aldol breaks the bond between the α and β carbons — and the sense that β-oxidation and γ-carboxylation are named for.

What is examined here

Almost never the reaction itself — it has no cofactor, no regulation and a ΔG°′ of nearly zero, so there is little to ask. What is asked, repeatedly, is the reason: "why must glucose 6-phosphate be isomerised before cleavage?" The answer that scores is the one about where the aldol bond lies, not the vague one about "making the two halves the same". The two halves are not the same when they come off the aldolase — that is step 5's job, one reaction later.

The trap on step 2 · this is an isomerisation, not an epimerisation

Glucose and fructose are constitutional isomers (aldose against ketose); glucose and mannose are epimers (they differ at one stereocentre, C2); glucose and galactose are epimers at C4. An option that calls step 2 an epimerisation, or that says the enzyme "inverts C2", is wrong — nothing here is inverted: C2 passes through a planar enediol and comes out as a carbonyl carbon with no stereochemistry at all, so the C2 stereocentre is destroyed rather than epimerised (glucose 6-phosphate has four stereocentres, fructose 6-phosphate three), and running the enzyme backwards regenerates C2 in the gluco configuration, never the manno one. That is the difference between an isomerase and an epimerase. The other half of this trap is directional: the enzyme is freely reversible and carries the same name in both directions, so gluconeogenesis uses this very enzyme rather than a bypass. Only the three irreversible steps need bypassing.

Go deeper · the ring forms, and the enediol that both isomerases use

In water, less than one glucose molecule in a hundred is in the open-chain form the diagrams draw; the rest is α- or β-D-glucopyranose, a six-membered ring closed by the C5 hydroxyl attacking the C1 aldehyde to give a hemiacetal. Fructose 6-phosphate is likewise β-D-fructofuranose 6-phosphate, a five-membered ring closed by the C5 hydroxyl onto the C2 ketone. Phosphohexose isomerase therefore does three things in sequence: it opens the pyranose ring, it isomerises the open chain, and it closes the furanose ring.

The isomerisation itself is a two-step proton shuffle through a cis-enediolate. An active-site glutamate removes the C2 proton, the C1=O becomes C1–O⁻, and the intermediate is a flat two-carbon fragment carrying a delocalised negative charge across O1–C1=C2–O2. Reprotonating on C1 instead of C2 completes the move: the carbonyl has migrated one carbon along. Nothing was oxidised — the same electrons are present throughout, only rearranged, which is why the free-energy change is so small.

This is worth the effort because triose phosphate isomerase at step 5 uses the identical chemistry on a three-carbon substrate. Learn the enediol once and you have the mechanism of two of the ten steps. It also sets up a piece of pathology one step further on: the triose phosphate enediolate, if it escapes, β-eliminates its C3 phosphate and collapses to methylglyoxal, a reactive dicarbonyl implicated in diabetic tissue damage — which is why triose phosphate isomerase carries a mobile loop that closes over the intermediate and releases it only after reprotonation. The hexose enediol has no phosphate β to the double bond, so phosphohexose isomerase needs no such lid.

One curiosity that occasionally appears in a stem: phosphohexose isomerase is a moonlighting protein. The same polypeptide, secreted, acts as neuroleukin and as autocrine motility factor. Its deficiency causes a chronic non-spherocytic haemolytic anaemia — the red cell, having no mitochondria, feels every glycolytic lesion.

§ 03

Step 3 · PFK-1 — the committed step

The ideaNow the cell spends its second and last unit of energy, putting a phosphate on the other end of the sugar. This is the point of no return. Everything before it could still have been diverted into storage or into another pathway; after it there is nowhere for the molecule to go but down the line. Because it is the point of no return, it is also where nearly all of the control lives — this one enzyme listens to more signals than the other nine put together.

fructose 6-phosphate + ATP → fructose 1,6-bisphosphate + ADP ΔG°′ = −14.2 kJ·mol⁻¹ phosphofructokinase-1 (EC 2.7.1.11), Mg²⁺; committed, rate-limiting and irreversible

Why phosphorylate twice before cutting anything

Put the question the other way round: what would happen if the cell cut first and phosphorylated afterwards? It would produce two uncharged trioses, both membrane-permeant, both able to walk out of the cell before an enzyme reached them, and it would then have to spend the same two ATP anyway to tag them. Phosphorylating first costs nothing extra and closes the leak. The investment phase is not an energetic priming — the sugar is not made "more reactive" in any useful sense — it is a labelling operation. The tag traps, and the tag is also the handle every downstream enzyme grips.

Energetics and regulation

ΔG°′ = −14.2 kJ·mol⁻¹, cellular ΔG ≈ −22 kJ·mol⁻¹. Being irreversible, it is bypassed in gluconeogenesis by fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase, which hydrolyses the C1 phosphate rather than handing it back to ADP — the classic pattern for undoing a kinase.

The regulation is the most examined in metabolism and note 04 § 03 and § 08 treat it at length; the summary that belongs on this page is that PFK-1 is switched on by , ADP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate and off by ATP, citrate and H⁺. The ATP effect is the one to understand rather than memorise: ATP is the substrate at the active site and an inhibitor at a separate low-affinity allosteric site, and binding there does not lower Vmax — it makes the fructose 6-phosphate saturation curve sigmoidal, raising the apparent Km. AMP competes that effect away. So the enzyme is not reading [ATP]; it is reading the ATP-to-AMP ratio, which is a far sharper signal.

What is examined here

Three things, in order of frequency. (1) That this — not hexokinase — is the committed and rate-limiting step. (2) That fructose 2,6-bisphosphate is the activator of PFK-1 while fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is the activator of pyruvate kinase; swapping those two numbers is the commonest single distractor in carbohydrate metabolism. (3) That PFK-1 is inhibited by its own substrate ATP, which sounds contradictory until you separate the catalytic site from the allosteric one.

The trap on step 3 · PFK-1 is not PFK-2, and F2,6BP is not an intermediate

PFK-2 is a different protein with a different job: it makes fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, which is a signal, sits on no pathway, and is consumed by nothing. It exists only to be sensed by PFK-1 and by fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase. An option describing F2,6BP as "an intermediate of glycolysis" is always wrong. A second trap: muscle PFK-1 deficiency is Tarui disease, GSD VII, and it looks like McArdle disease — exercise intolerance, cramps, no rise in lactate — but with two distinguishing features. There is no second wind, and a high-carbohydrate meal makes the patient worse, because glucose cannot be used and it suppresses the free fatty acids the muscle was relying on. There is usually a mild haemolysis too, since red cells carry the muscle subunit as well.

§ 04

Step 4 · Aldolase — the cut

The ideaThis is the step the pathway is named after. A six-carbon sugar carrying a phosphate at each end is cut exactly in the middle, and out come two three-carbon pieces, each holding one of the phosphates. On paper the reaction looks impossible: it runs strongly uphill under standard conditions, more steeply uphill than any other step in glycolysis. It runs forward anyway, briskly, all day, and understanding why is worth more marks than the reaction itself.

fructose 1,6-bisphosphate ⇌ DHAP + glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate ΔG°′ = +23.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (EC 4.1.2.13), a lyase; class I in animals — Schiff base, no metal

The chemistry: a Schiff base doing the work a carbonyl would otherwise have to do

Breaking a carbon–carbon bond leaves a carbanion, and a bare carbanion is far too unstable to form. Every aldol cleavage therefore needs an electron sink next door to absorb the pair of electrons as the bond goes. The C2 carbonyl could serve, and in the class II aldolases of fungi and bacteria a Zn²⁺ ion polarises it so that it does. Animal aldolase — class I — goes one better: an active-site lysine attacks the C2 ketone and forms a protonated Schiff base (an iminium ion), which is a much better electron sink than a neutral carbonyl because it is positively charged. The C3–C4 bond then breaks with the electrons flowing onto that nitrogen, giving a stabilised enamine that becomes DHAP when it is protonated and hydrolysed off the enzyme.

Two consequences follow that examiners like. Because animal aldolase works through a Schiff base, it is inactivated by sodium borohydride in the presence of substrate — the reagent reduces the iminium to a stable secondary amine and traps the enzyme covalently, which is how the mechanism was proved. And because it needs no metal, animal aldolase is not inhibited by EDTA, whereas the class II enzyme is. A stem that offers "chelator-sensitive" as a property of human aldolase is testing exactly this.

Energetics: the most instructive ΔG°′ in metabolism

ΔG°′ = +23.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ corresponds to K′eq ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁵. At 1 mol·L⁻¹ concentrations this reaction would sit almost entirely on the left; the enzyme is, after all, more usually named for the direction in which it condenses two trioses. What rescues it is that the cell operates nowhere near 1 mol·L⁻¹. Splitting one molecule into two makes ΔG concentration-dependent in a way that ordinary reactions are not: the mass-action ratio has two micromolar concentrations multiplied on top and only one underneath, so it collapses to something of order 10⁻⁴ and the RT ln Q term is worth about −24 kJ·mol⁻¹, cancelling ΔG°′ almost exactly.

Worked example 1 — showing that a +23.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ step is actually at equilibrium

In a human erythrocyte the steady-state concentrations are approximately [F1,6BP] = 31 µM, [DHAP] = 138 µM and [G3P] = 18.5 µM. Take T = 310 K and R = 8.315 × 10⁻³ kJ·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹. What is the actual ΔG of the aldolase reaction?

  1. Write the mass-action ratio with concentrations expressed in mol·L⁻¹, relative to the 1 mol·L⁻¹ standard state: Q = [DHAP][G3P][F1,6BP] = (1.38 × 10⁻⁴ × 1.85 × 10⁻⁵) / (3.1 × 10⁻⁵) = 8.2 × 10⁻⁵.
  2. RT = 8.315 × 10⁻³ × 310 = 2.578 kJ·mol⁻¹, and ln (8.2 × 10⁻⁵) = −9.40.
  3. RT ln Q = 2.578 × (−9.40) = −24.2 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  4. ΔG = +23.8 − 24.2 = −0.4 kJ·mol⁻¹. Essentially zero: the reaction is at equilibrium and will run whichever way the cell drains it.
  5. Sanity check on the physics. The reason Q is so small is not that the cell is doing anything clever, but that the products appear twice in the numerator. Halve every concentration and Q halves as well — dilution alone drives a one-into-two reaction forward. This is why gluconeogenesis can run the same enzyme backwards without any bypass at all.

Generalise it: ΔG°′ tells you where a reaction would sit in a 1 mol·L⁻¹ bottle, and no cell is a 1 mol·L⁻¹ bottle. When a stem gives you concentrations, it wants ΔG; when it gives you none, it can only be asking about ΔG°′.

Q = 8.2 × 10⁻⁵ · RT ln Q = −24.2 · ΔG ≈ −0.4 kJ·mol⁻¹

The trap on step 4 · "+23.8, therefore it runs backwards"

This is the single most reliable ΔG trap in the subject, and it is set from both sides. A stem may offer "the aldolase step is thermodynamically unfavourable and therefore requires ATP" — it requires no ATP and consumes none. Or it may offer "ΔG°′ is positive, so the cell must run this reaction in the gluconeogenic direction" — the cell runs it forward continuously, because triose phosphates are removed as fast as they appear. The rule to carry: ΔG°′ describes a bottle, ΔG describes a cell, and only ΔG decides direction. A related trap concerns the two products — DHAP is drawn first in most textbooks, which leads candidates to say it is the one that continues. It is not. Only G3P continues, and DHAP has to be converted into it.

Go deeper · the aldolase isozymes, and the one that causes disease on fruit

Humans express three aldolases from three genes, all class I and all cleaving fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, but differing in how well they handle fructose 1-phosphate:

  • Aldolase A — muscle and erythrocyte. Deficiency gives a haemolytic anaemia, sometimes with myopathy.
  • Aldolase B — liver, kidney cortex, small intestine. This is the only one with real activity toward fructose 1-phosphate, so it is the enzyme of the fructose pathway. Its deficiency is hereditary fructose intolerance, covered in note 04 § 06: fructose 1-phosphate accumulates, sequesters inorganic phosphate, and the cell can neither regenerate ATP nor run glycogen phosphorylase — which is why glucagon fails to raise blood glucose during an episode.
  • Aldolase C — brain.

The exam-relevant point is the contrast between the two directions the same chemistry can run. In glycolysis, aldolase cleaves a bisphosphate and both halves are tagged. In the fructose pathway it cleaves a monophosphate, fructose 1-phosphate, and only one half comes out tagged: DHAP is phosphorylated, glyceraldehyde is not, and the free glyceraldehyde then needs triose kinase and an ATP to catch up. That asymmetry is the reason fructose enters glycolysis below PFK-1 and therefore bypasses the pathway's only real control point — the metabolic argument behind the interest in dietary fructose.

§ 05

Step 5 · Triose phosphate isomerase — making the halves identical

The ideaThe two halves that came out of the cut are not the same molecule, and only one of them can go on. So the cell converts one into the other, and it does so faster than almost any enzyme known — fast enough that the two halves behave as a single interchangeable pool. That is what lets everything after this point be counted simply as "twice". It is also the step at which one half of the sugar is effectively turned back to front, and that single fact decides the answer to every carbon-labelling question in the topic.

dihydroxyacetone phosphate ⇌ glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate ΔG°′ = +7.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ triose phosphate isomerase (EC 5.3.1.1); no cofactor; catalytically perfect — limited only by diffusion

A catalytically perfect enzyme, and why that matters here

Triose phosphate isomerase is the standard example of catalytic perfection: its kcat/Km is around 10⁸ mol⁻¹·L·s⁻¹, which is the rate at which substrate and enzyme collide in solution. No further improvement to the chemistry could speed it up, because the chemistry is no longer what limits it. Evolution has finished with this enzyme.

That is not a decorative fact. The equilibrium of this reaction lies about 95 : 5 in favour of DHAP, so at any instant most of the upper half of the sugar is sitting in the wrong isomer. Glycolysis is unaffected only because the interconversion is so fast that the pool re-equilibrates instantly as GAPDH removes G3P. Slow the enzyme down and the pathway slows with it — which is precisely what TPI deficiency does.

The enzyme's speed also creates a hazard it must manage. The cis-enediolate intermediate, if it escapes the active site, eliminates phosphate and becomes methylglyoxal, a highly reactive dicarbonyl that glycates proteins and DNA. Triose phosphate isomerase carries a mobile loop that closes over the intermediate for the microseconds it exists. An enzyme this fast is partly fast in order to be safe.

Worked example 2 — recovering ΔG°′ from the equilibrium mixture, and vice versa

(a) At equilibrium, an isolated solution of triose phosphates is found to be 95.4 % DHAP and 4.6 % G3P. What is ΔG°′ at 298 K? (b) Going the other way, what percentage would you predict from the textbook value ΔG°′ = +7.5 kJ·mol⁻¹?

  1. The equilibrium constant is written products over reactants in the direction as drawn, DHAP → G3P: K′eq = [G3P][DHAP] = 4.6 / 95.4 = 0.048.
  2. ΔG°′ = −RT ln K′eq = −(2.478)(ln 0.048) = −(2.478)(−3.03) = +7.5 kJ·mol⁻¹. The textbook figure falls straight out.
  3. Reverse the calculation: K′eq = e−7.5/2.478 = e−3.03 = 0.049, so the fraction present as G3P is 0.049 / (1 + 0.049) = 4.6 %.
  4. Now the part that is actually being examined. Only about one triose phosphate in twenty is in the form the pathway can use — yet all six carbons of the glucose reach pyruvate. Nothing is stranded, because GAPDH continuously removes G3P and the isomerase, running at the diffusion limit, refills it from the DHAP pool. Equilibrium position tells you the ratio at rest; it tells you nothing about flux in an open system that is draining one side.

Useful constant to carry: at 298 K, RT = 2.478 kJ·mol⁻¹ and a factor of ten in K′eq is 5.7 kJ·mol⁻¹. Those two numbers convert any ΔG°′ on this page into an equilibrium position in about fifteen seconds.

(a) K′eq = 0.048 → ΔG°′ = +7.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ · (b) 4.6 % G3P at equilibrium

The trap on step 5 · "most of it is DHAP, so half the carbon is lost"

Two distractors are built on this step and both are about confusing equilibrium with flux. The first says that because equilibrium favours DHAP, only half — or worse, only 5 % — of the glucose carbon reaches pyruvate. All of it does: the yield of glycolysis is two pyruvate per glucose, not one. The second says DHAP is "a dead end" or "a waste product". It is neither; it is a fully productive intermediate that also happens to be the entry point for glycerol from fat and the exit point toward glycerol 3-phosphate for triacylglycerol synthesis. Then a factual trap worth knowing: TPI deficiency is the most severe of all the glycolytic enzymopathies — haemolytic anaemia from birth followed by progressive neuromuscular degeneration, usually fatal in childhood — whereas pyruvate kinase deficiency, five steps later, causes anaemia alone. The difference is the toxic dead-end metabolite, not the lost ATP.

§ 06

Step 6 · GAPDH — oxidation paid into a bond

The ideaHere the pathway finally starts earning. This one reaction does two things at once — it strips electrons off the sugar, and it attaches a phosphate picked up free from the surrounding fluid — and the trick is that it does both in the same active site, so the energy released by the first pays for the second. What comes out is a molecule holding its new phosphate so loosely that it can hand it straight to ADP. This is the only oxidation in the whole of glycolysis, and every NADH the pathway makes is made here.

G3P + Pi + NAD⁺ ⇌ 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + NADH + H⁺ ΔG°′ = +6.3 kJ·mol⁻¹ glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.1.12), an oxidoreductase; active-site Cys, NAD⁺

Why oxidise and phosphorylate in the same active site

Oxidising an aldehyde to a carboxylic acid releases a great deal of free energy — about 43 kJ·mol⁻¹ — and if the cell simply let that happen it would all leave as heat. The problem glycolysis has to solve is how to catch it. A phosphate cannot be attached to a carboxylate afterwards; the resulting acyl phosphate is far too high in energy to be built by an ordinary condensation. So the two events must be coupled, and they are coupled through a covalent intermediate.

The aldehyde adds to an active-site cysteine thiol, giving a thiohemiacetal. then takes a hydride from that carbon, and the product is a thioester — an already high-energy linkage, because sulfur's orbitals overlap poorly with carbon's and the ester has none of the resonance stabilisation an oxygen ester enjoys. The oxidation energy is now sitting in a bond rather than in the solvent. Inorganic phosphate then attacks the thioester carbon and displaces the enzyme's thiol, and the acyl group leaves as an acyl phosphate. Nothing was spent; the energy was simply passed along, from an oxidation, into a thioester, into a phosphoanhydride, and at step 7 into ATP.

What is examined here

That this is the only oxidation–reduction step of glycolysis, so all the NADH comes from it and the NAD⁺ that anaerobic metabolism must regenerate is the NAD⁺ this step consumed. That the phosphate incorporated comes from , not ATP — which is why glycolysis consumes inorganic phosphate and why a cell starved of Pi stops here. And that the sequence of intermediates is thiohemiacetal → thioester → acyl phosphate, in that order; questions often give the three in the wrong order and ask which is the oxidised species.

The trap on step 6 · arsenate does not inhibit anything

Note 04 § 02 sets this out and it is worth restating in its mechanistic home. Iodoacetate alkylates the active-site cysteine and stops the enzyme dead — a true inhibitor. Arsenate does the opposite of stopping it: it is accepted in place of phosphate, giving 1-arseno-3-phosphoglycerate, which hydrolyses spontaneously before any kinase can reach it. Glycolysis keeps running, NADH is still made, oxygen consumption is unaffected — and the ATP silently disappears. That is an uncoupler, and telling an uncoupler from an inhibitor is the discrimination the question is built on. Do not confuse arsenate with arsenite, which chelates lipoamide and poisons the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex — a mitochondrial lesion, not a glycolytic one.

Go deeper · the mechanism in five moves, and what each classical poison does to it
  1. Addition. The thiolate of the active-site cysteine attacks the C1 aldehyde carbon, giving a tetrahedral thiohemiacetal. Note that nothing has been oxidised yet — this is a nucleophilic addition.
  2. Hydride transfer. The C1 hydrogen leaves as a hydride to the nicotinamide ring of tightly bound NAD⁺, giving NADH. C1 is now a thioester carbon; the substrate has been oxidised, and the enzyme is holding the product covalently.
  3. Cofactor exchange. The NADH dissociates and a fresh NAD⁺ binds. This is the slow step, and it is why the enzyme's activity depends on the cytosolic NAD⁺/NADH ratio rather than merely on the presence of NAD⁺.
  4. Phosphorolysis. Inorganic phosphate attacks the thioester carbon; the enzyme thiolate leaves; the product is 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, an acyl phosphate.
  5. Release. The enzyme is regenerated unchanged, ready for the next aldehyde.

Now overlay the poisons. Iodoacetate hits move 1 — no thiolate, no reaction, and the pathway stops with triose phosphates accumulating. Arsenate hits move 4 — it is the same shape and charge as phosphate, forms the acyl arsenate, and that anhydride hydrolyses in water within seconds, delivering 3-phosphoglycerate directly and skipping the ATP of step 7 altogether. A useful mental test: an inhibitor makes the substrate pile up; an uncoupler lets the pathway run and steals the profit.

One more property of this enzyme that appears in stems: GAPDH is a classic moonlighting protein and a classic loading control in Western blotting, on the assumption that a housekeeping glycolytic enzyme is expressed at a constant level. That assumption fails in hypoxia and in tumours, where glycolytic enzymes are strongly induced — a small methodological point that occasionally becomes a question.

§ 07

Step 7 · Phosphoglycerate kinase — the first ATP

The ideaThis is where the first ATP of the pathway is actually made, and it is made with no membrane, no oxygen and no machinery: one molecule simply hands a phosphate to another, because it is holding it more loosely than will. Since everything after the split happens twice, this single reaction repays the entire cost of the first half of the pathway. The cell is now back where it started, with all the profit still to come.

1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + ADP ⇌ 3-phosphoglycerate + ATP ΔG°′ = −18.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ phosphoglycerate kinase (EC 2.7.2.3), Mg²⁺; substrate-level phosphorylation, 1 of 2

Why the pathway now breaks even, and why that is not the same as profit

Two ATP went out at steps 1 and 3, on the single six-carbon molecule. Two ATP come back here — one from each of the two three-carbon molecules that the cut produced. The ledger is level. Every ATP made from step 8 onwards is net gain, and since only step 10 remains as a producer, the entire net yield of glycolysis is the two ATP made by pyruvate kinase. It is worth saying that plainly, because it explains why pyruvate kinase and not phosphoglycerate kinase is the regulated one of the two.

Worked example 3 — a strongly negative ΔG°′ that still does not make a step irreversible

Compare phosphoglycerate kinase (ΔG°′ = −18.5 kJ·mol⁻¹) with pyruvate kinase (ΔG°′ = −31.4 kJ·mol⁻¹) at 298 K, where RT = 2.478 kJ·mol⁻¹. Compute both equilibrium constants, then explain why only the second reaction is irreversible in a cell.

  1. K′eq = e−ΔG°′/RT. For step 7: e18.5/2.478 = e7.47 ≈ 1.7 × 10³.
  2. For step 10: e31.4/2.478 = e12.67 ≈ 3.2 × 10⁵ — nearly two hundred times larger.
  3. Now the mass-action ratio. For step 7 the cell holds Q = [3-PG][ATP][1,3-BPG][ADP] very close to K′eq, because 1,3-BPG is kept vanishingly low (about 1 µM) while 3-PG sits around 60 µM and the ATP/ADP ratio is around 10. Q rises to meet K, and the measured cellular ΔG is within about a kilojoule of zero.
  4. For step 10 the same arithmetic does not close the gap: PEP is held low and pyruvate is drained away continuously, so Q stays orders of magnitude below K and the cellular ΔG remains near −17 kJ·mol⁻¹.
  5. The lesson to take into the exam: ΔG°′ ranks reactions; concentrations decide them. A step is irreversible in a cell when the cell holds the mass-action ratio far from equilibrium, and that is a statement about ΔG, never about ΔG°′ alone.
step 7 K′eq ≈ 1.7 × 10³, cellular ΔG ≈ 0 · step 10 K′eq ≈ 3.2 × 10⁵, cellular ΔG ≈ −17

The trap on step 7 · "it makes ATP, so it must be irreversible"

Both of the ATP-producing steps of glycolysis are offered as candidates for the "irreversible" list, and only one of them belongs there. Step 7 is reversible: it is the enzyme gluconeogenesis uses, running the other way, to spend an ATP putting the phosphate back onto 3-phosphoglycerate. Step 10 is not. The reason is transfer potential — 1,3-BPG's −49.3 kJ·mol⁻¹ is only 19 above ATP's −30.5, a gap ADP can pay back, whereas PEP's −61.9 is 31 above it and nothing in the cell can buy that phosphate back. A second, subtler trap is the enzyme's name. Phosphoglycerate kinase is named for the reaction it catalyses in the gluconeogenic direction, in which phosphoglycerate is the acceptor. In glycolysis it is doing the reverse of what its name describes, exactly as pyruvate kinase does.

§ 08

Step 8 · Phosphoglycerate mutase — moving the phosphate

The ideaNothing is gained or lost here. A phosphate simply moves from the end of a three-carbon molecule to the middle, and the free-energy change is almost nothing. The cell is repositioning it, because the next reaction can only build a high-energy compound if the phosphate is sitting on the carbon next to the carboxylate. This is the pathway thinking one move ahead, and it is the only step in glycolysis whose sole purpose is to set up the step after it.

3-phosphoglycerate ⇌ 2-phosphoglycerate ΔG°′ = +4.4 kJ·mol⁻¹ phosphoglycerate mutase (EC 5.4.2.11), an intramolecular transferase; catalytic 2,3-BPG required

Why the phosphate has to be on C2 before step 9

Enolase removes a water molecule across C2 and C3 to create a double bond between them. If the phosphate were still sitting on C3, the resulting compound would be an ordinary phosphate ester with a double bond somewhere else, and nothing would have been gained. With the phosphate on C2 the double bond forms directly between the phosphate-bearing carbon and C3, producing an enol phosphate — and an enol phosphate is special, because its hydrolysis liberates an enol that immediately tautomerises to a ketone. Step 8 costs the cell nothing and buys the whole of step 10. Nothing else in glycolysis has that character.

The trap on step 8 · "a mutase is an intramolecular transfer of the same group"

The definition is right in net terms and wrong in mechanism, and the question is usually written to catch exactly that gap. In the mammalian enzyme, the phosphoryl group that ends on C2 comes from the enzyme, and the one that was on C3 ends on the enzyme. If the stem describes a 32P-labelling experiment — label 3-phosphoglycerate at C3, run one turnover, ask where the label is — the answer is that it distributes, because the label enters the enzyme's histidine pool. The second trap is the 2,3-BPG requirement: it is catalytic, not stoichiometric. An option saying the reaction "consumes 2,3-BPG" or "produces 2,3-BPG as a product" is wrong; 2,3-BPG is made deliberately in the erythrocyte by a separate enzyme, and that is a different story with a different purpose.

Go deeper · 2,3-BPG, the shunt that costs an ATP on purpose

The red cell does something no other cell does with this part of the pathway. An enzyme called bisphosphoglycerate mutase converts 1,3-BPG into 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, and a phosphatase then converts that to 3-phosphoglycerate. The carbon rejoins glycolysis one step further on, but the phosphate that would have become ATP at step 7 has been hydrolysed away instead. This is the Rapoport–Luebering shunt, and it deliberately throws away ATP.

It does so because 2,3-BPG binds in the central cavity of deoxyhaemoglobin, between the two β chains, and stabilises the T state. Raising it shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right and unloads more oxygen in the tissues. The erythrocyte pays one ATP per triose to run its own oxygen-delivery control. Adaptations that raise 2,3-BPG are altitude, chronic anaemia, chronic lung disease and alkalosis; stored blood loses it within days, which is why old transfused red cells release oxygen poorly until they regenerate it.

Two examinable consequences follow. First, an arithmetic one: if every triose in a red cell went round the shunt, the net ATP yield of glycolysis would fall from two per glucose to zero — the two invested at steps 1 and 3 would be recovered only at step 10. Second, a diagnostic one: pyruvate kinase deficiency raises 2,3-BPG, because everything upstream of the block accumulates, and the resulting right shift is why these patients tolerate their anaemia better than the haemoglobin concentration suggests. Deficiency of hexokinase, at the top of the pathway, does the opposite: it lowers 2,3-BPG and those patients tolerate anaemia worse. Where the block sits decides which way the curve moves.

§ 09

Step 9 · Enolase — a water out, an enol phosphate in

The ideaThis step adds no energy at all. It removes a molecule of water — and yet what comes out of it is the highest-energy phosphate compound in the whole of metabolism. That is not a contradiction, and understanding why it is not is the most transferable idea on this page. The energy is not stored in the bond that was made. It is stored in what the molecule is desperate to turn into the moment that bond is broken.

2-phosphoglycerate ⇌ phosphoenolpyruvate + H₂O ΔG°′ = +7.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ enolase (EC 4.2.1.11), a hydro-lyase; two Mg²⁺ per active site; inhibited by fluoride

Where the energy of PEP actually lives

Hydrolysing PEP can be read as two events happening in succession. Breaking the phosphate ester itself is worth only about −16 kJ·mol⁻¹ — unremarkable, less than ATP. What follows is the remarkable part: the enolpyruvate released is the enol tautomer of pyruvate, and it converts to the keto form immediately and essentially completely, worth about −46 kJ·mol⁻¹ more. Add them and you get the quoted −61.9.

So the high transfer potential of PEP is not a property of the phosphorus–oxygen bond at all. It is a property of what the leaving group does next. The phosphate on C2 is holding pyruvate trapped in an unstable tautomer, like a catch holding a spring; take the catch off and the spring releases. This explains two things at once. It explains why glycolysis went to the trouble of steps 8 and 9 to create an enol phosphate rather than any other kind. And it explains why step 10 is irreversible — you cannot force a tautomerisation backwards by offering a phosphate, so pyruvate kinase can never be run in reverse no matter what the concentrations are.

The trap on step 9 · nothing here is "high-energy" until it is

Options that say enolase "adds a high-energy phosphate bond", "phosphorylates 2-phosphoglycerate" or "requires ATP" are all wrong for the same reason: enolase is a dehydratase and transfers no phosphate whatever. The molecule ends with exactly the phosphate it began with. The practical corollary is the one about blood tubes: fluoride inhibits enolase, which is why the grey-topped sodium-fluoride tube is used for glucose measurement — it stops the red cells in the sample from consuming the analyte. Fluoride does not inhibit hexokinase, and it does not inhibit GAPDH; if an option pairs fluoride with any enzyme other than enolase, it is wrong.

§ 10

Step 10 · Pyruvate kinase — the second ATP

The ideaThe last step collects the second and larger ATP payment, and it is the one reaction in glycolysis that could never be run backwards no matter what the cell did to the concentrations. The reason is not the size of the energy change but its nature: the product rearranges itself into a more stable form the instant it is released, and no amount of pushing will make it rearrange back. This is also the pathway's exit gate, and like the entry gate it is under tight control.

phosphoenolpyruvate + ADP → pyruvate + ATP ΔG°′ = −31.4 kJ·mol⁻¹ pyruvate kinase (EC 2.7.1.40), Mg²⁺ and K⁺; substrate-level phosphorylation, 2 of 2

Regulation, and why the exit gate is controlled at all

PFK-1 has already committed the molecule, so why control the last step too? Because a committed intermediate still has to be allowed out, and because the liver needs to be able to stop glycolysis without stopping the reactions upstream that gluconeogenesis also uses. Pyruvate kinase is activated by fructose 1,6-bisphosphate — the product of PFK-1, a feed-forward signal that opens the exit as soon as the entrance opens — and inhibited by ATP, , alanine and long-chain fatty acids, all of which mean "there is already plenty of fuel past this point". The liver L isoenzyme carries an extra layer: PKA phosphorylates and inactivates it in response to glucagon, so a fasting liver stops burning the very glucose it is trying to export. Muscle's M1 isoenzyme has no such site, which is exactly right — muscle has no business shutting down glycolysis because blood sugar is low. Note 04 § 03 gives the full regulator table.

What is examined here

That pyruvate kinase requires a monovalent cation, K⁺, as well as Mg²⁺ — it is the only glycolytic enzyme that does, and it is a favourite one-line fact. That the reaction is irreversible and therefore must be bypassed in gluconeogenesis by two enzymes, pyruvate carboxylase and PEP carboxykinase, at a cost of one ATP and one : three of the four gluconeogenic bypass enzymes exist to undo this single step and the PFK-1 step. And that pyruvate kinase deficiency is the second commonest enzymopathic haemolytic anaemia after G6PD deficiency, is autosomal recessive rather than X-linked, and produces a chronic non-spherocytic anaemia rather than episodic haemolysis.

The trap on step 10 · the name says the opposite of what happens

A kinase is named for the molecule that receives a phosphate. In glycolysis, pyruvate kinase takes a phosphate off PEP and gives it to ADP — the name describes the reverse reaction, which does not occur physiologically at all. Candidates who reason from the name conclude that this step consumes ATP, and lose two marks: one on the ledger and one on the direction. The related trap is the arithmetic: because this step runs twice per glucose, it yields 2 ATP, and with the 2 from step 7 that is 4 gross, 2 net. An option offering "net 4 ATP" is counting gross; an option offering "net 2 ATP and 2 NADH" is the correct ledger for the pathway as far as pyruvate.

Go deeper · four isozymes, and the one that tumours prefer

Two genes make four pyruvate kinases, and each is tuned to what its tissue is for.

  • L — liver. Allosteric, activated by F1,6BP, and the only one inactivated by PKA phosphorylation. It is switched off by glucagon so that hepatic gluconeogenesis is not immediately undone by hepatic glycolysis.
  • R — erythrocyte, from the same gene as L. Its deficiency is the classic pyruvate kinase deficiency: the red cell, with no mitochondria and no alternative ATP supply, cannot maintain its Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, becomes rigid and is destroyed in the spleen. 2,3-BPG accumulates upstream of the block and shifts oxygen delivery to the tissues, which is why symptoms are milder than the haemoglobin suggests.
  • M1 — skeletal muscle, heart, brain. Constitutively active, no phosphorylation site, no hormonal control. Muscle burns glucose when muscle needs to.
  • M2 — fetal tissue, and re-expressed in most tumours. It is a less active, more heavily regulated form, and cancer cells prefer it precisely because a partial block at the last step backs the pathway up and makes the upstream intermediates available for biosynthesis — nucleotides via the pentose phosphate pathway, serine from 3-phosphoglycerate, lipids from DHAP. This is one of the mechanisms behind the Warburg effect: a proliferating cell wants carbon skeletons more than it wants the last two ATP.

The general lesson is worth more than the list. Almost every glycolytic enzyme has tissue-specific isozymes — aldolase A, B and C; the muscle and brain forms of phosphoglycerate mutase, whose muscle deficiency is GSD X; β-enolase, whose deficiency is GSD XIII. What is confined to the regulated steps is isozyme-specific allosteric and hormonal control: elsewhere the isozymes differ only in kinetics, stability and which tissue expresses them, which is why hexokinase, PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase are the ones whose isozymes carry different regulators and phosphoglycerate mutase is not.

§ 11

The ten steps on one page

The ideaHaving gone through the ten reactions one at a time, the useful thing is to see them side by side, because the pattern only shows up in the column. Three of the ten reactions run far downhill inside a living cell; the other seven sit close to equilibrium there, including two whose standard numbers look large. That is not an accident of measurement — it is the shape of every regulated pathway in the body, and it is the reason the three enzymes worth memorising are the three that stand out in the column.

Read the deficiencies as one pattern, not ten facts

Every glycolytic deficiency in the table hits the erythrocyte hardest, and always for the same reason: a red cell has no mitochondria, no nucleus and no alternative ATP supply, so glycolysis is not one option among several but the whole of its energy metabolism. Muscle is hit second, and a muscle phenotype means the deficient isozyme is the muscle one. § 12

Memory hooks, in English

The ideaA memory hook is only worth carrying if it is faster than working the answer out. These are in English on purpose: the paper is in English, and a hook you have to translate under time pressure costs you the seconds it was supposed to save. Each one below says what it covers and, just as importantly, what it does not — and where something can be derived in a few seconds, there is a principle here instead of a rhyme, because a second thing to remember is a cost, not a help.

The eleven intermediates, in order

Goodness Gracious, Father Franklin Did Go By Picking Pumpkins To Prepare Pies

This is the standard circulating hook for glycolysis and it is the one to use, but check it before you trust it, because it carries twelve words for eleven intermediates. Goodness — glucose. Gracious — glucose 6-phosphate. Father — fructose 6-phosphate. Franklin — fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. Did — dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Go — glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. By — 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate. Picking — 3-phosphoglycerate. Pumpkins — 2-phosphoglycerate. To — nothing; it is grammatical glue and you must know that. Prepare — phosphoenolpyruvate. Pies — pyruvate. Three internal checks make it self-correcting. The two G words at the front are the two glucose species and the two F words are the two fructose species, so the preparatory phase is G G F F. Did Go are the two trioses, in the order they are conventionally drawn — DHAP as the upper half, G3P as the lower. And in Picking Pumpkins the numbers count down, 3-phosphoglycerate then 2-phosphoglycerate — which is the detail most people reverse. The line is standard; the observation that "To" is a spare word is mine, and if the spare word bothers you, trimming it to "Goodness Gracious, Father Franklin Did Go By Picking Pumpkins, Preparing Pies" gives eleven words for eleven intermediates — but that version is my adjustment, not the circulating one, so do not expect anyone else to recognise it. What the hook does not carry: the enzymes, the ΔG°′ values, which steps cost or make ATP, or the fact that everything from Did Go onwards happens twice. It is a list of names in order and nothing more.

The ten enzymes, in order

Hot Pans Produce Awful Toast; Grandma Keeps Making Extra Pancakes

Ten words for ten enzymes, and the semicolon falls exactly where the pathway changes character. Hot — hexokinase. Pans — phosphohexose isomerase. Produce — phosphofructokinase-1. Awful — aldolase. Toast — triose phosphate isomerase. That is the whole investment phase, and after the semicolon everything happens twice: Grandma — glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase. Keeps — phosphoglycerate kinase. Making — phosphoglycerate mutase. Extra — enolase. Pancakes — pyruvate kinase. The second half reads as a sentence about getting extra food, which is what the payoff phase is. Two of the pairs need a moment's care and both resolve by reasoning rather than rote. Pans and Produce both begin with P, but the order is forced: the carbonyl must move to C2 before C1 can be phosphorylated, so isomerase always precedes kinase. And Keeps Making puts the kinase before the mutase, which is also forced — you cannot move a phosphate that has not yet been cashed in. This one is my own, not a standard hook, so nobody else will know it. It does not tell you which enzymes are irreversible, which need Mg²⁺, or which make ATP — those are the next three hooks. It also silently uses the short name "phosphohexose isomerase"; some papers write "phosphoglucose isomerase" or "glucose 6-phosphate isomerase" for the same enzyme, and all three are acceptable.

The three irreversible steps

In at one, committed at three, out at ten

Steps 1, 3 and 10 — hexokinase, phosphofructokinase-1, pyruvate kinase — and the three-clause line encodes what each one is as well as where it sits. Step 1 is the entrance, and it is irreversible but not committed, because glucose 6-phosphate still has four exits. Step 3 is the committed and rate-limiting one, because fructose 1,6-bisphosphate has nowhere else to go. Step 10 is the exit. All three are kinases; all three are the regulated steps; all three are what gluconeogenesis has to bypass, and it needs four enzymes to bypass three steps because undoing step 10 takes two of them. Mine, and deliberately thin, because most of this is derivable and you should derive it rather than store it. The derivation: irreversible steps are the ones the cell holds far from equilibrium, which in glycolysis are the ones coupled to ATP except step 7. Step 7 is the exception precisely because 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate's transfer potential is only 19 kJ·mol⁻¹ above ATP's, a gap ADP can pay back, while PEP's is 31 above and nothing can. So the honest rule is: all three irreversible steps involve ATP, but not every ATP step is irreversible — and the one exception has a reason you can state. The hook does not cover the regulators of those three enzymes; note 04 § 03 shows why signing each molecule beats memorising a list.

The ATP ledger

Two to prime the pump, four from the two halves, two to keep

The costs are paid on the six-carbon molecule and the income is collected on the three-carbon ones — which is the whole reason the income doubles and the costs do not. One ATP out at step 1, one out at step 3, both on a single hexose: −2. One ATP in at step 7 and one at step 10, but the pathway below the aldolase runs twice: +4. Net +2 ATP, plus 2 from step 6 running twice. If you can say which molecule each transaction happens to, you never have to remember whether to double it. Mine. It covers free glucose under any conditions, and nothing else. It does not cover the glycogen-derived residue, which enters as glucose 1-phosphate and skips the hexokinase ATP, giving three net anaerobically; it does not cover the aerobic totals, which depend on which shuttle the tissue uses; and it does not cover the erythrocyte's 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate shunt, which sacrifices the step 7 ATP on purpose and can take the net as low as zero. Note 04 § 04 holds the first two; the 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate shunt is set out in § 08 of this page. All three are arithmetic, not memory.

Which step does which job — the three numbers worth knowing

Six oxidises; seven and ten pay

Step 6 is the only oxidation–reduction reaction in glycolysis, so every NADH the pathway makes comes from there, and the NAD⁺ that fermentation exists to regenerate is the NAD⁺ that step 6 consumed. Steps 7 and 10 are the two substrate-level phosphorylations — ATP made by direct transfer from a metabolite, with no membrane, no gradient and no oxygen involved. A neat cross-check comes free: the two steps that spend ATP are kinases named after the molecule being phosphorylated (hexokinase, phosphofructokinase), while the two that make it are kinases named after the molecule that receives the phosphate in the reverse direction (phosphoglycerate kinase, pyruvate kinase). Both of the earners therefore have names that describe the opposite of what glycolysis does with them. Mine. It does not tell you that step 7 is reversible while step 10 is not — that is the previous hook's job, and the two are constantly confused because both make ATP. It also says nothing about where the phosphate comes from at step 6: not ATP, but free inorganic phosphate, which is a favourite single-fact question and is written into the diagram in § 06 rather than into a rhyme.

Where a hook would cost you more than it saves

Three things in this topic are regularly turned into mnemonics and should not be. In each case the derivation is faster than the recall, and a wrong hook is worse than none.

  1. The allosteric regulators. Sign the molecule instead: AMP and ADP mean "short of energy" and activate; ATP means "plenty" and inhibits; citrate means "carbon already in surplus downstream" and inhibits; alanine and acetyl-CoA are pyruvate in disguise and inhibit pyruvate kinase. Only two facts do not sign, and they are the two worth rote — fructose 2,6-bisphosphate activates PFK-1, fructose 1,6-bisphosphate activates pyruvate kinase.
  2. The ΔG°′ values. Nobody is asked to recall +4.4 for step 8. What is asked is which steps sit far from equilibrium in the cell — 1, 3 and 10 — and whether a stem is asking about ΔG°′ or about ΔG. Note that the standard-state column does not pick those three out on its own: step 7 is −18.5, a larger negative than step 3, and step 4 is +23.8 yet still runs forward. That mismatch is the whole point. Learn which three are held from equilibrium and why; leave the digits in the table.
  3. The carbon map. Do not memorise which carbon becomes what. Write 1 2 3 | 4 5 6 in the margin, reverse the left half only, read down the columns, and confirm each pair sums to seven. The procedure takes about fifteen seconds, works for any label the examiner chooses, and cannot be misremembered. Note 04 § 05 sets it out as four pen strokes.
