Cells, Organelles, Membranes and Transport

One idea runs through this whole note: a cell is small because diffusion is slow, and it is divided into rooms because chemistry that would ruin itself has to be kept apart. Every organelle is the answer to one problem — an enzyme that needs acid, a reaction that makes bleach, a genome that must be edited before it is read. And because every room is walled by the same greasy sheet, the same short list of questions decides what gets in: is there a gradient, can it dissolve in oil, and if not, who is paying?

§ 01

Why cells are small, and why they are divided up

The ideaA cell has to get things from where they are made to where they are used, and its only free way of doing that is to let molecules wander. Wandering is fast over short distances and hopeless over long ones, so being small is not an accident — it is the price of not needing a delivery system. And once a cell is more than a bag of one reaction, it has a second problem: chemistry that would destroy other chemistry has to be kept somewhere else. Every organelle on this page is a room built to solve exactly one such problem.

Definition & the two scaling laws

Cell theory
Every organism is made of cells; the cell is the smallest unit with the properties of life; and every cell arises from a pre-existing cell (Virchow's omnis cellula e cellula, which is the part that killed spontaneous generation).
SA/V = 3/r (sphere) · 6/s (cube) — surface supplies, volume demands exchange scales with area, need scales with contents, and the two do not keep pace
Full definition, symbols and units
Surface-area-to-volume ratio
For any shape, doubling every linear dimension multiplies surface area by 4 and volume by 8, so the ratio halves. Growth therefore always makes a cell worse at exchanging with its surroundings, never better.
Diffusion time
The mean time for a molecule to random-walk a distance x scales with x², not with x. Ten times the distance is a hundred times the wait. This is the single most useful quantitative fact in cell biology.
Compartmentalisation
Enclosing a reaction in its own membrane lets the cell set the pH, the ion composition, the redox state and the enzyme complement of that volume independently of the cytosol — and lets it concentrate substrates that would be uselessly dilute if spread through the whole cell.

Take the two laws one at a time, because they answer two different questions and examiners like to swap them. The surface-to-volume law answers "how big can a cell be before it starves?" — the membrane is the only place where oxygen enters and waste leaves, so supply grows as r² while demand grows as r³. The diffusion law answers a different question: "how long until a molecule made here arrives there?" A cell can have a perfectly adequate surface and still be paralysed internally if it is too wide, because nothing arrives in time.

Cells that must be large cheat, and the cheats are worth recognising because they are the same three tricks every time. Flatten: a red blood cell is a biconcave disc, so no part of the cytoplasm is more than about a micrometre from the surface. Fold: intestinal and kidney tubule cells carry microvilli, multiplying the absorptive surface many times over without changing the volume. Divide the labour: a skeletal muscle fibre gives up on being one cell with one nucleus and becomes a syncytium with hundreds, each serving its own patch of cytoplasm. When a question describes an unusual cell shape, the answer is nearly always one of those three.

What this section actually gets asked as

Rarely as "state the cell theory". Usually as (i) a ratio calculation with a size change — note that the question may ask for the change in surface area (×4 when the edge doubles) or the change in the ratio (÷2), and these are different numbers; (ii) "why can't a cell simply grow larger?", where the expected answer is the exchange argument, not "the nucleus can't control it"; (iii) a cell-fractionation item — differential centrifugation pellets things in order of size and density, so nuclei come down first at low speed, then mitochondria and chloroplasts, then microsomes (fragmented ER) at very high speed, with the cytosol left in the final supernatant. Marker enzymes are the giveaway: catalase for peroxisomes, acid phosphatase for lysosomes, succinate dehydrogenase or cytochrome c oxidase for mitochondria, glucose-6-phosphatase for ER.

Worked example 1 — the size limit, in numbers

A roughly cubical cell has an edge of 4 µm. (a) Give its surface-area-to-volume ratio. (b) It grows until its edge is 8 µm — what happens to the surface area, the volume and the ratio? (c) Roughly how long does a small metabolite take to diffuse from the membrane to the centre in each case, taking D = 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²·s⁻¹? (d) What would that time be for a 1 mm cell?

  1. (a) SA = 6s² = 6 × 16 = 96 µm², V = s³ = 64 µm³, so SA/V = 96/64 = 1.5 µm⁻¹ — which is just 6/s = 6/4.
  2. (b) SA = 6 × 64 = 384 µm² — four times as much. V = 512 µm³ — eight times as much. SA/V = 384/512 = 0.75 µm⁻¹ — exactly half. Four times the surface sounds like an improvement until you notice that the volume it has to supply went up eight times, so the cell now has half as much surface per unit of demand as before. Write the ratio out rather than reasoning from the "×4" alone; that is the commonest slip in the whole topic.
  3. (c) Centre-to-surface distance is s/2. At 4 µm that is 2 µm: t = (2 × 10⁻⁶)² / (2 × 5 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 4 × 10⁻¹² / 10⁻⁹ = 4 × 10⁻³ s — 4 ms. At 8 µm the distance is 4 µm, so t = 16 × 10⁻¹² / 10⁻⁹ = 16 ms. Doubling the size quadrupled the delivery time while halving the delivery surface.
  4. (d) Half of 1 mm is 5 × 10⁻⁴ m: t = (5 × 10⁻⁴)² / 10⁻⁹ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ / 10⁻⁹ = 250 s ≈ 4 minutes for one molecule to reach the middle. A cell whose oxygen takes four minutes to arrive cannot respire at a useful rate: the interior is anoxic long before the supply gets there.
  5. The moral: the two penalties compound. Surface falls behind volume and the internal journey gets longer, and the second effect is quadratic. That is why unicellular giants are always either flat, thin, hollow, or full of inert storage — never solid spheres of active cytoplasm.

Exam technique · scaling questions, and the number they actually asked for

  1. Write the powers down before you read the options — for a linear scale factor k: surface ×k², volume ×k³, SA/V ÷k, diffusion time ×k². Doubling an edge therefore produces four different numbers — ×4, ×8, ÷2, ×4 — and the stem will offer you at least two of them.
  2. Underline the noun in the question — surface area, volume, ratio or time. The distractors on this item are overwhelmingly correct answers to one of the other three, so an option can be a perfectly true sentence about the same cell and still be wrong for this stem.
  3. Kill the linear options first, with no arithmetic — anything that scales diffusion time in proportion to distance dies on t ≈ x²/2D alone. That is usually one or two of five gone in five seconds, and under Part I's −0.25 marking a single elimination already makes answering strictly better than a blank.
  4. The five-second sanity check — for a cube, SA/V = 6/s. Edge 3 µm gives 2.0 µm⁻¹; edge 6 µm gives 1.0 µm⁻¹. If your reasoning produces anything other than exactly half, you have quoted the change in surface area where the ratio was asked for.
  5. There is no mnemonic in this section and you do not need one — everything here is generated by two sentences: area goes as the square while volume goes as the cube, and diffusion time goes as the square of distance. A hook would be a third thing to remember that adds nothing to those two, and it would decay faster than they do.
  6. Ten seconds, not sixty — recognise the shape, apply the powers, move on. Bank the time for § 09 and § 10, where the marks genuinely need arithmetic.

A textbook sentence that is true, and a follow-up that is not

"As a cell grows, its surface-area-to-volume ratio decreases" is correct and is worth a mark. The sentence often printed after it — "so cells cannot exceed about 100 µm" — is not correct, and it appears as a distractor. An ostrich egg is a single cell several centimetres across; Caulerpa, a green alga, is a single coenocytic cell up to a metre long; a human motor neuron reaches from the spinal cord to the foot. None of these break the physics. They evade it: the egg's cytoplasm is mostly inert yolk with the living cytoplasm in a thin disc, Caulerpa has thousands of nuclei and a streaming cytoplasm, and the neuron is a micrometre-wide thread with a motor-driven transport system. The correct general statement is that metabolically active, solid cytoplasm cannot be far from a surface — not that cells cannot be large.

§ 02

Prokaryote and eukaryote: what the distinction really claims

The ideaThe usual sentence is that prokaryotes have no nucleus and no organelles. The first half is right. The second half is a shorthand that will cost you a mark, because bacteria fold their membranes inwards, build enclosed reaction vessels, and have proteins that are recognisably the ancestors of actin and tubulin. What a prokaryote genuinely lacks is an envelope around its genome and a system of compartments served by vesicles. The difference is about traffic and address labels, not about being simple.

Definition & the real dividing line

Prokaryote
A cell whose genome sits in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid, with no surrounding membrane, and whose cytoplasm contains no compartment that exchanges material with others by budding and fusing vesicles. Bacteria and Archaea are both prokaryotic in this sense; they are otherwise about as different from each other as either is from us.
eukaryote = nuclear envelope + vesicle traffic + endomembrane sorting not "has organelles" and not "is bigger" — those are consequences, not the definition
Full definition, symbols and units
Nucleoid
The irregular region occupied by the bacterial chromosome — typically a single covalently closed circular DNA, supercoiled and organised by nucleoid-associated proteins, but not bounded by membrane and not separated from the ribosomes.
Plasmid
A small, separately replicating circular DNA carrying non-essential but often useful genes — antibiotic resistance, toxins, conjugation machinery. Not part of the chromosome, and copy number is regulated independently. Covered in detail in note 07.
Coupled transcription–translation
Because there is no nuclear envelope, a bacterial ribosome can begin translating an mRNA whose 3′ end is still being synthesised. In a eukaryote this is impossible, which is precisely why the envelope exists: it buys time for splicing, capping and editing before the message is read.

Work through the comparison thinking about why each difference exists rather than memorising the row. A bacterium has a single circular chromosome because it divides by simply pulling two copies apart — no spindle, no centromere, so no need for the linear chromosome architecture that mitosis requires (see note 02). It has operons because transcription and translation happen in the same place at the same time, so grouping genes that are needed together on one message is the cheapest possible regulation. It has almost no introns because there is no gap between transcription and translation in which to remove them.

The ribosome numbers, without doing arithmetic that does not work

bacteria count 3 – 5 – 7 · eukaryotic cytosol counts 4 – 6 – 8

Read each triple in tens of Svedbergs, and always in the order small subunit, large subunit, whole particle: bacteria 30S + 50S = 70S; eukaryotic cytosol 40S + 60S = 80S. Odd numbers for the prokaryote, even for the eukaryote. Because you are reciting a counting sequence rather than adding, the hook also inoculates you against the standard trap: S units do not add, since sedimentation depends on shape and compactness as well as mass, which is why 50 + 30 is not 80 and 60 + 40 is not 100. The odd/even counting trick is a common teaching device rather than my invention, though I am not attributing it to a particular source. It covers only the two cytosolic systems. The organelle numbers are handled two different ways and you should keep them apart: the chloroplast's 70S you derive — it is a domesticated cyanobacterium, so of course it runs a bacterial ribosome — whereas the mammalian mitoribosome's 55S is the one value on this page that no rule generates, because it drifted away from the bacterial pattern by shedding rRNA and gaining protein. Memorise 55S on its own; do not try to bend the hook around it.

Four sentences that look like textbook prose and are wrong

"Prokaryotic cells contain no internal membranes." They contain no membrane-bounded compartments served by vesicle traffic. Cyanobacteria are stuffed with thylakoid membranes; nitrifying bacteria have extensive intracytoplasmic membranes; the anammox bacteria have a genuine membrane-bounded organelle, the anammoxosome.

"Prokaryotes lack a cytoskeleton." FtsZ builds the division ring and is the ancestor of tubulin; MreB determines rod shape and is the ancestor of actin.

"Prokaryotic and eukaryotic flagella work the same way." One is a rotating propeller built of flagellin and turned by protons flowing down their gradient; the other is a bundle of microtubules bent by dynein burning ATP. Nothing is shared but the word.

"Archaea are a kind of bacterium." They are a separate domain. Their membrane lipids are ether-linked branched isoprenoids attached to glycerol of the opposite stereochemistry, and in the hyperthermophiles they form a covalently linked monolayer rather than a bilayer — which is exactly what you would build if you needed a membrane that survives at 100 °C.

Where the eukaryotic cell came from — the bit worth saying

Two separate events, and a question that mixes them up is easy to answer if you keep them apart. The nuclear envelope and endomembrane system are generally explained by infolding of the plasma membrane of an archaeal-type host — which is why the nuclear envelope is continuous with the ER and why its outer membrane carries ribosomes. The mitochondrion and chloroplast came from endosymbiosis, the engulfment of a free-living bacterium (§ 05). Only the second is endosymbiotic. An option claiming the nucleus arose by endosymbiosis is wrong, and it is a distractor that appears regularly.

§ 03

The nucleus, the ribosome and where a protein's address is decided

The ideaThe nucleus is not a safe; it is a room with doors that check credentials. Nothing much larger than a small protein gets in or out without carrying a label the doorkeepers recognise, and the reason for the room is that a message has to be finished and edited before anyone reads it. Ribosomes, meanwhile, are not committed to a location. Every one of them starts free in the cytosol, and it is the first few amino acids of the protein it happens to be making that decide whether it stays there or gets dragged onto the endoplasmic reticulum.

Definition & the addressing rule

Nuclear envelope
Two membranes with a perinuclear space between them. The outer membrane is continuous with the rough ER and carries ribosomes; the perinuclear space is therefore continuous with the ER lumen. The inner membrane is lined by the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of intermediate filaments (lamins A, B, C).
no signal → stays in cytosol · signal → goes somewhere the default destination is the cytosol; every other address is a positive instruction
Full definition, symbols and units
Nuclear pore complex (NPC)
An assembly of about 30 different nucleoporins in eightfold rotational symmetry, ~120 MDa in vertebrates. Molecules below roughly 40 kDa (a channel of ~9 nm) diffuse through freely; anything larger must be carried by a transport receptor reading a signal.
Nucleolus
Not an organelle in the membrane sense — it has no membrane. It is the region organised around the rRNA gene repeats, where 45S pre-rRNA is transcribed by RNA polymerase I, processed, and assembled with imported ribosomal proteins into 60S and 40S subunits. The subunits are exported separately and only meet on an mRNA in the cytosol.
Signal hypothesis
Blobel's proposal, now established: proteins destined for the secretory pathway carry an N-terminal hydrophobic signal peptide of 15–30 residues that is recognised by the signal recognition particle (SRP) as it emerges from the ribosome. SRP pauses elongation, docks on the SRP receptor of the ER, hands the ribosome to the Sec61 translocon, and translation resumes directly into the ER lumen. Signal peptidase clips the signal off.

Two features of the envelope repay attention because they turn up in questions that look like they are about something else. First, the continuity: outer nuclear membrane → ER membrane, perinuclear space → ER lumen. That is why a protein translocated into the ER is, topologically, already outside the cell (see § 04). Second, the lamina: it is made of intermediate filaments, it gives the nucleus its mechanical shape, and it is disassembled by phosphorylation at the start of mitosis and reassembled at the end — which is the mechanism behind nuclear envelope breakdown in note 02. Mutations in lamin A cause a family of diseases including Hutchinson–Gilford progeria, in which cells have visibly misshapen nuclei; that is a genuinely useful clinical hook because it links a structural protein to a visible phenotype.

Now the ribosome, and the point most easily missed. There is no such thing as a bound ribosome in the sense of a permanently attached one. The ribosomes on the rough ER and the ribosomes free in the cytosol are chemically identical and drawn from the same pool. A ribosome begins translating in the cytosol; if the emerging chain begins with a hydrophobic signal peptide, SRP grabs it and the whole ribosome–mRNA–nascent chain complex is delivered to the ER. If it does not, the ribosome finishes in the cytosol and the protein goes to the cytosol, the nucleus, a mitochondrion, a chloroplast or a peroxisome — all of which import their proteins after synthesis is complete.

Co-translational versus post-translational, and why it matters

Only one route is co-translational: entry into the ER. Everything else is post-translational, and the difference has a real consequence. A protein entering the ER threads through the Sec61 pore as an unfolded chain, because it never had a chance to fold. A protein entering a mitochondrion must be actively held unfolded by cytosolic Hsp70 chaperones so that it can thread through TOM and TIM23. But a protein entering a peroxisome goes in fully folded, and can even go in as an assembled oligomer — the Pex5 receptor carries it bodily across. That is unique among organelles and it is exactly the kind of detail a five-option question is built around.

Exam technique · targeting questions, read in the order the cell reads them

  1. One question first: where on the chain does the signal sit? N-terminal and hydrophobic → read as it emerges → co-translational → the ER and everything downstream of it. Anything else — C-terminal, internal, or a sugar — is read after the chain is finished, so it is post-translational. That single split settles most stems before you have looked at the options.
  2. Then match tag to compartment, and there are only five to match — C-terminal –SKL → peroxisome; C-terminal KDEL → fetched back to the ER; internal basic stretch → nucleus; N-terminal amphipathic presequence → mitochondrial matrix (a chloroplast transit peptide is also N-terminal, so let the organism in the stem decide between those two); a sugar rather than a peptide → lysosome.
  3. Elimination that costs no thought — exactly one route is co-translational. Any option pairing "co-translational" with the nucleus, a mitochondrion, a chloroplast or a peroxisome is dead on sight, as is any option calling mannose-6-phosphate a signal peptide. With five options, that is often two gone before you have thought about the biology.
  4. Let the folding state pick the compartment — if the stem says the protein enters folded, or as an assembled oligomer, the answer is the peroxisome; it is the only one that can. "Held unfolded by cytosolic chaperones" means a mitochondrion or a chloroplast. "Threaded through as it is made" means the ER.
  5. Check which of four questions was asked — which signal, which machinery, which compartment and what happens to the signal afterwards are four different questions off one table, and the distractors are usually true rows answering the wrong one. KDEL is the classic: it does not stop a protein leaving the ER, it only guarantees retrieval — so "prevents export from the ER" reads exactly like textbook prose and is not what happens.
Go deeper — how nuclear traffic knows which way is out

Import and export through the nuclear pore consume GTP, but not on the cargo — the pore itself is a passive sieve. Direction comes entirely from a single asymmetry: Ran-GTP is abundant inside the nucleus and essentially absent outside. That gradient is maintained by putting the two regulators on opposite sides. RCC1, the exchange factor that loads GTP onto Ran, is bound to chromatin and therefore only ever inside; RanGAP, which triggers hydrolysis to Ran-GDP, is anchored on the cytoplasmic face of the pore.

The consequences fall out immediately. An importin binds its NLS cargo in the cytosol, where there is no Ran-GTP to interfere, drags it through, and then meets Ran-GTP inside — which binds the importin and forces it to let go. Import therefore releases its cargo where Ran-GTP is, i.e. inside. An exportin does exactly the opposite: it only binds cargo when Ran-GTP is bound, so it can only load inside, and it is forced to release when RanGAP at the cytoplasmic face hydrolyses the GTP. One gradient, two opposite behaviours, no ATP touching the cargo at any point.

This is worth understanding rather than memorising because it is the same trick used everywhere in the cell: a nucleotide-state gradient sets a direction, and different proteins read it with opposite sign. The same logic reappears in vesicle coats (§ 04) and in the Rab GTPases that give each membrane its identity.

Three quiet errors

"Svedberg units add." They do not. 50S + 30S = 70S and 60S + 40S = 80S, and both look like arithmetic failures because they are: S measures how fast something sediments, which depends on shape and density as well as mass, and an assembled particle is more compact than its separated pieces. An option that says "the eukaryotic ribosome is 100S" is testing this.

"The nucleolus is a membrane-bound organelle." It has no membrane. Neither does the ribosome, the centriole, the proteasome or the cytoskeleton. "Which of these is not membrane-bound?" is a stock question and the nucleolus is the usual answer.

"Rough ER is rough because ribosomes are permanently attached to it." They cycle on and off continuously with each round of translation. Whether a given piece of ER looks rough at any moment depends on how much secretory protein the cell is currently making — which is why a plasma cell is almost solid rough ER and a steroid-secreting adrenal cell is almost solid smooth ER.

§ 04

The endomembrane system as a production line

The ideaPicture a factory. One end makes the parts and does the first quality check; the middle adds the finishing and sorts by destination; the far end packs and ships. Nothing walks between departments — everything travels in bubbles that pinch off one membrane and fuse with the next. That one sentence carries a surprising consequence: the inside of the ER, the inside of a vesicle, the inside of a lysosome and the outside of the cell are all the same space. A protein that entered the ER lumen has, in a real topological sense, already left the cell; everything after that is just deciding where it gets put down.

Definition & the route

Endomembrane system
The nuclear envelope, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles, transport vesicles and plasma membrane, connected either by direct physical continuity or by vesicle traffic. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are not part of it — they neither send nor receive vesicles. Peroxisomes are a borderline case: they can bud from the ER, but they do not participate in vesicle traffic thereafter.
ER →(COPII)→ cis Golgi → medial → trans → TGN → lysosome / secretory vesicle / plasma membrane and COPI runs the whole route backwards, retrieving anything that escaped
Full definition, symbols and units
Rough ER
Ribosome-studded cisternae. Site of co-translational translocation, N-linked glycosylation, disulphide bond formation and the first quality-control checkpoint. Its lumen is oxidising, which is why disulphides form there and essentially never in the reducing cytosol.
Smooth ER
No ribosomes. Site of phospholipid and steroid synthesis, of drug and toxin detoxification by the cytochrome P450 system, and of Ca²⁺ storage — the sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle is a specialised smooth ER. In liver it also holds glucose-6-phosphatase, so the last step of gluconeogenesis and of glycogen breakdown happens in the ER lumen, not the cytosol.
Golgi apparatus
A stack of flattened cisternae with a definite polarity: the cis face receives from the ER, the trans face ships out. Along the stack, glycans are trimmed and rebuilt, O-linked sugars and sulphate are added, and proproteins are cleaved. The trans-Golgi network is the sorting station.
Lysosome
A membrane sac at pH 4.5–5.0, holding ~50 acid hydrolases — proteases, nucleases, lipases, glycosidases, phosphatases. Its own membrane proteins are heavily glycosylated on the lumenal face to protect them from the contents.

Take the stations in order. In the rough ER, three things happen to a newly translocated chain: it is glycosylated on asparagines in the sequon N–X–S/T with a pre-made 14-sugar block; it forms disulphide bonds, catalysed by protein disulphide isomerase, which is possible only because the lumen is oxidising; and it is held by the chaperones calnexin and calreticulin until it folds. Proteins that fail are retro-translocated back to the cytosol, ubiquitinated and destroyed by the proteasome — ER-associated degradation. If misfolded protein accumulates faster than that, the cell triggers the unfolded protein response: slow down translation, make more chaperones, and if that fails, apoptose. The commonest mutation in cystic fibrosis, ΔF508 CFTR, produces a channel that would actually work but never leaves the ER, because quality control judges it misfolded and destroys it. That is a trafficking disease, not a channel-function disease, and questions like to make the distinction.

The Golgi is where the address is written. Its cisternae are chemically distinct: the enzyme set changes from cis to trans, and so does the pH. Two things happen that matter for the exam. Lysosomal enzymes are recognised in the cis Golgi by GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase, which reads a three-dimensional signal patch on the folded enzyme and phosphorylates a mannose — creating mannose-6-phosphate. In the trans-Golgi network, M6P receptors collect those enzymes into clathrin-coated vesicles and deliver them to the endosome, where the low pH makes the receptor let go and recycle. Lose the phosphotransferase and you get I-cell disease (mucolipidosis II): the enzymes are secreted into the blood instead, and the lysosomes fill with undigested material. The diagnostic finding — high serum lysosomal enzymes with empty lysosomal function — is a beautiful illustration of what an address label does.

Three coats, and which way each one runs

COP two, out the door · COP one, back for more · clathrin runs the last mile

COP two, out the door — COPII (assembled by Sar1) buds outwards from the ER, anterograde to the cis Golgi; if the rhyme ever slips, read the numeral as a homophone — COP-II is COP-to, to the Golgi. COP one, back for more — COPI (Arf1) runs the same road backwards, Golgi → ER and cisterna → cisterna, going back to fetch what should not have left, which is how a KDEL-bearing resident is retrieved. Clathrin runs the last mile — clathrin works the far end of the line, where cargo leaves the sorting stack for its final destination: at the trans-Golgi network with the AP1 adaptor for the lysosomal branch, and at the plasma membrane with AP2 for endocytosis. Two out, one back, and clathrin closes the loop. The numeral-to-direction pairing is standard teaching; the rhyme is mine. That pairing is the whole job, because it is the only genuinely arbitrary part — everything else about coats the figure already gives you: a coat deforms the membrane and selects the cargo in the same act, and it is shed immediately afterwards, because a coated vesicle cannot fuse. "The last mile" is a position cue for the secretory line, not a claim that clathrin is found nowhere else.

Worked example 2 — follow four proteins to their compartments

For each protein, state where it ends up and give the single feature that decides it. (a) A protein with an N-terminal hydrophobic 22-residue stretch and, at its C terminus, the sequence …KDEL. (b) A protein with the same N-terminal stretch but no C-terminal sequence, whose folded surface presents the signal patch recognised by GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase. (c) A protein with no N-terminal signal but with –SKL at its extreme C terminus. (d) A protein with no N-terminal signal and an internal PKKKRKV stretch.

  1. (a) The N-terminal hydrophobic stretch is a signal peptide, so SRP delivers it co-translationally to the ER. It travels with the bulk flow to the Golgi — but the KDEL receptor there binds it and COPI vesicles carry it back to the ER. Net result: an ER-resident soluble protein (BiP and PDI are the real examples). Note that KDEL does not stop it leaving; it only guarantees it is fetched back, which is why KDEL proteins are found in the Golgi too.
  2. (b) Same entry into the ER, but in the cis Golgi the phosphotransferase tags a mannose, producing M6P. The trans-Golgi network sorts it by that tag into a clathrin-coated vesicle bound for the endosome and hence the lysosome. The decisive feature is a post-translational sugar modification, not any part of the amino-acid sequence — which is why lysosomal targeting is the odd one out in the whole table.
  3. (c) No signal peptide means no ER, so translation finishes in the cytosol. –SKL is PTS1, read by Pex5, which carries the protein into the peroxisome already folded. Catalase is the standard example.
  4. (d) PKKKRKV is the classical SV40 nuclear localisation signal — basic, internal, not cleaved. Importin-α/β take it through the pore, and Ran-GTP inside makes them release it. Destination: nucleus. Because the signal is retained, the protein is re-imported after every mitosis, which is exactly what a histone or a transcription factor needs.
  5. The pattern worth taking away: where a signal sits tells you when it is read. N-terminal and hydrophobic → read as it emerges → co-translational → ER. Anything else → read after folding → post-translational.

The four addresses worked example 2 just walked through

Oily head, ER bed · SKL tail, peroxisome mail · sugar tag, lysosome bag · KDEL, return to sender

Oily head, ER bed — an N-terminal (the head of the chain) hydrophobic, oily signal peptide takes the ribosome to the rough ER, co-translationally, and everything after that is downstream sorting. SKL tail, peroxisome mail — a C-terminal (the tail) –Ser-Lys-Leu is PTS1, read by Pex5, into the peroxisome; and "mail" is the exact image, because uniquely this cargo is posted in folded, a finished parcel rather than a thread. Sugar tag, lysosome bag — mannose-6-phosphate, added in the cis Golgi, is the lysosome address, and it is the only address in the table not made of amino acids at all: a sugar stuck on afterwards, not a sequence written in. KDEL, return to sender — the odd one out, and deliberately last, because it is not a forward address but a return address: KDEL does not hold a protein in the ER, it calls it back from the Golgi by COPI, which is exactly why KDEL proteins are found in the Golgi too. My own rhyme; the four facts are standard. It deliberately covers only the four addresses that are arbitrary strings, and it leaves out the nuclear and organellar signals on purpose. Those need no hook: NLS in / NES out follows from the single fact that Ran-GTP is high inside the nucleus and absent outside, and the mitochondrial and chloroplast presequences are all just "N-terminal, and cleaved on arrival". Building a fifth and sixth line into the rhyme would cost you more than it returns.

Beyond the Golgi the line forks three ways, and the fork is worth naming. Constitutive secretion runs continuously and needs no signal — collagen, serum albumin and the steady replacement of plasma membrane all go this way. Regulated secretion stores product in dense granules that wait for a rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ before fusing — insulin, digestive zymogens, neurotransmitters. And the lysosomal branch is the M6P route. A cell type can be recognised from which branch dominates: an acinar pancreatic cell is nearly all regulated secretion, a fibroblast nearly all constitutive.

Lysosomes: the diseases that get asked, and the one that is not a lysosomal disease

A lysosomal storage disease is a missing hydrolase, so the substrate accumulates. Tay–Sachs — hexosaminidase A, GM2 ganglioside accumulates, cherry-red macula, no hepatosplenomegaly. Gaucher — glucocerebrosidase, the commonest of them, hepatosplenomegaly and bone crises. Pompe — acid α-glucosidase, glycogen accumulates in lysosomes, and note the trap: Pompe is a glycogen storage disease and a lysosomal one, unlike the other glycogenoses which are cytosolic enzyme defects. Hurler — α-L-iduronidase, mucopolysaccharide accumulation. I-cell disease is the odd one out: no hydrolase is missing, the tag is.

And the classic misconception: lysosomes do not carry out apoptosis. The old phrase "suicide bags" describes autolysis after death, not programmed cell death. Apoptosis is executed by caspases and is triggered from the mitochondrion (cytochrome c release) or from death receptors. Lysosomes do run autophagy: a double membrane wraps a piece of cytoplasm to form an autophagosome, which fuses with a lysosome. Chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine work partly by raising lysosomal pH and blocking that fusion step.

Go deeper — coats, SNAREs, Rabs, and how a vesicle knows where to go

Vesicle traffic solves three problems separately, and keeping them separate makes the machinery comprehensible. Budding is done by coat proteins that deform the membrane and select cargo at the same time: COPII (assembled by the GTPase Sar1) buds vesicles out of the ER, COPI (Arf1) buds retrograde vesicles from the Golgi back towards the ER, and clathrin with its adaptor complexes (AP1 at the TGN, AP2 at the plasma membrane) buds the lysosomal and endocytic vesicles. The coat is shed immediately after budding — a coated vesicle cannot fuse.

Targeting is done by Rab GTPases and their effectors. Each membrane compartment carries a characteristic set of active Rab proteins, which is effectively its postcode; tethering factors on the target membrane recognise the right Rab and reel the vesicle in. Fusion is done by SNAREs: a v-SNARE on the vesicle (VAMP/synaptobrevin) zips together with t-SNAREs on the target (syntaxin and SNAP-25), and the energy of that zipping pulls the two bilayers together until they merge. In regulated secretion, synaptotagmin acts as the Ca²⁺ sensor that releases the brake.

The clinical payoff is direct. Botulinum toxin is a protease that cleaves SNAP-25 or VAMP at the neuromuscular junction, so acetylcholine vesicles cannot fuse — flaccid paralysis, and the basis of its cosmetic and therapeutic use. Tetanus toxin cleaves the same class of target but is taken up and transported retrogradely into inhibitory interneurons of the spinal cord, blocking release of glycine and GABA — so the failure of inhibition produces spastic paralysis. Same molecular mechanism, opposite clinical picture, decided entirely by which neuron the toxin reaches.

One genuine controversy worth knowing exists: does cargo move through the Golgi in vesicles between static cisternae (vesicular transport), or do whole cisternae mature from cis to trans while enzymes are moved backwards by COPI (cisternal maturation)? The evidence now favours cisternal maturation, decisively for large cargo such as procollagen aggregates, which are far too big to fit in a vesicle yet still traverse the stack.

Endomembrane sentences that sound right

"The Golgi apparatus synthesises proteins." It modifies and sorts them. It does synthesise things — most of the cell's polysaccharides, including plant cell-wall pectins and hemicelluloses — but not proteins.

"Secretory proteins are made by free ribosomes and then imported into the ER." They are made on the ER, co-translationally: SRP delivers the ribosome to the translocon while the chain is still short. (A minor post-translational route into the ER does exist — very short proteins, and tail-anchored proteins whose anchor only emerges after the ribosome has finished — but that is not the "finish in the cytosol, then import it like a mitochondrial protein" picture the distractor is selling.)

"Plant cells have no lysosomes." Weak. Plants do the lytic job in the central vacuole, which contains acid hydrolases and is acidified by a V-type ATPase in exactly the same way. A better statement is that plants concentrate the function in one large compartment which also handles turgor, storage and pigment — a good example of one organelle solving several problems at once.

§ 05

Mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes — endosymbiosis and its limits

The ideaTwo of the cell's compartments look like captured bacteria, and a third looks like it should be one and is not. Telling them apart is really a lesson about what counts as evidence. A double membrane is not enough, because a membrane can be acquired simply by being wrapped. Dividing by fission is not enough, because plenty of things divide. What settles it is whether the compartment still carries its own genome and its own bacterial-style machinery for reading it — and whether it can only ever be made by copying an existing one.

Definition & the test

Endosymbiotic theory
Mitochondria descend from an engulfed α-proteobacterium and chloroplasts from an engulfed cyanobacterium, each retained rather than digested. Argued in its modern form by Lynn Margulis and now supported by sequence phylogeny rather than by morphology alone.
own circular DNA + bacterial-type ribosomes + double membrane + binary fission + never made de novo all five hold for mitochondria and chloroplasts; the first two fail for peroxisomes
Full definition, symbols and units
Mitochondrial compartments
Four of them, and questions test which is which. The outer membrane is permeable to anything below ~5 kDa because it is full of porin (VDAC). The intermembrane space therefore has essentially cytosolic small-solute composition — and holds cytochrome c, whose release starts apoptosis. The inner membrane is folded into cristae, is highly impermeable, contains cardiolipin and no cholesterol, and carries the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. The matrix holds the TCA cycle, β-oxidation, pyruvate dehydrogenase, mtDNA and mitoribosomes.
Chloroplast compartments
Three membranes and three spaces: outer, inner, and the separate thylakoid system. The stroma (between inner membrane and thylakoids) holds the Calvin cycle and the chloroplast genome; the thylakoid lumen is where protons are pumped. That third membrane is why a chloroplast can build a proton gradient without using its inner membrane for it — see note 09.
Peroxisome
A single-membrane compartment with no DNA and no ribosomes, containing oxidases that make hydrogen peroxide and catalase that destroys it. It grows by importing proteins and lipids and divides, but it can also arise de novo by budding from the ER.

Exam technique · "which observation most decisively…" is a stem type, not a hint

  1. Recognise the wording and change what you are looking for — most decisively, strongest evidence, best supports, most directly demonstrates. In this stem type several options are true statements and only one is diagnostic. Marking a true-but-weak option is the intended failure, so "is this sentence correct?" is the wrong test here.
  2. Apply the discriminator question to each true option — could the rival explanation also produce this? A double membrane can be acquired simply by being wrapped, and everything in a cell divides, so neither settles ancestry. A residual genome with its own bacterial-type ribosomes — and its corollary, that the organelle can never be built de novo — cannot be acquired any other way. That is the answer.
  3. Cheap eliminations specific to this section — anything resting on size proves nothing. Anything asserting an enzyme is unique to eukaryotes is usually simply false (catalase is widespread in bacteria), and a confident-sounding false statement is the easiest distractor to write. "The peroxisome has a single membrane" is true, is offered often, and is never the decisive answer.
  4. The picture-stem shortcut, worth ten seconds not sixty — single membrane + no DNA + catalase → peroxisome, and the answer to "is it endosymbiotic?" is no. Two membranes + circular DNA + bacterial-type ribosomes → mitochondrion or chloroplast, and a third internal membrane system decides it is the chloroplast.
  5. No mnemonic for the five criteria, and you should not build one — they are a single idea unfolded. If the compartment is a domesticated bacterium, it kept the bacterium's genome, the bacterium's ribosomes and the bacterium's way of dividing, and it cannot be rebuilt from the host's information alone. Run that one sentence forwards and the whole table writes itself — including, with no extra fact, why chloramphenicol inhibits mitochondrial and chloroplast translation while cycloheximide inhibits the cytosol's.

The limit of the theory — and the sentence students over-claim

Endosymbiosis does not mean a mitochondrion is self-sufficient. A human mitochondrion contains well over a thousand different proteins and its own genome encodes only 13 of them. Everything else — every TCA enzyme, every β-oxidation enzyme, most of the electron transport chain, the whole import machinery — is encoded in the nucleus, translated on cytosolic 80S ribosomes, and imported. Over evolutionary time the endosymbiont's genes were transferred to the host nucleus; what remains in mtDNA is mostly a handful of extremely hydrophobic inner-membrane proteins that are difficult to import. So "mitochondria are semi-autonomous" is right and "mitochondria replicate independently of the cell" is wrong. They divide when the cell tells them to.

Go deeper — mtDNA, heteroplasmy and why mitochondrial disease is strange

Human mitochondrial DNA is a 16 569 bp circle present in many copies per mitochondrion and many mitochondria per cell, so a cell carries hundreds to thousands of genomes. Four consequences follow, and all four are examinable.

Maternal inheritance. The sperm contributes essentially no mitochondria to the zygote — the few that enter are ubiquitinated and destroyed. So a mitochondrial mutation passes from an affected mother to all her children, sons and daughters alike, but an affected father transmits to none. That pedigree pattern is unmistakable and it is the fastest way to identify the mode of inheritance in a genetics question (note 03, classical and chromosomal genetics).

Heteroplasmy and the threshold effect. A cell can carry a mixture of mutant and wild-type genomes. Below some proportion of mutants the remaining normal genomes compensate and the tissue is clinically normal; above it, function collapses. This explains why the same mutation gives wildly different severity in different family members and different tissues, and why the tissues that fail first are the ones with the highest oxidative demand — brain, retina, cochlea, cardiac and skeletal muscle. Hence the disease names: mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, aminoglycoside-induced deafness.

A high mutation rate. mtDNA sits next to the electron transport chain, the cell's main source of reactive oxygen species; it has no histones and limited repair. Its mutation rate is roughly an order of magnitude above nuclear DNA, which is why it is useful for tracing recent human population history.

A bacterial translation system, with a price. Because mitochondrial ribosomes are bacterial-type, aminoglycoside antibiotics can inhibit them — and a particular 12S rRNA variant makes that inhibition severe enough to cause irreversible deafness at ordinary doses. It is a striking demonstration that the endosymbiotic origin is not merely a historical curiosity.

The peroxisome deserves its own paragraph because it is under-taught and reliably examined. Its logic is containment: the oxidases inside strip hydrogens from substrates and hand them to O₂, producing hydrogen peroxide, which would be damaging if released — so catalase, one of the fastest enzymes known, sits in the same compartment and destroys it. Its substrates are the ones mitochondria handle badly. Very-long-chain fatty acids (longer than C22) are shortened by peroxisomal β-oxidation and only then passed to mitochondria to finish; the first steps of plasmalogen synthesis, needed for myelin, happen there; so do the side-chain reactions of bile-acid synthesis and the detoxification of glyoxylate. The diseases follow the chemistry: Zellweger syndrome is a failure of peroxisome biogenesis (PEX mutations) with VLCFA accumulation and severe neurological disease; X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy is a failure of the ABCD1 transporter that imports VLCFA; primary hyperoxaluria type 1 is a mistargeted peroxisomal enzyme — alanine–glyoxylate aminotransferase ends up in mitochondria instead, and the patient forms oxalate stones. In germinating seeds the same organelle appears as the glyoxysome, running the glyoxylate cycle to convert stored fat into sugar (note 08).

Three peroxisome traps

"Catalase produces hydrogen peroxide." It destroys it; the oxidases produce it. "β-oxidation occurs only in mitochondria." It occurs in both, and very-long-chain fatty acids must start in the peroxisome — a fact that is also worth carrying into biochemistry note 07. "Peroxisomes have a double membrane." One membrane. If a question shows you an organelle with a single membrane, catalase and no DNA, it is a peroxisome, and the answer to "is it endosymbiotic?" is no.

§ 06

The cytoskeleton, motor proteins and the 9+2 axoneme

The ideaA cell is not a bag of soup. It is strung with ropes and rails that hold its shape, pull its chromosomes apart, drag cargo about and let it crawl. Three kinds of filament do this and they are not interchangeable. One is thin and does the pulling, with a motor that walks along it. One is thick, hollow and polar, and serves as both the railway and the mitotic spindle. The third is tough rope with no polarity and no motor at all, and its only job is to resist being torn.

Definition & the three filaments

Cytoskeleton
A dynamic network of protein polymers giving mechanical support, generating movement and organising the interior. In two of the three systems the polymer is polar — its two ends are chemically different — and that polarity is what allows directed transport.
microfilament 7 nm (actin) · intermediate filament 8–12 nm · microtubule 25 nm (tubulin) the diameters are asked directly; note the middle one is named for sitting between the other two
Full definition, symbols and units
Microfilament
Two twisted strands of polymerised globular actin (G-actin → F-actin). Polar: the fast-growing plus (barbed) end and the slow minus (pointed) end. ATP-bound actin adds at the plus end and hydrolyses, so filaments can treadmill — grow at one end and shrink at the other while staying the same length.
Intermediate filament
A rope of coiled-coil dimers assembled into antiparallel tetramers, so the filament has no polarity, binds no nucleotide, and has no motor. It is the most stable and the most tension-resistant of the three, and it is tissue-specific — which makes it a diagnostic tool.
Microtubule
A hollow tube of 13 protofilaments, each a stack of α/β-tubulin heterodimers, 25 nm across. Polar, with β-tubulin exposed at the plus end. Shows dynamic instability: an individual microtubule grows steadily while it keeps a GTP cap on its plus end and then shrinks catastrophically when the cap is lost.

Because each tissue expresses its own type, an antibody against the filament identifies the tissue of origin of a metastasis whose appearance gives nothing away: keratin means carcinoma, vimentin means sarcoma, GFAP means glioma, neurofilament means neural. It is the only place in this note where a structural protein is used as a diagnostic label, and it is worth the two minutes.

Now motors, where the reasoning is genuinely simple once you fix the geometry. In an animal cell, microtubules are nucleated at the centrosome, so their minus ends point inwards and their plus ends outwards towards the periphery. Kinesin walks to the plus end, therefore outwards — anterograde in an axon, from cell body to synapse. Dynein walks to the minus end, therefore inwards — retrograde, from synapse back to cell body. Once that is fixed, a whole set of facts falls out without extra memorising: newly made synaptic vesicles travel out by kinesin, worn-out material comes back by dynein, and so do the pathogens that exploit the route. Rabies virus, herpes simplex and tetanus toxin all reach the central nervous system by hitching a ride on retrograde dynein transport from a peripheral site.

Which motor walks which way

dynein dives in · kinesin kicks out

Dynein → minus end. Kinesin → plus end. The in/out half only follows once you have fixed the geometry the note just gave you: microtubules are nucleated at the centrosome, so their minus ends point inwards and their plus ends outwards. Hence dynein carries cargo inwards — retrograde, synapse to cell body — and kinesin carries it outwards, anterograde, cell body to synapse. Fix that and the consequences come free: new synaptic vesicles ride out on kinesin, worn-out material and the pathogens that exploit the route (rabies, herpes simplex, tetanus toxin) come back on dynein. A common circulating hook, and the note's own figure uses the same wording. Two limits. It encodes end-directionality; "in" and "out" are only true where minus ends point inwards, which is the standard animal-cell arrangement but not universal — dendritic microtubules are mixed in polarity. And it says nothing about the third motor: myosin walks along actin, and the ones you meet walk towards actin's plus (barbed) end.

Worked example 3 — counting an axoneme

From the cross-section above: (a) how many microtubules are in the axoneme? (b) How many tubulin protofilaments in total? (c) How many microtubules are in the basal body from which it grew? (d) A patient's cilia are immotile, and electron microscopy shows a normal 9+2 arrangement with the outer and inner arms missing. What is the diagnosis and what else would you expect to find?

  1. (a) Nine doublets are nine pairs, so 9 × 2 = 18, plus the two central singlets: 20 microtubules. The commonest error is to read "9+2" as eleven.
  2. (b) A complete microtubule has 13 protofilaments. In a doublet the A tubule is complete (13) and the B tubule is incomplete, sharing part of the A tubule's wall, so it contributes about 10. Doublets: 9 × (13 + 10) = 9 × 23 = 207. Central pair: 2 × 13 = 26. Total 233.
  3. (c) The basal body is structurally a centriole: nine triplets and no central pair — the "9+0" arrangement — so 9 × 3 = 27 microtubules. Note that the A tubule of each axonemal doublet is continuous with the A tubule of the basal-body triplet; the C tubule simply stops. That is why the count drops from 27 to 20 at the transition zone.
  4. (d) Missing dynein arms with everything else intact is primary ciliary dyskinesia. Because the same axoneme appears wherever cilia and flagella appear, the syndrome is systemic: chronic sinusitis and bronchiectasis (airway cilia cannot clear mucus), male infertility (sperm flagella cannot beat), reduced female fertility (oviduct cilia), and in about half of patients situs inversus — because the left–right axis of the embryo is set by the rotational beating of nodal cilia, and with no beat the axis is randomised. The triad of situs inversus, bronchiectasis and sinusitis is Kartagener syndrome. A question that gives you "immotile cilia" and asks why the heart is on the right is testing exactly this chain.

Exam technique · counting a cilium in twenty seconds

  1. Translate "9+2" before you do anything else — the nine are doublets, so they are 18 tubules, not 9. Axoneme = 9 × 2 + 2 = 20.   2. Kill "11" on sight — it is 9 + 2 read as arithmetic and it is the commonest distractor on this item. "18" is the second commonest: the doublets counted correctly and the central pair forgotten. Both die without any biology, which is one or two of five options gone for free.
  3. If the stem says "9+0", ask which 9+0 — nine triplets, no centre = basal body or centriole. Nine doublets, no centre and no dynein arms = primary cilium: non-motile, one per cell, a signalling antenna (Hedgehog, polycystin, the photoreceptor outer segment). Both are called 9+0 and an option that conflates them is wrong.
  4. Recognition rule — immotile cilia with a structurally normal 9+2 and the arms missing → dynein → primary ciliary dyskinesia. If a stem asks why the heart is on the right, this is the chain it wants and you should not look for a cardiac answer.
  5. Eliminate by structure, not by name — cilia and eukaryotic flagella are ultrastructurally identical, so any option asserting a structural difference between them is wrong before you finish reading it. The genuine structural contrast is with the bacterial flagellum: flagellin not tubulin, rotary not bending, proton-motive force not ATP.

"9+0" names two different things, and cilia versus flagella names almost nothing

A basal body or centriole is 9 triplets with no central pair. A primary cilium is 9 doublets with no central pair and no dynein arms — a single, non-motile, antenna-like projection present on almost every vertebrate cell, where it concentrates receptors for Hedgehog and other signals. Both are called "9+0" and they are not the same structure. Defects in the primary cilium cause autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (polycystin sits in it), Bardet–Biedl syndrome and several retinal dystrophies — the photoreceptor outer segment is a modified primary cilium.

Cilia and eukaryotic flagella have identical ultrastructure. They differ only in length, number and beat: cilia are short and many and row with a power-and-recovery stroke; flagella are long and few and propagate an undulating wave. An option claiming a structural difference between them is wrong. The genuine structural contrast is with the bacterial flagellum, which shares nothing — flagellin rather than tubulin, a rotary motor rather than a bending one, and the proton-motive force rather than ATP as the energy source.

Two more distinctions worth having ready

Colchicine versus taxol. Both are anti-microtubule and both arrest mitosis, by opposite mechanisms. Colchicine and the vinca alkaloids bind tubulin dimers and prevent polymerisation, so the spindle never forms. Taxol (paclitaxel) binds and stabilises assembled microtubules so they cannot depolymerise — and since anaphase requires shortening, the spindle is equally useless. "Which drug prevents microtubule disassembly?" is a stock item.

Centrioles are not required for mitosis. Plant cells have no centrioles and divide perfectly well, nucleating their spindles from dispersed MTOCs; so do most fungi. Centrioles are required for building cilia and flagella. That is why a plant question and an animal question about the spindle can have different correct answers.

§ 07

Outside the cell: matrix, wall and the four junctions

The ideaCells in a tissue are not merely packed together. They are glued, sealed, riveted and wired, and those are four separate jobs done by four separate structures. Once you decide which job a question is about, the answer follows: sealing means nothing leaks between the cells; riveting means the sheet survives being stretched; wiring means small molecules pass through, cytoplasm to cytoplasm, without ever entering the space outside.

Definition & the four jobs

Extracellular matrix (ECM)
The secreted network outside animal cells: fibrous proteins (collagen for tensile strength, elastin for recoil), adhesive glycoproteins (fibronectin, laminin) and a hydrated proteoglycan gel. It is not packing material — cells read it through integrins, and what it tells them changes their gene expression.
tight = seal · adherens = glue · desmosome = rivet · gap & plasmodesma = wire four jobs; the fifth structure (hemidesmosome) anchors the cell to the matrix rather than to a neighbour
Full definition, symbols and units
Tight junction (zonula occludens)
Rows of claudin and occludin in the two membranes press together to obliterate the intercellular space. It does two things: it blocks the paracellular route, and it acts as a fence in the plane of the membrane, keeping apical proteins apical and basolateral proteins basolateral.
Desmosome (macula adherens)
A spot weld. Desmoglein and desmocollin — cadherins, so Ca²⁺-dependent — bridge the gap and connect through a cytoplasmic plaque (desmoplakin) to intermediate filaments, distributing tension through the whole sheet rather than through individual membranes.
Gap junction
Six connexin subunits form a connexon; two connexons, one from each cell, dock to make a continuous aqueous pore of roughly 1.5–2 nm. It passes ions, sugars, amino acids and second messengers up to about 1 kDa, and excludes proteins and nucleic acids.
Plasmodesma
The plant equivalent in function only. A channel through the cell wall lined by continuous plasma membrane, with a strand of appressed ER (the desmotubule) running through the middle. Its exclusion limit is much larger and is actively regulated by callose deposition.

The order down the cell, and what each junction pulls on

TAD, top to bottom · A pulls actin, D pulls desm-type (intermediate) filaments

TAD, top to bottom — in a typical epithelium the junctional complex runs apical to basal in that fixed order: Tight, then Adherens, then Desmosome. Gap junctions are deliberately not in the word, because they are not in the sequence — they sit anywhere along the lateral membrane. A pulls actin — the initial does the work: the adherens junction anchors to actin through α/β-catenin. D pulls desm-type filaments — the desmosome anchors to intermediate filaments through desmoplakin, and the whole desm- family (desmoglein, desmocollin, desmoplakin, desmin) points the same way, since desmin is itself an intermediate filament. In an epithelium the filament in question is keratin. The hemidesmosome then needs no separate memorising: it is half a desmosome, so it keeps the desmosome's cytoskeleton (keratin) and swaps only the partner — basal lamina instead of a neighbouring cell. TAD is a common circulating device for the apical-to-basal order; the A/D initial pairing is my addition. Two things it does not encode. The tight junction also links to actin, via ZO proteins — the hook fixes the adherens-actin versus desmosome-IF contrast, which is the pair actually examined, and makes no exclusive claim. And it says nothing about function; for that the note's own line is already the better hook and needs no help from me — tight = seal, adherens = glue, desmosome = rivet, gap = wire.

Two points about tight junctions repay the effort. The first is that a tight junction is a selective seal, not an absolute one: different claudins give different permeabilities, so the proximal tubule of the kidney is deliberately leaky while the distal tubule and the bladder epithelium are tight. The second is the fence function, which is what makes vectorial transport possible at all. In an intestinal cell, SGLT1 is confined to the apical membrane and GLUT2 plus the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase to the basolateral membrane, and glucose therefore flows in one direction through the cell (§ 10). Without a fence keeping those proteins apart, the same cell would pump glucose in a circle. This is the clearest example on the page of a structure whose purpose only becomes visible once you know what happens downstream.

Two blistering diseases that distinguish two junctions

Pemphigus vulgaris: autoantibodies against desmoglein 3 dissolve the connections between keratinocytes, so the split is intraepidermal. The blisters are flaccid, rupture easily, and gentle lateral pressure extends them (Nikolsky sign positive). Mucosal involvement is usual. Bullous pemphigoid: autoantibodies against hemidesmosomal BP180/BP230 detach the whole epidermis from the dermis, so the split is subepidermal. The blisters are tense, do not rupture easily, Nikolsky is negative, mucosa is usually spared. One pair of diseases, one anatomical principle: attack the cell-to-cell rivet and cells come apart from each other; attack the cell-to-matrix rivet and the sheet comes off the floor.

Go deeper — integrins, and what the matrix tells a cell

Integrins are αβ heterodimers spanning the membrane, binding ECM ligands outside (fibronectin, laminin, collagen) and, through talin and vinculin, the actin cytoskeleton inside. Clusters of them form focal adhesions. They are the only receptor family on this page that transmits information in both directions: "outside-in" signalling tells the cell what it is attached to, and "inside-out" signalling lets the cell change its own adhesiveness — which is how a resting platelet's αIIbβ3 integrin switches to its fibrinogen-binding conformation only after the platelet is activated. (Abciximab blocks that integrin; that is why it is an antiplatelet drug.)

The outside-in half explains a fact that is otherwise arbitrary: most normal cells will not divide, and will eventually apoptose, unless they are anchored to an appropriate matrix. The phenomenon is called anchorage dependence, and the anchorage-triggered death is anoikis. Loss of both is characteristic of transformed cells, and the ability to grow in soft agar is a standard laboratory test for malignancy. It also explains why cancer cells that survive in the circulation have usually acquired a way to signal as if they were attached when they are not.

Mechanotransduction closes the loop. The stiffness of the matrix a cell sits on is read through integrins and changes its transcriptional programme — mesenchymal stem cells cultured on soft gels tend towards neural fates, on stiff ones towards bone. This is well past Campbell, but it is the sort of "beyond textbook" item that appears in a minority of cell-biology questions, and it makes the general point that the ECM is an instruction, not a scaffold.

Junction confusions worth pre-empting

"The tight junction is the strongest attachment." No — it is a seal, and mechanically it is the weakest of the three. The mechanical anchor is the desmosome, which is why skin, cardiac muscle and cervix are rich in them.

"Gap junctions and plasmodesmata are the same structure." They are analogous, not homologous. A gap junction is built from connexin proteins and creates a narrow protein-lined pore. A plasmodesma is a hole through the cell wall lined by continuous plasma membrane, with a desmotubule of ER threading it, and it can pass molecules far larger than 1 kDa. Nothing about their protein composition is shared.

"Cadherins bind their partners regardless of conditions." Cadherin adhesion is Ca²⁺-dependent — remove calcium and epithelial sheets fall apart, which is exactly how cells are dissociated for culture. Selectins and integrins also need divalent cations; the immunoglobulin superfamily adhesion molecules do not.

Finally, walls. A plant cell wall is layered: the middle lamella of pectin glues neighbours together; the primary wall of cellulose microfibrils in a hemicellulose and pectin matrix is laid down while the cell can still expand; and in some cells a secondary wall, often lignified, is added inside the primary wall once growth stops, giving compression strength to xylem and sclerenchyma. Fungal walls are chitin, bacterial walls are peptidoglycan, and animal cells have no wall at all — which is precisely why animal cells must control their own osmotic balance by pumping (§ 10) while a plant cell can simply let its wall take the strain (§ 09). The wall material is covered where it belongs: plants in note 08, microbes in note 07.

§ 08

The membrane itself: mosaic, fluid, asymmetric

The ideaA membrane is a two-dimensional liquid. The molecules that make it are not bonded to one another at all — they are simply refusing to have their oily ends next to water while insisting that their other ends stay wet, and that alone holds the sheet together. Because nothing is bonded, everything in the sheet can slide sideways. And because the two faces are built by different machinery and hardly ever swap places, the sheet has a definite inside and outside that the cell can rely on.

Definition & the model

Fluid mosaic model
Singer and Nicolson, 1972: proteins are embedded in and float within a fluid phospholipid bilayer, rather than coating it as a continuous protein layer. The "mosaic" is the patchwork of protein in lipid; the "fluid" is the lateral mobility of both.
the bilayer is held together by the hydrophobic effect — entropy, not lipid–lipid attraction which is why it self-seals, self-assembles, and cannot be "unzipped" at an edge
Full definition, symbols and units
Amphipathic
Having a polar head and a nonpolar tail in the same molecule. Placed in water, such molecules aggregate so that the tails are hidden — micelle if the molecule is cone-shaped with one tail, bilayer if it is cylindrical with two. Phospholipids are cylindrical, so they make sheets. The chemistry behind this is in biochemistry note 01.
Membrane protein classes
Integral proteins penetrate the hydrophobic core (transmembrane ones cross it, usually as α-helices of ~20 hydrophobic residues, or as β-barrels in bacterial outer membranes and mitochondrial porins) and can only be removed with detergent. Peripheral proteins sit on a face, bound by electrostatic and hydrogen-bond interactions, and wash off with high salt or a pH change. Lipid-anchored proteins are covalently attached to a lipid — GPI anchors on the outer face, prenyl or myristoyl groups on the inner.
Transition temperature (Tm)
The temperature at which the bilayer passes between a fluid, disordered liquid-crystalline state and an ordered gel state. It rises with chain length and with saturation, and falls with unsaturation.

Start with the fact that fixes the whole model: the lipids of a bilayer are not bonded to one another at all — no covalent bond, no ionic bond, nothing tying one phospholipid to the next. (The exceptions are about proteins rather than about the sheet: a lipid-anchored protein is covalently tied to its lipid, and some membrane proteins are disulphide-linked to each other.) The bilayer exists because burying the tails releases ordered water, and it is an entropic arrangement rather than a bonded structure (the argument is worked through in biochemistry note 01). Three consequences follow immediately, and each is examinable. A membrane self-seals: puncture it and it closes, because an exposed edge of hydrocarbon is intolerable. A membrane self-assembles: mix phospholipids with water and you get vesicles without any machinery. And a membrane is fluid: since the lipids are not bonded to one another, they and most proteins diffuse laterally at roughly a micrometre per second.

The two experiments this topic is examined through

Frye and Edidin, 1970. A mouse cell and a human cell were fused into a single heterokaryon, and their surface proteins labelled with differently coloured fluorescent antibodies. Within about 40 minutes at 37 °C the two colours were completely intermixed over the whole surface. Cooling the cells stopped the mixing. Conclusion: membrane proteins move laterally, and the movement is thermally driven diffusion, not transport.

Freeze-fracture. A frozen membrane fractured mechanically splits preferentially down the middle of the bilayer, between the two leaflets — because that is the plane with no bonds crossing it. The exposed faces are studded with particles, and those particles are integral proteins. This is the observation that killed the Davson–Danielli "protein sandwich" model, in which protein merely coated the outside of the lipid, and it is the direct structural evidence for the "mosaic" half of the fluid mosaic model. The two faces are named for what they belonged to: the P-face is the leaflet next to the protoplasm and carries most of the particles; the E-face is the exoplasmic leaflet and carries fewer.

What controls fluidity is the standard calculation-free reasoning question of this topic, so be precise about each factor and about the direction it pushes. Temperature: higher is more fluid, trivially. Unsaturation: a cis double bond puts a permanent kink in the tail, the tails cannot pack, so more unsaturation means more fluid and a lower Tm. Chain length: shorter tails have less surface in contact and fewer van der Waals interactions, so shorter means more fluid. These three are consistent and predictable, which makes the fourth factor the trap.

Cholesterol does not simply "make membranes less fluid"

Cholesterol is a rigid, flat, mostly hydrophobic ring system with a single hydroxyl. It inserts among the phospholipids with its OH at the level of the head groups and its rings alongside the first few carbons of the tails. From there it does two opposite things depending on which side of Tm you are on.

Above Tm (a warm, fluid membrane) the rings restrict the wagging of the neighbouring tails, so cholesterol decreases fluidity and reduces permeability to small solutes. Below Tm (a cold membrane trying to freeze) cholesterol physically gets in the way of the tails packing into an ordered gel, so it increases fluidity and prevents the phase transition.

The correct one-line description is therefore fluidity buffer, or "temperature buffer" — it flattens the response of the membrane to temperature in both directions. The distractor "cholesterol always makes the membrane more rigid" is exactly half the truth and appears constantly. Note also the parallel adaptation, homeoviscous adaptation: organisms that live cold, and the parts of a reindeer's leg nearest the ice, put more unsaturated fatty acids into their membranes so that the membrane is still fluid at low temperature. Bacteria do the same when the growth temperature drops.

Asymmetry is the second structural fact and it is under-taught. The two leaflets have different lipid compositions — phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin outside, phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylserine inside — and that asymmetry is actively maintained. Flippases move lipids inwards using ATP, floppases move them outwards using ATP, and scramblases collapse the asymmetry rapidly and without ATP when activated by Ca²⁺. The point of maintaining it is that phosphatidylserine on the outside becomes a signal. When a cell apoptoses, scramblase exposes PS on the outer face, and macrophages read it as an "eat me" tag — which is the basis of the annexin V assay for apoptosis. In platelets the same exposed PS provides the negatively charged surface on which the clotting factor complexes assemble. One structural asymmetry, two completely different physiological readouts.

Carbohydrate is asymmetric too, and necessarily so. Glycosylation happens in the ER and Golgi lumen, and by the topology rule of § 04 the lumenal face becomes the outer face. So the glycocalyx — the sugar coat of glycoproteins and glycolipids — always faces outwards, never into the cytosol. It mediates cell–cell recognition, protects the surface, and carries the ABO blood group antigens, which are differences in the terminal sugars of a common glycan core (A adds N-acetylgalactosamine, B adds galactose, O adds nothing).

Which lipid is on which face — read the head group, do not learn a list

Choline out, amine in — and every sugar out

Choline out — the two outer-leaflet lipids both carry a choline head: phosphatidylcholine, and sphingomyelin, whose head group is phosphocholine. Amine in — the two inner-leaflet lipids both carry an amine: phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylserine. So you are not holding four lipids in memory, you are asking one question about a head group. Every sugar out — and that third clause you derive rather than remember: glycosylation happens in the ER and Golgi lumen, and by the topology rule of § 04 a lumenal face becomes an outer face. Nothing in the system ever turns inside out. This is a chemical regularity dressed as a hook rather than an invented one, which is exactly why it is worth using — it cannot decay into a wrong order the way an arbitrary chant can. One lipid sits outside the rule and must be held separately: PIP₂ is inner-leaflet with an inositol head, not an amine, and it is inside because that is where signalling reads it. Keep hold of what the asymmetry is for: PS is normally hidden, so PS appearing on the outer face is a signal — "eat me" to a macrophage in apoptosis, and the negatively charged assembly surface for clotting factors on an activated platelet.

"Proteins float freely in the bilayer" — the model as usually stated is too loose

Singer and Nicolson were right that proteins are embedded and mobile, but the picture of free-floating icebergs is a simplification that current work does not support, and beyond-textbook items exploit this. Much of the protein in a real plasma membrane is restrained: by attachment to the cortical cytoskeleton, which divides the membrane into micrometre-scale "corrals"; by the tight-junction fence in polarised cells; by anchorage to the ECM; and by partitioning into lipid rafts — thicker, more ordered microdomains rich in cholesterol and sphingolipids, in which GPI-anchored proteins and certain receptors concentrate. Caveolae, the flask-shaped invaginations formed by caveolin, are a specialised version. The safest general statement is that the membrane is fluid but organised: mobility exists, but it is not uniform and not unrestricted.

§ 09

Passive transport: diffusion, osmosis and tonicity

The ideaNothing in this section costs the cell anything. Molecules move because they were already moving randomly, and random movement empties a crowded region into an empty one for the same reason that a shuffled deck does not stay sorted. So there are only three questions: is there a gradient, can the molecule dissolve in the greasy middle of the membrane, and if it cannot, is there a protein willing to let it through? Water is the special case, because water always has a gradient — you only have to remember that water's gradient runs opposite to the solute's.

Definition & the quantities

Simple diffusion
Net movement of a substance down its own concentration gradient, straight through the lipid bilayer, with no protein involved. It has no specificity, cannot be inhibited, and — the diagnostic property — does not saturate: the rate rises linearly with the gradient for as long as you care to raise it.
osmosis: water moves toward the lower water potential — i.e. toward the more concentrated solution only non-penetrating solutes count, which is the whole of tonicity
Full definition, symbols and units
Osmolarity
Total concentration of osmotically active particles, in osmol per litre of solution. A 1 mol·L⁻¹ solution of glucose is 1 osmol·L⁻¹; a 1 mol·L⁻¹ solution of NaCl is close to 2 osmol·L⁻¹ because it dissociates. Osmolality is the same quantity per kilogram of solvent — the two are interchangeable for dilute biological solutions, and clinical laboratories report osmolality.
Tonicity
Not a property of a solution on its own. Tonicity is defined relative to a particular cell and counts only the solutes that cannot cross that cell's membrane. A solution is hypotonic, isotonic or hypertonic according to whether the cell placed in it gains, keeps or loses water.
Facilitated diffusion
Passive movement down a gradient through a protein — a channel or a carrier. Because a protein is involved it is specific, saturable and inhibitable. It is still passive: no ATP, no movement against the gradient.
Water potential Ψ
The plant-physiology form of the same idea. Ψ = Ψs + Ψp, where Ψs is the solute (osmotic) potential — always ≤ 0 — and Ψp is pressure potential, positive in a turgid cell and negative in xylem under tension. Pure water at atmospheric pressure has Ψ = 0, and water always moves from higher Ψ to lower Ψ.

What crosses the bilayer unaided follows one rule — oil-solubility beats size — with one important qualification. Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂, and steroid hormones) cross fast, which is why gas exchange needs no transporter anywhere in the body and why steroid receptors are intracellular while peptide-hormone receptors are on the surface. Small uncharged polar molecules (water, urea, glycerol, ethanol) cross slowly but measurably. Large polar molecules (glucose, sucrose, amino acids, nucleotides) essentially do not. And ions are the worst of all, despite being the smallest — because an ion in water carries a tightly held hydration shell, and stripping that shell to enter the hydrocarbon core costs an enormous amount of energy. A bare Na⁺ is smaller than a glucose molecule and still crosses a protein-free bilayer many orders of magnitude more slowly. If a distractor says ions cross easily "because they are small", the reasoning is the giveaway.

Now osmosis, stated carefully because the sloppy version causes most of the errors. Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from the region of higher water potential to the region of lower water potential — equivalently, towards the more concentrated solution. The usual shorthand "water moves down its own concentration gradient" is not false, but it invites the fatal simplification that water follows total solute concentration. It does not. Water follows only the solutes that cannot cross, and that distinction is the whole of tonicity.

Worked example 4 — four solutions and a red cell

A red cell has an internal osmolarity of about 290 mOsm·L⁻¹. Calculate the osmolarity of each of the following and predict what the cell does in it. (a) 0.9 % (w/v) NaCl. (b) 0.45 % (w/v) NaCl. (c) 5 % (w/v) glucose. (d) 300 mmol·L⁻¹ urea. Take M(NaCl) = 58.44, M(glucose) = 180.16 g·mol⁻¹.

  1. (a) 0.9 % w/v = 9 g·L⁻¹. 9 / 58.44 = 0.154 mol·L⁻¹ = 154 mmol·L⁻¹. NaCl gives two particles, so osmolarity = 2 × 154 = 308 mOsm·L⁻¹. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ do not freely cross, so it is both isosmotic and isotonic: the cell is unchanged. This is normal saline, and the slight excess over 290 is why it is described as very mildly hypertonic in practice.
  2. (b) Half the concentration, so 2 × 77 = 154 mOsm·L⁻¹ — half-normal saline, clearly hypotonic. Water enters and the cell swells; at this degree of dilution red cells do begin to haemolyse.
  3. (c) 50 g·L⁻¹ ÷ 180.16 = 0.2775 mol·L⁻¹ = 278 mOsm·L⁻¹. In the test tube that is very slightly hypo-osmotic and the cell barely changes. In the patient it behaves as pure water, because the glucose is taken up and metabolised within minutes, leaving only the water behind — which is why 5 % dextrose is used to treat water deficit and is never used as a volume expander. Same solution, different verdict, decided entirely by whether the solute stays put.
  4. (d) Urea does not dissociate, so 300 mmol·L⁻¹ is 300 mOsm·L⁻¹ — essentially isosmotic with the cell. But urea crosses the red cell membrane through the urea transporter UT-B, so it rapidly equilibrates across the membrane and contributes nothing to the effective gradient. Once it has equilibrated, the cell is effectively sitting in water: it swells and lyses. The solution is isosmotic and hypotonic at the same time.
  5. The general rule to take away: compute osmolarity from chemistry, then ask separately, for each solute, "can it cross?" Only the ones that cannot contribute to tonicity. Any question that offers you a penetrating solute — urea, glycerol, ethanol, or glucose in a metabolising cell — is testing this and nothing else.
(a) 308 mOsm·L⁻¹, isotonic · (b) 154, hypotonic, swells · (c) 278, effectively free water in vivo · (d) 300 mOsm·L⁻¹ but hypotonic — lyses

Exam technique · osmolarity to tonicity, in two separate passes

  1. Pass one is chemistry and it is mechanical — a percentage (w/v) is grams per 100 mL, so ×10 gives g·L⁻¹; divide by the molar mass for mol·L⁻¹; multiply by the number of particles the solute dissociates into. Fifteen seconds, no thinking.
  2. Pass two is the half that carries the mark: for each solute, can it cross this cell's membrane? Strike out every solute that can, then compare what is left against ≈290 mOsm·L⁻¹. Only impermeant solutes contribute to tonicity, and a solution can be isosmotic and hypotonic at the same time.
  3. Recognition rule — if a stem offers urea, glycerol, ethanol, or glucose in a living cell, the examiner is testing isosmotic-versus-hypotonic and nothing else, and that option is usually the answer to "in which does the cell lyse?". Conversely, if every solute in the stem is impermeant, the item is pure arithmetic and deserves ten seconds, not sixty.
  4. The anchor that catches factor-of-two slips — 0.9 % NaCl is 308 mOsm·L⁻¹, always. Any NaCl answer of yours should scale cleanly off it: half-normal 154, twice-normal 616. If it does not, you have dropped either the dissociation factor or the ×10 from the percentage. Plasma is ≈290, so 308 is very slightly hypertonic and is still called isotonic.
  5. Direction check before you commit — hypotonic outside → water in → animal cell lyses, plant cell goes turgid. Hypertonic outside → water out → animal cell crenates, plant cell plasmolyses. An option giving the animal verdict for a plant stem, or the reverse, is the standard trap on this figure, and reading the grid across rather than down is what catches it.
  6. No mnemonic for hyper- and hypo-, and you do not need one — water chases the impermeant solute generates every cell of that grid, in both organisms, in both directions. A hook here would be a second thing to remember pointing at a sentence you already have.

Isosmotic is not isotonic, and three other slips

Isosmotic ≠ isotonic. Osmolarity is a property of a solution in a bottle. Tonicity is a property of a solution with respect to a given cell, and it counts only impermeant solutes. Two solutions of identical osmolarity can have opposite effects on the same cell, as worked example 4 shows.

"Facilitated diffusion is a form of active transport because it uses a protein." No. A protein is a doorway, not a pump. The test is direction relative to the electrochemical gradient, never the presence of protein, specificity, or saturation.

"Osmosis is diffusion of water, so it needs no membrane." Diffusion of water needs no membrane; osmosis is defined as occurring across a selectively permeable one. Without selectivity, the solute simply diffuses too and there is no net water movement.

"Water can only cross through aquaporins." Water crosses lipid bilayers slowly by simple diffusion — it is small and uncharged. Aquaporins raise the rate by orders of magnitude where high flux is needed (kidney collecting duct, red cell, plant tonoplast), and their absence reduces water permeability without abolishing it.

The last piece of the passive picture is facilitated diffusion, and the distinction that gets asked is channel versus carrier. A channel is a continuous, water-filled pore: it does not bind its substrate in a conformational cycle, it simply opens and lets things through, so it is fast — 10⁶ to 10⁸ ions per second — and it is usually gated by voltage, by a ligand, or by mechanical force. A carrier (uniporter) binds its substrate on one side, changes shape, and releases it on the other, so it is far slower — 10² to 10⁴ molecules per second — and, because binding is involved, it shows a Km, saturates at a Vmax, and can be competitively inhibited by structural analogues. That kinetic signature is exactly the signature of an enzyme, and questions often present transport data as a saturation curve to see whether you recognise it.

Two channels worth knowing individually

Aquaporins pass around 10⁹ water molecules per second per channel and yet exclude protons — which matters enormously, because a water wire that conducted protons would short out every proton gradient in the cell. They do it with a pair of asparagine–proline–alanine (NPA) motifs and a positive electrostatic centre that forces the passing water molecule to reorient, breaking the hydrogen-bond chain that a proton would hop along. AQP2 is the vasopressin-regulated aquaporin inserted into the collecting duct apical membrane; mutations in it, or in the vasopressin receptor, cause nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

The potassium channel passes K⁺ about 10 000 times better than Na⁺ — even though Na⁺ is smaller. The selectivity filter is lined with backbone carbonyl oxygens spaced to mimic exactly the hydration shell of K⁺, so K⁺ pays almost nothing to shed its water. Na⁺ is too small to contact all of them at once, so shedding its water is not repaid. Selectivity here is about fit and energetic compensation, not about a sieve — and "the channel is too small for the larger ion" is a distractor that gets the physics backwards.

§ 10

Active transport, and moving things too big to fit

The ideaTo move something against its gradient you have to pay, and there are only two ways to pay. Either you spend ATP directly on the pump, or you let something else fall down a gradient that a pump built earlier and take a ride on the way down. Everything called "secondary" is that second arrangement — which is why poisoning the sodium pump eventually stops glucose absorption in the gut, even though the glucose carrier never touches ATP. And for anything too large to fit through a protein at all, the cell does not transport it across the membrane at all: it wraps it.

Definition & the pump that pays for everything

Active transport
Movement of a solute against its electrochemical gradient, requiring an input of free energy. Primary active transport takes that energy directly from ATP hydrolysis on the transport protein itself; secondary active transport takes it from an ion gradient that primary transport established.
Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: 3 Na⁺ out · 2 K⁺ in · 1 ATP — net +1 charge exported per cycle unequal stoichiometry makes it electrogenic; the pump is not merely a housekeeper

Take the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase first, because almost everything else in animal physiology is downstream of it. Its mechanism is the defining P-type cycle. In the E1 state the pump faces inwards and has high affinity for Na⁺; it binds three of them, and ATP phosphorylates a specific aspartate residue. Phosphorylation drives the change to E2, which faces outwards and has low affinity for Na⁺ — so the three sodiums are released outside — and high affinity for K⁺. Two K⁺ bind, dephosphorylation follows, and the pump returns to E1, releasing the potassium inside. The affinity switch is the whole trick: the pump does not push ions, it changes its mind about which ion it likes on each side. "P-type" simply names the phosphorylated aspartate intermediate, which is what distinguishes this family from the V-type and F-type ATPases.

What does the cell buy with that ATP? Three separate things, and questions test all three. First, an energy store. The sodium gradient is a rechargeable battery that every secondary transporter in the cell draws on. Second, osmotic survival. A cell is full of impermeant protein and nucleic-acid anions; to balance their charge, cations accumulate, and by the Donnan effect the cell would gain solute, gain water, and burst. Continuously pumping Na⁺ out makes sodium behave as though it were an impermeant external solute, which balances the impermeant internal ones. That is why cells treated with ouabain swell — and it is a far better answer to "why does the pump matter?" than "to maintain the resting potential". Third, a small direct contribution to the membrane potential: because it exports a net positive charge, it hyperpolarises the cell — by only a few millivolts. The bulk of the resting potential is the potassium diffusion potential, and an option claiming the pump "generates the resting potential" is overstating it.

Digoxin, and why blocking a pump strengthens a heartbeat

Cardiac glycosides — digoxin, and ouabain in the laboratory — bind the extracellular K⁺ site of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and inhibit it. Follow the chain rather than memorising the answer. Less pumping → intracellular Na⁺ rises → the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX), which normally expels Ca²⁺ by importing Na⁺ down its gradient, now has a weaker gradient to work with → less Ca²⁺ is expelled → intracellular Ca²⁺ rises → more Ca²⁺ is loaded into the sarcoplasmic reticulum → each contraction is stronger. Positive inotropy from inhibiting a pump. Two riders make this a good exam item: hypokalaemia potentiates digoxin toxicity, because K⁺ competes with digoxin for the same site, and the therapeutic index is famously narrow.

Go deeper — the four ATPase families, and the one that is a channel

P-type pumps form a covalent phosphoaspartate intermediate during the cycle. The family includes the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, the plasma-membrane Ca²⁺-ATPase (PMCA), the sarcoplasmic-reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA, the target of thapsigargin), the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase that acidifies the stomach — inhibited irreversibly by omeprazole and the other proton-pump inhibitors — and the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase that energises plant and fungal cells the way the Na⁺ pump energises ours.

V-type pumps acidify compartments: lysosomes, endosomes, secretory granules, the plant vacuole, and the ruffled border of the osteoclast, which dissolves bone mineral with the acid it secretes. They are multi-subunit rotary machines with no phosphorylated intermediate, and bafilomycin inhibits them.

F-type is the same architecture running backwards: ATP synthase lets protons flow down their gradient and makes ATP instead of spending it — the subject of biochemistry note 06. The structural kinship between the V- and F-types is a good illustration that "pump" and "generator" are the same machine in two directions.

ABC transporters are defined by two ATP-binding cassettes and move an extraordinary range of substrates — lipids, peptides, drugs, bile salts. Three matter clinically. P-glycoprotein (MDR1) exports hydrophobic drugs from cells and is a major cause of multidrug resistance in tumours. TAP pumps peptides into the ER for loading onto MHC class I. And CFTR is the anomaly worth knowing: it belongs structurally to the ABC family, but it does not pump — ATP binding and hydrolysis gate it open as a chloride channel, so chloride then flows passively down its gradient. Cystic fibrosis is therefore a channel disease caused by a mutation in a transporter family, and the commonest allele, ΔF508, does not even reach the membrane (§ 04). If a question asks whether CFTR performs active transport, the answer is no.

Secondary active transport is the arrangement that makes the sodium gradient useful. A coupled carrier binds two things at once and can only complete its cycle when both are bound; the driver ion falls downhill and drags the cargo uphill. In a symporter both move the same way — SGLT1 in the intestine and kidney carries 2 Na⁺ inwards with each glucose, and NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb carries Na⁺, K⁺ and 2 Cl⁻ inwards together (this is what furosemide blocks). In an antiporter they move in opposite directions — NCX exports one Ca²⁺ for three Na⁺ entering; the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger expels acid on the way in. Plants and fungi run the identical scheme with a proton gradient instead of a sodium one, because their primary pump is an H⁺-ATPase; the sucrose–H⁺ symporter that loads phloem is the same logic in a different currency (note 08).

The coupling numbers that actually get asked

3 Na out, 2 K in · two tow the sugar · three toss the calcium · NKCC signs its own name

3 Na out, 2 K in — the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase sends 3 Na⁺ out and brings 2 K⁺ in per ATP, so it exports a net +1 charge per cycle and is therefore electrogenic. Two tow the sugar — SGLT1 carries 2 Na⁺ per glucose, and they tow it inwards with them, which is why it reaches a concentration ratio of ~28 000-fold where a 1:1 carrier manages only ~170-fold. Three toss the calcium — NCX lets 3 Na⁺ in to throw 1 Ca²⁺ out, and that is the step digoxin sabotages. NKCC signs its own name — the fourth needs no hook at all, because the name already spells the stoichiometry: Na⁺, K⁺ and — the doubled C — two Cl⁻ inwards together, the carrier furosemide blocks. My own compression; the numbers themselves are standard. Note what it does not encode: the direction each solute travels. Take that from the mechanism instead, where it is free — the driver ion always falls down its gradient, so Na⁺ always enters, and the cargo then moves the same way in a symporter and the opposite way in an antiporter. One more number sits outside the line: SGLT2 in the early proximal tubule is 1 Na⁺ : 1 glucose, deliberately weaker and lower-affinity, because it only has to shift bulk while SGLT1 downstream scavenges the last traces.

Worked example 5 — how far can the sodium gradient concentrate glucose?

An intestinal epithelial cell at 37 °C holds [Na⁺]out = 145 mmol·L⁻¹, [Na⁺]in = 12 mmol·L⁻¹, membrane potential −70 mV. (a) How much free energy is released when one mole of Na⁺ enters? (b) What is the maximum glucose concentration ratio (inside : outside) a 1 Na⁺ : 1 glucose symporter could sustain? (c) And a 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose symporter, as SGLT1 actually is? (d) Why does that answer explain oral rehydration therapy? Take R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, T = 310 K, F = 96 485 C·mol⁻¹.

  1. (a) Two terms. Chemical: RT ln(12/145) = 2.577 × ln(0.0828) = 2.577 × (−2.492) = −6.42 kJ·mol⁻¹. Electrical: zFΔψ = (+1)(96 485)(−0.070 V) = −6754 J·mol⁻¹ = −6.75 kJ·mol⁻¹. Total ΔG ≈ −13.2 kJ·mol⁻¹. Note how nearly equal the two halves are: dropping the electrical term would halve the answer, and that is the standard mistake.
  2. (b) At the limit, the energy released by the sodium exactly pays for the glucose: RT ln(ratio) = 13.2 kJ·mol⁻¹, so ln(ratio) = 13.18/2.577 = 5.11 and ratio = e5.11 ≈ 166. A one-to-one symporter could concentrate glucose roughly 170-fold.
  3. (c) With two sodiums the available energy doubles to −26.4 kJ·mol⁻¹, so the exponent doubles and the ratio squares: e10.22 ≈ 2.8 × 10⁴, about 28 000-fold. Doubling the coupling stoichiometry does not double the reach — it squares it. That is why the intestinal transporter evolved to use two Na⁺, and why the kidney's SGLT2, which only has to reabsorb the bulk of a large filtered load rather than scavenge the last traces, gets away with a 1:1 stoichiometry and lower affinity while SGLT1 in the late proximal tubule mops up.
  4. (d) SGLT1 only cycles when it has both Na⁺ and glucose bound. In cholera, the toxin locks adenylyl cyclase on and CFTR pours chloride, and therefore water, into the gut lumen — but it leaves SGLT1 entirely intact. Give a solution containing sodium and glucose together and the transporter still works, sodium is absorbed, and water follows it osmotically. Give sugar alone or salt alone and it does not. Oral rehydration solution is a direct application of coupled-transport stoichiometry, and it is one of the highest-impact pieces of physiology in medicine.

One picture that carries three sections: glucose across an intestinal cell

The apical membrane has SGLT1, a secondary active symporter, so glucose is pulled from the lumen into the cell even when lumenal glucose is low. The basolateral membrane has GLUT2, a passive carrier, so glucose leaves down its gradient into the blood. And the basolateral membrane also has the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which keeps intracellular Na⁺ low and therefore keeps SGLT1 running. The three of them together move glucose uphill overall, from a dilute lumen to a richer bloodstream. Two extra points make it exam-ready: the arrangement only works because the tight junction's fence function (§ 07) keeps SGLT1 apical and the others basolateral; and blocking the pump with ouabain abolishes glucose absorption within minutes, even though ouabain never touches SGLT1.

Finally, bulk transport, for cargo that will not fit through any protein. Exocytosis fuses a vesicle with the plasma membrane, delivering its contents outside and its membrane into the plasma membrane — which is how integral membrane proteins and the glycocalyx are installed, with the topology of § 04 guaranteeing that they end up the right way round. Endocytosis runs the reverse. It comes in three flavours that differ in selectivity, and the difference is the examinable part.

Cholesterol travels in the blood as LDL particles; cells that need cholesterol display LDL receptors, which cluster in clathrin-coated pits. Exam technique · "is this active transport?" — a three-question filter

  1. Question 1: which way, relative to the electrochemical gradient? Down it is passive, however elaborate the protein; against it is active. For an ion you must include the membrane potential — at −70 mV the electrical term for Na⁺ entry is about as large as the chemical one, so any argument built on concentrations alone has silently discarded half the driving force.
  2. Question 2: who paid, and where? ATP hydrolysed on the transport protein itself → primary. Riding an ion gradient that some other protein built earlier → secondary. "No ATP at the transporter" is not the same claim as "no ATP", and the distractor that secondary active transport does not require ATP lives in precisely that gap.
  3. Question 3: did the cargo actually cross the bilayer? If it was wrapped in a vesicle, it did not — it is still topologically outside the cell. That is why bulk transport is classified separately however much ATP it burns, and why "receptor-mediated endocytosis delivers the ligand into the cytosol" is wrong while sounding entirely reasonable.
  4. Three properties that decide nothing and are offered as though they did — uses a protein (channels and carriers are passive), saturates or shows a Km (that is binding, not energy), and moves the solute into the cell (direction is not part of the definition: PMCA, the ABC exporters and the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase all pump outwards). Each of these kills an option on sight, and one elimination is already enough to make answering better than a blank.
  5. The chain check on any "poison the pump" stem — inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and everything downstream of the sodium gradient fails on a delay, even though the poison never touches those proteins: ouabain abolishes intestinal glucose absorption without binding SGLT1, digoxin weakens NCX and so raises intracellular Ca²⁺. An option that denies a knock-on effect because "the drug does not bind that transporter" has missed what the stem is testing.
  6. Where the time goes — classification items in this section are ten-second recognition. Spend the minutes you save on the ΔG and concentration-ratio arithmetic, which is the only part of § 10 that can honestly eat a full 66 seconds.

The definitional trap, and three more

"Endocytosis is a form of active transport." It costs ATP, but the cargo never crosses the membrane — it is wrapped, so it stays topologically outside, in a vesicle lumen continuous with the extracellular world. That is why bulk transport is classified separately. An option saying "receptor-mediated endocytosis transports the ligand into the cytosol" is wrong, and it is a good question because it sounds entirely reasonable.

"Secondary active transport does not require ATP." It does not require ATP at the transporter, but it absolutely requires ATP upstream to maintain the gradient. Cut off ATP and secondary transport stops as soon as the gradient runs down. The precise statement is that the ATP is spent by a different protein at a different time.

"Active transport means transport into the cell." Direction is not part of the definition. The Ca²⁺-ATPase pumps outwards, ABC transporters export, and the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase secretes acid. Active means against the electrochemical gradient, whichever way that points.

"Exocytosis and endocytosis are independent." They must balance. A macrophage can internalise an area equal to its entire plasma membrane in well under an hour, so membrane is continuously recycled; a cell that only endocytosed would shrink its surface away within minutes. This is why regulated secretion at a synapse is followed immediately by compensatory retrieval of the vesicle membrane.

Signalling — receptors, G proteins, second messengers and the amplification cascades — belongs to this same block of the syllabus but is treated in full in biochemistry note 09, and the lipid chemistry underlying membrane behaviour is in biochemistry note 01. Read those two alongside this note rather than after it; the biology half and the biochemistry half of this paper ask about the same membrane from opposite ends, and the overlap is free marks.
