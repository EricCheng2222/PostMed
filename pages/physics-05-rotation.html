Physics · Note 05

Rotational Motion and Static Equilibrium

There is almost nothing new in this chapter. Every law of rotation is a law you already know with six substitutions: x→θ, v→ω, a→α, m→I, F→τ, p→L. The one genuinely new piece of physics is I — the discovery that for rotation it matters not only how much mass a body has but how far from the axis that mass sits. Hold that, and the hoop losing to the sphere, the skater speeding up, and the biceps needing 400 N to hold 50 N all become consequences rather than facts.

§ 01

Angular kinematics: 1-D motion in a new alphabet

Definition & formula

Angular position θ
The angle between a line painted on the rotating body and a fixed reference direction, measured as arc length divided by radius. That ratio is the radian.
Angular velocity ω
The rate at which that angle changes. It is a property of the whole rigid body, not of a point on it.
Angular acceleration α
The rate at which ω changes — the rotational counterpart of a, and equally a signed quantity.
θ = s / r   (1 rev = 2π rad = 360°) definition of the radian; s measured along the arc
ω = Δθ / Δt  ·  α = Δω / Δtaverage values; instantaneous = the Δt → 0 limit
ω = ω₀ + αtconstant α only
Δθ = ω₀t + ½αt²constant α only
ω² = ω₀² + 2αΔθconstant α only; no t
Δθ = ½(ω₀ + ω)tconstant α only; no α
θangular position / displacement (rad)
ωangular velocity (rad·s⁻¹)
αangular acceleration (rad·s⁻²)
sarc length swept by a point (m)
rdistance of that point from the axis (m)
telapsed time (s)

A rigid body turning about a fixed axis has exactly one degree of freedom: give me θ(t) and I can locate every atom in it. That is why rotational kinematics is not a new subject — it is one-dimensional kinematics with θ where x used to be. The four constant-α equations are not four new formulas to memorise; they are the four you already carry, retyped.

Linear (constant a)Angular (constant α)Variable that is missingUse it when…

Sign convention — settle it before you write anything

Choose counter-clockwise as positive and stay with it for the whole problem. Formally the direction is set by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the body turns and your thumb points along the ω vector, out of the page for counter-clockwise. In every plane problem this reduces to a single sign, exactly as ± did in 1-D motion.

Trap · "α is negative" ≠ "it is slowing down"

A spinning body slows only when ω and α have opposite signs. A wheel with ω = −6 rad·s⁻¹ and α = −2 rad·s⁻² is speeding up (clockwise, faster and faster). This is the same trap as "negative acceleration means deceleration" in linear motion, and it is examined in exactly the same way.

Worked example 1 · Spinning up a clinical centrifuge

A rotor starts from rest and reaches 4000 rpm in 20 s with constant angular acceleration. Find α, and the number of revolutions made during the spin-up.

  1. Convert to SI. ω = 4000 rev·min⁻¹ × (2π rad/rev) ÷ (60 s/min) = 4000 × 0.10472 = 418.9 rad·s⁻¹. Never leave rpm in a formula.
  2. α from ω = ω₀ + αt with ω₀ = 0: α = 418.9 / 20 = 20.9 rad·s⁻².
  3. Angle from Δθ = ½(ω₀ + ω)t — no need for α at all: Δθ = ½(0 + 418.9)(20) = 4189 rad.
  4. Back to revolutions. 4189 / 2π = 666.7 rev.
  5. Sanity check. At a steady 4000 rpm it would make 4000/60 × 20 = 1333 rev in 20 s; starting from rest and ramping linearly it should make exactly half of that — 667 rev. ✓
α ≈ 20.9 rad·s⁻² · Δθ ≈ 4.19 × 10³ rad ≈ 6.7 × 10² revolutions

The whole chapter in one line

x→θ, v→ω, a→α, m→I, F→τ, p→L. Learn the six swaps and you have already read most of this page.

§ 02

The linear–angular bridge, and why radians

Definition & formula

Tangential (linear) speed v
The speed with which a particular point of the rotating body moves through space — proportional to its distance from the axis.
Tangential acceleration at
The component of a point's acceleration along its direction of travel; it exists only when the body's rotation rate is changing.
Centripetal acceleration ac
The component pointing at the axis; it exists whenever the body is rotating at all, even at constant ω.
v = rω ω in rad·s⁻¹; r measured ⟂ from the axis
s = rθθ in radians
at = rαzero when ω is constant
ac = rω² = v² / rnever zero while the body turns
a = √(at² + ac²)the two components are perpendicular
vtangential speed of one point (m·s⁻¹)
attangential acceleration (m·s⁻²)
accentripetal acceleration (m·s⁻²)
atotal linear acceleration of the point (m·s⁻²)
rperpendicular distance from the axis (m)
ω, αrad·s⁻¹, rad·s⁻² — the same for every point

These four relations are the entire connection between the rotational world and the linear one. The structural fact behind all of them: ω and α belong to the body, v and a belong to a point. Two children on a roundabout share ω exactly; the one at the rim is moving twice as fast as the one at half the radius, and feels twice the centripetal acceleration. Every "which quantity is the same for both points?" exam question is testing this one sentence.

Trap · degrees will silently destroy your answer

s = rθ, v = rω and at = rα hold only in radians, because the radian is defined as s/r. Feed in degrees and every one of them comes out 180/π ≈ 57.3 times too large. This is also why "rad" never breaks a unit check: it is a ratio of two metres, i.e. dimensionless, so m × rad·s⁻¹ correctly gives m·s⁻¹.

Worked example 2 · The g-force inside that centrifuge

Same rotor at its working speed of 4000 rpm (ω = 418.9 rad·s⁻¹). A blood sample sits at r = 8.0 cm from the axis. Find its tangential speed and its centripetal acceleration, expressed as a multiple of g.

  1. Tangential speed. v = rω = 0.080 × 418.9 = 33.5 m·s⁻¹ — about 120 km·h⁻¹.
  2. Centripetal acceleration. ac = rω² = 0.080 × (418.9)² = 0.080 × 1.755 × 10⁵ = 1.40 × 10⁴ m·s⁻².
  3. As a multiple of g. 1.40 × 10⁴ / 9.8 = 1.43 × 10³.
  4. Read it physically. This "relative centrifugal field" of ≈1.4 × 10³ g is what makes erythrocytes, buffy coat and plasma separate in minutes instead of days — sedimentation rate scales with the effective gravitational field. Note it goes as ω², so doubling the rotor speed quadruples the separation force, while doubling the radius only doubles it.
v ≈ 33.5 m·s⁻¹ · ac ≈ 1.4 × 10⁴ m·s⁻² ≈ 1.4 × 10³ g
§ 03

Torque: force that has found a lever arm

Definition & formula

Torque (moment) τ
The turning effect of a force about a chosen axis: the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action.
Lever arm r⊥
The shortest (perpendicular) distance from the axis to the infinite line along which the force acts — not the distance to the point where the force is applied.
τ = r F sin θ = r⊥ F = r F⊥ θ = angle between r and F; axis must be named first
r⊥ = r sin θ  ·  F⊥ = F sin θtwo readings of the same product
Στ = τ₁ + τ₂ + …  (signed sum)counter-clockwise +, clockwise −
τ = r × Fvector form; direction by the right-hand rule
τtorque about the stated axis (N·m)
rdistance from axis to the point of application (m)
Fapplied force (N)
θangle between r and F (rad or °)
r⊥lever arm / moment arm (m)
F⊥component of F perpendicular to r (N)

Force alone does not tell you whether something will turn. Push on a door at its hinge, however hard, and nothing happens; the same push at the handle swings it open. The quantity that decides is the product of force and lever arm, and the sin θ in the formula is doing exactly one job: it strips away the component of the force that points along r, because that component only pulls the body toward or away from the axis, where the axle absorbs it.

Choosing the sign, and reading the special angles

θ = 90° gives the maximum torque rF — this is why you push a spanner at right angles and why a muscle acting perpendicular to a bone is at its most efficient. θ = 0° or 180° gives zero torque: the line of action passes straight through the axis, so r⊥ = 0. Every force whose line of action goes through your chosen axis drops out of the torque equation — and that is the fact you will exploit relentlessly in §09.

Trap · N·m is not a joule

Torque and work have identical dimensions and you must still never write torque in joules. Work is force times displacement along the force; torque is force times distance perpendicular to the force. They are different physical objects that happen to share a unit combination. Torque: N·m. Work: J. Rotational work does convert one to the other, W = τΔθ, because the radian is dimensionless.

Exam point

"Torque" is meaningless until you name the axis. The same force has a different torque about the elbow, about the shoulder, and about the centre of mass. In a multi-part question, check which axis each part is asking about before substituting anything.

§ 04

Moment of inertia: where the mass sits

Definition & formula

Moment of inertia I
A body's resistance to angular acceleration about a specified axis: the sum of each mass element multiplied by the square of its perpendicular distance from that axis.
Parallel-axis theorem
The statement that I about any axis equals I about the parallel axis through the centre of mass, plus Md² — so the centre-of-mass axis always gives the smallest possible I of that family.
I = Σ mi ri² r measured ⟂ to the axis; the axis must be stated
I = β M R²shape factor β: 0.4 sphere, 0.5 disc, 1 hoop
I = Icm + M d²parallel-axis theorem; d from the CM axis, axes parallel
Iz = Ix + Iyperpendicular-axis theorem; flat laminae only
Imoment of inertia (kg·m²)
mimass of the i-th element (kg)
ri⟂ distance of that element from the axis (m)
Mtotal mass of the body (kg)
R, Lradius / length of the body (m)
dseparation of the two parallel axes (m)
βdimensionless shape factor I/MR²

This is the only genuinely new idea in the chapter. Mass measures resistance to being pushed; moment of inertia measures resistance to being turned, and it is not a fixed number for a body. The r² is what makes it interesting: move a gram from 1 cm to 10 cm from the axis and its contribution rises a hundredfold. That single square is the reason a hoop loses a race, a skater speeds up by folding her arms in, and a tightrope walker carries a long pole.

Exam point · the β ladder is the whole of §07

Sphere 0.40 < disc 0.50 < shell 0.67 < hoop 1.00. Learn the order, not just the numbers: the further the mass sits from the axis, the larger β. Every rolling-race, rolling-energy and rolling-acceleration question is decided by this single ladder.

The parallel-axis theorem, and what it is not

I = Icm + Md² lets you shift the axis without redoing any sum, but the term Icm must be the moment of inertia about the axis through the centre of mass and parallel to the new one. You cannot chain it from an arbitrary axis to another arbitrary axis. Because Md² is never negative, the centre-of-mass axis always has the smallest I of its parallel family — the easiest of them to spin the body about. A freely thrown object does tumble about its centre of mass, but for a separate reason: gravity exerts no torque about that point, so nothing is available to start it turning about any other.

Worked example 3 · Swinging a leg from the hip

Model an adult leg as a uniform rod of mass M = 12 kg and length L = 0.90 m, swinging about a transverse axis at the hip. Find I, and compare with the tempting shortcut of putting all 12 kg at the leg's centre of mass.

  1. Rod about one end. I = ⅓ML² = ⅓ × 12 × (0.90)² = ⅓ × 12 × 0.81 = 3.24 kg·m².
  2. The shortcut. Point mass at the CM, 0.45 m out: I = Md² = 12 × (0.45)² = 12 × 0.2025 = 2.43 kg·m² — 25% too small.
  3. Where the missing bit went. Parallel axis: I = Icm + Md², and Icm = 1/12 ML² = 12 × 0.81 / 12 = 0.81 kg·m². Then 0.81 + 2.43 = 3.24 ✓.
  4. The lesson. Collapsing a body to a point at its centre of mass is legal for translation (Newton's second law) and illegal for rotation — you always lose exactly Icm.
Ihip = 3.24 kg·m² · the point-mass shortcut loses Icm = 0.81 kg·m²

Trap · "the moment of inertia of a disc"

There is no such thing. A disc has ½MR² about its central axis, ¼MR² about a diameter, and 3/2 MR² about a tangent line perpendicular to its face (½MR² + MR²). If a question changes the axis between parts, I changes — and so does α for the same torque.

§ 05

Newton's second law for rotation

Definition & formula

Newton's second law for rotation
The statement that the net external torque on a rigid body about a fixed axis equals its moment of inertia about that axis multiplied by its angular acceleration.
Στ = I α rigid body, fixed axis (or an axis through the CM)
Στ = ΔL / Δtthe more general form; valid when I changes too
α = Στ / Isame torque, bigger I → smaller α
Στnet external torque about the axis (N·m)
Imoment of inertia about that same axis (kg·m²)
αangular acceleration (rad·s⁻²)
Langular momentum (kg·m²·s⁻¹)

Everything on this page below §04 follows from putting Στ = Iα next to ΣF = ma. Note the two conditions that are easy to skip: the torques must be external (internal forces cancel in pairs and contribute nothing), and I and α must refer to the same axis. In problems where a body both translates and rotates — a rolling wheel, a falling yo-yo — the legitimate move is to write ΣF = Ma for the centre of mass and Στ = Icmα about the centre of mass, then link them with the rolling condition.

Worked example 4 · A pulley with mass is not a frictionless hook

A block of m = 1.5 kg hangs from a light inextensible string wound over a pulley modelled as a solid disc, M = 2.0 kg, R = 0.10 m, free to turn on a frictionless axle. The string does not slip. Find the block's acceleration, the string tension, and the pulley's α. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Block (translation), taking downward positive: mg − T = ma.
  2. Pulley (rotation) about its axle: the only force with a lever arm is T, acting tangentially at r⊥ = R. So TR = I α = (½MR²)α.
  3. Link them. No slipping between string and rim means a = Rα, i.e. α = a/R. Substituting: TR = ½MR² (a/R) → T = ½Ma. The tension depends only on the pulley's mass, not its radius.
  4. Solve. mg = ma + ½Ma → a = mg/(m + M/2) = (1.5)(9.8)/(1.5 + 1.0) = 14.7/2.5 = 5.88 m·s⁻².
  5. Back-substitute. T = ½(2.0)(5.88) = 5.88 N; α = a/R = 5.88/0.10 = 58.8 rad·s⁻².
  6. Check. mg − T = 14.7 − 5.88 = 8.82 N, and ma = 1.5 × 5.88 = 8.82 N ✓. Also note a < g, as it must be: some of the weight is spent spinning the pulley up. Set M = 0 and you recover a = g = 9.8 m·s⁻².
a = 5.88 m·s⁻² · T = 5.88 N · α = 58.8 rad·s⁻²

Trap · equal tensions on both sides of a pulley

"The tension is the same throughout the string" holds only for a massless pulley. A pulley with moment of inertia needs a net torque to spin up, and a net torque requires unequal tensions: T₁R − T₂R = Iα. If a problem gives the pulley a mass, it is telling you that T₁ ≠ T₂, and any solution that assumes otherwise has already lost.

§ 06

Rotational kinetic energy and the rolling body

Definition & formula

Rotational kinetic energy
The kinetic energy a body has purely because it is spinning — the sum of ½mivi² over all its parts, rewritten using vi = riω.
Kinetic energy of a rolling body
The exact statement that any motion of a rigid body splits into translation of its centre of mass plus rotation about the centre of mass — and so does its kinetic energy.
Krot = ½ I ω² ω in rad·s⁻¹, I about the rotation axis
K = ½ M vcm² + ½ Icm ω²König's split; always valid for a rigid body
K = ½ M v² (1 + β)rolling without slipping, with Icm = βMR²
W = τ Δθ  ·  P = τ ωconstant τ (otherwise W = ∫τ dθ); work–energy theorem: Wnet = ΔKrot
Krotrotational kinetic energy (J)
vcmspeed of the centre of mass (m·s⁻¹)
Icmmoment of inertia about the CM axis (kg·m²)
βshape factor, Icm/MR² (dimensionless)
Wrotational work (J)
Ppower delivered by a torque (W)

A spinning flywheel whose centre of mass is at rest still stores energy, and the amount is ½Iω². Nothing new is being asserted: take Σ½mivi², substitute vi = riω, pull the common ω² out, and what is left inside is Σmiri² = I. The ω² is why a modest flywheel at high speed stores usable energy, and why a burst rotor is a genuine laboratory hazard.

For a body that both moves and spins — a wheel, a bowling ball, a limb segment swinging — the kinetic energy is the sum of two terms and never a choice between them. Written with the shape factor, K = ½Mv²(1 + β), so the fraction of the total energy tied up in spin is β/(1 + β): 28.6% for a solid sphere, 33.3% for a disc, 50% for a hoop. A hoop arriving at the bottom of a slope has spent half of its released potential energy on turning, and only half on getting there.

Exam point · when to use energy and when to use torque

If the question asks for a speed after a drop and no time appears, use Mgh = ½Mv²(1 + β) and you are done in one line. If it asks for an acceleration, a time, or a friction force, you need ΣF = Ma together with Στ = Icmα. Picking the wrong tool costs more marks than any arithmetic slip.

§ 07

Rolling without slipping, and the incline race

Definition & formula

Rolling without slipping
Motion in which the contact point of the body is instantaneously at rest relative to the surface, so the distance travelled equals the arc length unwound from the rim.
Static friction in rolling
The only force in the problem with a lever arm about the centre of mass — gravity acts at the centre of mass and the normal force points straight at it. Friction is therefore what spins the body up, and because it acts at a point of zero velocity it does no work.
vcm = R ω  ·  acm = R α  ·  s = R θ rolling without slipping only
a = g sin θ / (1 + β)rolling down a slope of angle θ, from Στ and ΣF
v = √( 2gh / (1 + β) )from rest, after dropping a height h
fs = β M g sin θ / (1 + β)the static friction actually required
tan θ ≤ μs (1 + β) / βsteeper than this and it slips instead
vcmspeed of the centre (m·s⁻¹)
Rrolling radius (m)
hvertical drop of the centre of mass (m)
θangle of the incline to the horizontal (° or rad)
fsstatic friction at the contact patch (N)
μscoefficient of static friction (dimensionless)
βshape factor Icm/MR² (dimensionless)

Rolling is the superposition of two motions: the whole body sliding forward at v, plus a pure spin about the centre at ω = v/R. Add them at the bottom of the wheel and they cancel exactly — the contact point is momentarily stationary. Add them at the top and they reinforce — the top of a rolling wheel moves at 2v. That is why the spokes of a moving bicycle wheel blur at the top and stay sharp at the bottom.

Why the hoop always loses — and why M and R never appear

Write energy conservation for a body released from rest and dropping a height h while rolling: Mgh = ½Mv² + ½Icmω². Substitute Icm = βMR² and ω = v/R, and the R² in I cancels the 1/R² in ω² exactly; then M cancels across the whole equation. What survives is v = √(2gh/(1 + β)) — a result that depends on shape alone. A solid steel sphere the size of a marble and a solid wooden sphere the size of a beach ball reach the bottom of the same slope at the same speed and at the same instant.

Worked example 5 · Sphere against hoop, 1.5 m of drop

A solid sphere (m = 0.20 kg, R = 3.0 cm) and a steel hoop (m = 1.4 kg, R = 12 cm) are released from rest at the top of the same incline and roll without slipping through a vertical drop of h = 1.5 m. Find each centre-of-mass speed at the bottom, and the ratio.

  1. Set up energy conservation. Friction is static at a point of zero velocity, so it removes no energy: Mgh = ½Mv²(1 + β).
  2. Rearrange once, use twice. v = √(2gh/(1 + β)), and 2gh = 2(9.8)(1.5) = 29.4 m²·s⁻².
  3. Sphere, β = 0.4: v = √(29.4/1.4) = √21.0 = 4.58 m·s⁻¹.
  4. Hoop, β = 1: v = √(29.4/2) = √14.7 = 3.83 m·s⁻¹.
  5. Ratio. 4.58/3.83 = 1.20, i.e. √(2/1.4) = √1.4286 = 1.195 — set entirely by β, so the masses (0.20 kg vs 1.4 kg) and radii (3 cm vs 12 cm) given in the question are pure decoration.
  6. Check against the slider. A frictionless block from the same height reaches √29.4 = 5.42 m·s⁻¹ — faster than both, because it puts nothing into spin.
vsphere = 4.58 m·s⁻¹ · vhoop = 3.83 m·s⁻¹ · ratio 1.20, independent of M and R

Trap · "friction is present, so energy is lost"

In rolling without slipping the friction is static and acts at a point that is instantaneously at rest, so its work is F × 0 = 0. Mechanical energy is conserved even though friction is essential — remove it (put the ball on ice) and the ball slides without spinning. Energy is only dissipated when the surfaces actually slip past each other, which is kinetic friction.

Two ways to read the same fact

Energy view: the hoop wastes half its budget on spin. Dynamics view: the hoop has the largest I for its mass, so the same gravitational torque gives it the smallest α. Same answer, and the exam may ask for either.

§ 08

Angular momentum and its conservation

Definition & formula

Angular momentum L
The rotational analogue of linear momentum: for a rigid body about a fixed axis, its moment of inertia multiplied by its angular velocity.
Conservation of angular momentum
The statement that L is constant whenever the net external torque about the chosen axis is zero — regardless of how violently the body rearranges itself internally.
L = I ω  ⇒  I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ when Στext = 0 same axis on both sides

Krotrotational kinetic energy (J)

Στ = Iα is the special case of Στ = ΔL/Δt in which I is constant. The general form matters because the most interesting rotating objects — skaters, divers, cats, collapsing stars — change their own I. When they do, the torque law says that with no external torque, L is fixed and ω must move inversely to I. Internal muscular forces come in action–reaction pairs whose torques cancel, so they can redistribute mass but can never change L.

Exam point · why a diver in flight conserves L

Gravity acts effectively at the centre of mass, so its lever arm about the centre of mass is zero and its torque about that point vanishes. Once the diver leaves the board, L about the centre of mass is locked in for the whole flight, and the centre of mass itself follows a plain parabola no matter what the limbs do. The diver controls only the rate of rotation, never the total amount of turning available.

Worked example 6 · Two bodies that change their own I

(a) The skater. A skater spins on a frictionless vertical axis at ω₁ = 1.5 rad·s⁻¹ with arms outstretched, I₁ = 4.0 kg·m². She pulls her arms in to I₂ = 1.2 kg·m². Find ω₂ and the change in her rotational kinetic energy.

  1. No external torque about the vertical axis (the ice is frictionless, gravity and the normal force are parallel to the axis), so L = I₁ω₁ = 4.0 × 1.5 = 6.0 kg·m²·s⁻¹ is fixed.
  2. ω₂ = L/I₂ = 6.0 / 1.2 = 5.0 rad·s⁻¹ — from 0.24 to 0.80 revolutions per second.
  3. K₁ = ½I₁ω₁² = ½(4.0)(1.5)² = 4.5 J; K₂ = ½I₂ω₂² = ½(1.2)(5.0)² = 15 J.
  4. ΔK = +10.5 J, a factor of 15/4.5 = 3.33 — which is exactly I₁/I₂, as K = L²/(2I) predicts. The energy is not free: it is muscular work done pulling the arms inward against the force needed to keep them circling.
(a) ω₂ = 5.0 rad·s⁻¹ · K rises 4.5 J → 15 J (×3.33 = I₁/I₂)

(b) The diver. A diver leaves the board with L fixed, in a layout position with I₁ = 14 kg·m² and ω₁ = 3.0 rad·s⁻¹, and is airborne for 1.6 s. She tucks to I₂ = 3.5 kg·m² for 1.2 s, then opens back out to the layout for the last 0.40 s. How many somersaults does she complete, and how many would she have managed had she never tucked?

  1. L = 14 × 3.0 = 42 kg·m²·s⁻¹, unchanged in flight.
  2. Tucked: ω₂ = 42 / 3.5 = 12 rad·s⁻¹ — four times faster, because I fell by a factor of four.
  3. Angle turned = 12 × 1.2 + 3.0 × 0.40 = 14.4 + 1.2 = 15.6 rad.
  4. 15.6 / 2π = 2.48 revolutions — two and a half somersaults.
  5. Never tucking: 3.0 × 1.6 = 4.8 rad = 0.76 rev, barely three-quarters of one turn.
  6. Note what the diver cannot do: she cannot add L in the air. All of it was bought on the board, where the board did exert an external torque about her centre of mass.
(b) ωtuck = 12 rad·s⁻¹ · 2.48 rev instead of 0.76 rev

Trap · conserved L does not mean conserved energy

In every "pulls the arms in" problem, angular momentum is conserved and kinetic energy increases by the factor I₁/I₂. In the reverse move — arms going out — K decreases and the body does negative work. Treating these as elastic-collision-style "K is conserved" problems is one of the most reliably punished errors in the topic. Use K = L²/(2I) and the direction is automatic.

The same principle explains why a rotating patient on a frictionless turntable can reorient her whole body with no external help, why an ice skater's spin is stopped by scraping a blade (an external torque from the ice), and why the Earth's day would lengthen if its mass migrated outward. In physiology, the conservation statement is the reason a gymnast or a falling cat can change orientation in the air with L = 0 throughout, by counter-rotating one part of the body against another — orientation is not conserved, only angular momentum is.

§ 09

Static equilibrium: two conditions, free pivot

Definition & formula

Static equilibrium
The state of a rigid body that is at rest and stays at rest: its centre of mass does not accelerate and it does not begin to rotate.
Independence of the pivot
The fact that, once the net force is zero, the net torque takes the same value about every point — so the torque equation may be written about whichever point is most convenient.
ΣF = 0  and  Στ = 0 both are required; neither implies the other
ΣFx = 0 · ΣFy = 0 · Στz = 0a plane problem gives exactly three independent equations
ΣτB = ΣτA − d × ΣFd = displacement from pivot A to pivot B; the last term dies when ΣF = 0
ΣFx, ΣFynet force components (N)
Στnet torque about the chosen axis (N·m)
ddisplacement from the old pivot to the new one (m)
Nnormal / support force (N)
W = mgweight, acting at the centre of gravity (N)

Two conditions, because a rigid body has two ways to start moving. A pair of equal and opposite forces applied at different points — a couple — has ΣF = 0 and yet spins the body up, which is the clean proof that the force condition alone is not equilibrium. Conversely, a single force through the centre of mass gives Στcm = 0 and yet accelerates the body away.

Why the pivot is yours to choose

Move the reference point from A to B, a displacement d. Every position vector measured from the pivot is shortened by that same d, so the net torque changes by −d × ΣF. In equilibrium ΣF = 0, so the net torque is the same about every point in the plane. Practically this means: put your axis on the line of action of the force you least want to think about, and it vanishes from the equation. A support you cannot compute, a hinge reaction with two unknown components, a joint force — all can be deleted by a smart choice of pivot.

Exam point · three equations, three unknowns, no more

A plane statics problem yields exactly three independent equations. Writing torque equations about two different points is legitimate and often convenient — but the second one can only replace a force equation, never add a fourth piece of information. If you find yourself with four unknowns, the problem is statically indeterminate and you have missed a modelling assumption (a rope is inextensible, a rod is light, a contact is frictionless).

Worked example 7 · A plank on two supports

A uniform plank of length 4.0 m and mass 20 kg rests on a support A at its left end and a support B 3.0 m from A. A 60 kg person stands 2.5 m from A. Find the force each support exerts. Take g = 9.8 m·s⁻².

  1. Forces. Plank weight 20 × 9.8 = 196 N acting at its centre, 2.0 m from A (uniform ⇒ centre of gravity at the geometric centre). Person's weight 60 × 9.8 = 588 N at 2.5 m. Supports push up: NA at 0 m, NB at 3.0 m.
  2. Take torques about A — this kills NA, whose lever arm about A is zero: NB(3.0) = 196(2.0) + 588(2.5) = 392 + 1470 = 1862 N·m.
  3. NB = 1862 / 3.0 = 620.7 N.
  4. Now use ΣFy = 0: NA = 196 + 588 − 620.7 = 163.3 N.
  5. Independent check — torques about B (nothing new, but it must come out right): NA(3.0) = 196(1.0) + 588(0.5) → 490 = 196 + 294 = 490 ✓.
  6. Extension worth 30 seconds. If the person walks to 3.5 m — past support B — repeating step 2 gives NB = (392 + 2058)/3.0 = 816.7 N and hence NA = 784 − 816.7 = −32.7 N. A negative "push" is impossible for a support that can only push: the plank tips about B unless A is bolted down with 32.7 N of hold-down. A negative normal force is the algebra telling you the object has left the surface.
NA = 163 N · NB = 621 N (sum 784 N = total weight ✓)

Trap · weight acts at the centre of gravity, not "in the middle of the diagram"

For a uniform body the centre of gravity is at its geometric centre — but only if it is uniform. Put a weight on one end of the plank, or model a limb whose muscle mass is proximal, and the centre of gravity shifts. Draw the weight arrow from the centre of gravity every time; a lever arm measured from the wrong point is an error you will not spot in the arithmetic.

§ 10

Centre of gravity, base of support, stability

Definition & formula

Centre of gravity (CG)
The single point at which the whole weight of a body may be taken to act — the point about which the total gravitational torque is zero. In a uniform gravitational field it coincides exactly with the centre of mass.
Base of support
The area enclosed by all the points of contact between a body and the ground, including the space between two feet or between a foot and a walking stick.
Stable equilibrium
A configuration in which a small displacement raises the centre of gravity, so that gravity generates a restoring torque back toward the original position.
xcg = Σ mi xi / Σ mi and likewise for y; uniform g assumed
tan θcrit = (b/2) / hcguniform block of width b, CG centred over the base, on a tilting surface
stable ⇔ CG's vertical line falls inside the basethe practical criterion
xcghorizontal coordinate of the CG (m)
mi, ximass (kg) and position (m) of each part
bwidth of the base of support (m)
hcgheight of the CG above the base (m)
θcrittilt at which tipping begins (°)

Stability is a torque problem in disguise. Tilt an object and gravity, acting down through the CG, has a lever arm about the edge it is pivoting on. While the CG's vertical line still falls inside the base, that torque pushes the object back down; the instant the line crosses the edge, the same torque tips it over. Nothing about the object's mass appears — a heavy filing cabinet and a light cardboard box of the same proportions tip at the same angle.

Worked example 8 · How far can it lean?

A uniform cabinet is 0.40 m wide and 1.20 m tall and stands on a surface that is slowly tilted. At what tilt does it topple, and does the answer change if it is loaded with heavy files at the top?

  1. Locate the CG. Uniform ⇒ centre of the block: half-width 0.20 m from the pivot edge, height hcg = 0.60 m above the base.
  2. Tipping condition. The block tips when the CG's vertical line reaches the downhill edge, i.e. when tan θcrit = (b/2)/hcg = 0.20/0.60 = 0.3333.
  3. θcrit = arctan(0.3333) = 18.4°. Mass never entered the calculation.
  4. Loading the top. Files in the upper drawers raise hcg. Say the CG rises to 0.85 m: tan θcrit = 0.20/0.85 = 0.2353 → θcrit = 13.2°. Adding weight has made it less stable, because stability is set by CG height, not by weight.
  5. The design fix. Widen the base. Doubling b to 0.80 m with hcg = 0.60 m gives tan θ = 0.40/0.60 = 0.667 → 33.7°.
θcrit = 18.4° · falls to 13.2° when the CG rises to 0.85 m · mass is irrelevant

The human case

In an adult standing erect the centre of gravity sits at roughly 55–57% of standing height, just anterior to the second sacral vertebra. Quiet standing is not static: the body sways continuously and the postural muscles apply small corrective torques whenever the CG's projection drifts toward the edge of the base — the ankle strategy. Everything clinical about falls is a statement about this geometry:

  • A cane or walking frame enlarges the base of support, not the person's strength; it buys a larger margin between the CG projection and the edge.
  • Carrying a load in front shifts the CG anteriorly, which is why pregnancy and large anterior loads are compensated by increased lumbar lordosis and a backward lean — the body is moving the CG back over the feet.
  • A stooped (kyphotic) posture pushes the CG projection toward the toes, shrinking the forward margin and leaving the ankle plantarflexors with less room to correct — a direct mechanical contribution to fall risk in the elderly.
  • Reaching sideways past the foot moves the CG outside the base entirely; recovery then requires a step, i.e. a new base.

Exam point

Stability questions never need the mass. Compare (i) CG height and (ii) base width, and remember the third case: neutral equilibrium, where displacement neither raises nor lowers the CG (a sphere on a flat table, a cylinder lying on its side). Stable = CG rises; unstable = CG falls; neutral = CG stays level.

§ 11

The three classes of lever in the body

Definition & formula

Lever
A rigid bar free to turn about a fulcrum, on which an effort and a load act at different lever arms; the class is decided purely by which of the three lies between the other two.
Mechanical advantage MA
The ratio of load to effort achievable in equilibrium — equivalently, the ratio of the effort's lever arm to the load's.
FE dE = FL dL  ⇒  MA = dE/dL = FL/FE just Στ = 0 about the fulcrum
Class 1: E — F — L · Class 2: F — L — E · Class 3: F — E — Lreading along the bar; the middle letter names the class
Fjoint = FE + FL (signed)from ΣF = 0; the fulcrum reaction is never zero
FEeffort — the muscle force (N)
FLload — the resistance (N)
dE, dLlever arms of effort and load about the fulcrum (m)
MAmechanical advantage (dimensionless)
Fjointreaction force at the joint / fulcrum (N)

Every skeletal joint is a lever: bone is the bar, the joint is the fulcrum, muscle supplies the effort, and gravity or an external object supplies the load. The classification is not decoration — it tells you immediately whether the muscle force will be larger or smaller than the load it is holding.

ClassOrder along the barMechanical advantageWhat it buysIn the bodyEveryday
FirstE — F — Leither side of 1, depending on the two arms can trade force for range in either direction head balanced on the atlanto-occipital joint; triceps extending the elbow (olecranon)seesaw, crowbar, scissors
SecondF — L — Ealways > 1 (dE > dL by construction) force multiplier — effort smaller than the load it balances rising on tiptoe: metatarsal heads are the fulcrum, joint load at the ankle, Achilles at the calcaneuswheelbarrow, nutcracker

Naming the classes

1-2-3 → F-L-E in the middle. Class 1 has the Fulcrum in the middle, class 2 the Load, class 3 the Effort. Then remember only that class 3 is the body's default.

Worked example 9 · Three joints, three lever classes

(a) Elbow — third class. A horizontal forearm plus hand has mass 2.0 kg with its centre of gravity 0.15 m from the elbow joint. It holds a 5.0 kg ball whose centre is 0.35 m from the joint. The biceps inserts 0.050 m from the joint and pulls vertically upward. Find the biceps force and the force transmitted through the elbow joint.

  1. Weights: forearm 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N at 0.15 m; ball 5.0 × 9.8 = 49.0 N at 0.35 m.
  2. Torques about the elbow (this deletes the unknown joint force, whose lever arm there is zero): FB(0.050) = 19.6(0.15) + 49.0(0.35) = 2.94 + 17.15 = 20.09 N·m.
  3. FB = 20.09 / 0.050 = 402 N — roughly eight times the ball's weight. The ball alone accounts for 49.0 × (0.35/0.050) = 343 N of that, since its lever ratio is MA = 0.050/0.35 = 0.14; the forearm's own weight supplies the remaining 59 N.
  4. Joint force from ΣFy = 0: Fjoint = 19.6 + 49.0 − 402 = −333 N, i.e. 333 N pressing down on the forearm from the humerus. The joint carries far more than the load it is holding — which is why joint surfaces are broad and why small increases in held weight matter so much to a damaged joint.
(a) Fbiceps ≈ 4.0 × 10² N · elbow joint reaction ≈ 3.3 × 10² N

(b) Tiptoe — second class. A 700 N person rises onto the toes of one foot. The ground pushes up at the metatarsal heads; the ankle joint is 0.12 m behind them and the Achilles tendon inserts on the calcaneus 0.18 m behind them. Find the Achilles force and the ankle joint reaction.

  1. Treat the foot as the lever. Forces on it: N = 700 N up at the toes (the only ground contact, so it carries the whole body weight), the Achilles force FA up at 0.18 m, and the downward reaction R from the tibia at 0.12 m.
  2. Torques about the ankle to eliminate R: 700 × 0.12 = FA × 0.06, so FA = 84/0.06 = 1400 N = 2W.
  3. ΣFy = 0: R = 700 + 1400 = 2100 N = 3W.
  4. Now read the lever. MA = dE/dL = 0.18/0.12 = 1.5, and indeed FA = R/1.5 = 2100/1.5 = 1400 N ✓. The class-2 advantage is real — but it relates the tendon force to the joint load, not to body weight.
(b) Achilles ≈ 1.4 × 10³ N (2× body weight) · ankle reaction ≈ 2.1 × 10³ N (3× body weight)

(c) Head — first class. A 50 N head has its centre of gravity 0.030 m anterior to the atlanto-occipital joint; the posterior neck extensors act 0.050 m behind it. Find the muscle force, then repeat for a forward-flexed "phone" posture in which the head's effective lever arm grows to 0.080 m.

  1. Upright: FM(0.050) = 50(0.030) = 1.5 N·m → FM = 30 N; joint reaction = 50 + 30 = 80 N.
  2. Flexed: FM(0.050) = 50(0.080) = 4.0 N·m → FM = 80 N; joint reaction = 50 + 80 = 130 N.
  3. The head has not got heavier; its lever arm has lengthened by a factor of 2.7, and the extensor load and the joint load rise with it. This is the mechanics behind sustained-flexion neck pain, and the reason the clinical advice is always to raise the screen rather than to strengthen the neck.
(c) 30 N upright → 80 N at 0.080 m lever arm; joint load 80 N → 130 N

Trap · "mechanical advantage > 1 means the muscle works less than body weight"

MA compares the effort with the load acting on that lever, and in the tiptoe case the load is the ankle joint reaction (2100 N), not the 700 N body weight. Solve the free-body diagram; do not read a number straight off the lever ratio. The same caution applies in reverse for class 3: MA < 1 is not a design flaw — a 3 cm shortening of the biceps sweeps the hand through 20 cm, and speed of movement is worth far more to a limb than raw force.

Exam point

Two facts carry most of the marks here. (1) Most human joints are third-class levers, so muscle forces routinely exceed the load by an order of magnitude. (2) The joint reaction force is obtained from ΣF = 0 after the torque equation, and it is typically the largest force in the whole diagram.
