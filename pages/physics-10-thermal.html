Physics · Note 10

Thermal Physics and Thermodynamics

One idea carries this whole chapter: energy stored in random molecular motion behaves differently from energy stored in organised motion. Because the motion is random, you can always turn organised energy completely into random energy, but never the reverse — and every formula here, from Q = mcΔT to the Carnot limit, is a consequence of that asymmetry. Temperature is the bookkeeping variable for how much random energy sits in each molecule; entropy is the bookkeeping variable for how many ways it can be arranged.

Notation trap · three overloaded letters on this page

L is latent heat in Q = mL (J·kg⁻¹) but slab thickness in H = kAΔT/L (m). e is thermal efficiency in e = W/Qh — emissivity is written ε here, as in P = εσAT⁴. σ is thermal stress in σ = YαΔT (Pa) but the Stefan–Boltzmann constant in the same radiation formula. When in doubt, check the units — they disambiguate every one of these.

§ 01

Temperature, equilibrium and the zeroth law

Definition & formula

Thermal contact
Two systems are in thermal contact when energy can pass between them by microscopic collisions rather than by macroscopic work.
Thermal equilibrium
The state in which two systems in thermal contact exchange no net energy. Individual molecules still trade energy across the boundary; the traffic is simply balanced.
Temperature
The one property that is necessarily equal for two systems in thermal equilibrium — and therefore the property that decides the direction of net heat flow.
Zeroth law of thermodynamics
Thermal equilibrium is transitive: if A is in equilibrium with C, and B is in equilibrium with C, then A is in equilibrium with B.
TA = TC and TB = TC ⟹ TA = TB always; this is what makes a thermometer meaningful
net Q flows from high T to low Tspontaneous direction; never the reverse without work
Tabsolute temperature (K)
θtemperature on a relative scale (°C or °F)
Qheat — energy transferred because of a ΔT (J)

The zeroth law looks like a triviality and is not. It is the licence to use a third body — a thermometer — as a stand-in for direct comparison. Without transitivity, "the patient's temperature is 38.5 °C" would be a statement about the thermometer and nothing else. Because equilibrium is transitive, one number attached to the mercury column tells you what would happen if you put that patient in contact with any other body of known temperature.

It also fixes the logical order of the subject. Temperature is defined by equilibrium, not by "hotness"; heat is defined by temperature difference. Get that order backwards and the classic confusions in §4 become unavoidable.

Trap · equilibrium is not stillness

At equilibrium molecular exchange does not stop — the net exchange stops. Two objects at the same temperature in contact are still passing energy back and forth at an enormous rate; the two currents cancel. The same idea returns in dynamic equilibrium in chemistry and in resting membrane potential in physiology.

Exam point

Questions phrased as "objects A and B are each in equilibrium with C; what can you conclude about A and B?" are pure zeroth-law items. The answer is always TA = TB — and never anything about their internal energies, masses or heat capacities, which the zeroth law says nothing about.

§ 02

Scales, and what absolute zero means

Definition & formula

Celsius scale
A relative scale with 0 °C and 100 °C fixed near the ice point and steam point of water at 1 atm; the degree size is defined to equal the kelvin.
Kelvin (thermodynamic) scale
The SI absolute scale. Its zero is the temperature at which the pressure of an ideal gas at fixed volume extrapolates to zero — a point every gas agrees on, whatever it is made of.
Absolute zero
0 K = −273.15 °C: the state of minimum possible internal energy. Not "no motion" — quantum zero-point motion survives — but no thermally extractable energy left.
T/K = θ/°C + 273.15 exact by definition of the Celsius scale
θF = (9/5) θC + 32Celsius → Fahrenheit
θC = (5/9)(θF − 32)Fahrenheit → Celsius
ΔT: 1 K = 1 °C = 1.8 °Fintervals, not readings — no +273 and no +32
Tabsolute (thermodynamic) temperature (K)
θCCelsius reading (°C)
θFFahrenheit reading (°F)
ΔTtemperature interval (K, numerically = °C)

Why does an absolute zero exist at all? Take a fixed amount of dilute gas at constant volume and plot its pressure against Celsius temperature. The graph is a straight line. Do it with helium, with nitrogen, with argon, at different densities — you get different lines, but every one of them extrapolates to zero pressure at the same intercept, −273.15 °C. Pressure comes from molecular collisions with the wall (§8), so zero pressure means zero translational agitation. The scale that starts there is the only one on which P ∝ T and vrms ∝ √T are true statements rather than approximations with an offset.

Trap · reading vs interval

"The temperature rose by 20 °C" converts to "rose by 20 K", not 293 K. Add 273.15 only when converting an absolute reading. In every formula containing ΔT — Q = mcΔT, ΔL = αL₀ΔT, H = kAΔT/L — Celsius and kelvin give identical numbers, so you may use either. In every formula containing T alone — PV = nRT, P = eσAT⁴, e = 1 − Tc/Th, ΔS = Q/T — you must use kelvin.

One-line rule

Delta ⇒ either scale. Bare T ⇒ kelvin only. If the symbol T appears without a Δ in front of it, write the kelvin value down before you touch the calculator.

Exam point · the third law

Absolute zero is unattainable in a finite number of steps — each stage of cooling can only remove a fraction of what remains. This is why "efficiency = 1" (§11) and "entropy = 0" (§10) are limits, not achievable states. A question offering "an engine of 100 % efficiency is possible if the cold reservoir is at 0 K" is testing exactly this.

§ 03

Thermal expansion — and where water breaks the rule

Definition & formula

Thermal expansion
The change in a body's dimensions caused by a change in temperature, arising because the interatomic potential is asymmetric: atoms vibrating harder spend more time far apart than close together, so the mean separation grows.
Coefficient of linear expansion α
The fractional change in length per unit temperature change.
Coefficient of volume expansion β
The fractional change in volume per unit temperature change; for an isotropic solid β ≈ 3α.
ΔL = α L₀ ΔT α roughly constant over a modest ΔT; small strains only
ΔA = 2α A₀ ΔTisotropic solid, area
ΔV = β V₀ ΔT, β ≈ 3αisotropic solid or a liquid (β measured directly)
βideal gas = 1/Tconstant pressure; from PV = nRT
σ = Y α ΔTthermal stress if expansion is fully prevented
L₀, A₀, V₀original length (m), area (m²), volume (m³)
αlinear expansion coefficient (K⁻¹)
βvolume expansion coefficient (K⁻¹)
ΔTtemperature change (K)
YYoung's modulus (Pa)
σthermal stress (Pa)

Where β ≈ 3α comes from. A cube of side L₀ has volume L₀³. After heating, each side is L₀(1 + αΔT), so V = L₀³(1 + αΔT)³ = V₀(1 + 3αΔT + 3α²ΔT² + α³ΔT³). The dimensionless quantity that decides whether the higher terms matter is αΔT, not α by itself: with α ~ 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, even a 100 K rise gives αΔT ~ 10⁻³, so the quadratic term is a thousandth of the linear one and the cubic a millionth. Hence ΔV ≈ 3α V₀ ΔT, i.e. β = 3α. It is a linearisation, not a coincidence — and it is why β ≈ 3α is quoted for solids only: a liquid has no lattice whose every edge scales together, so its β is measured directly, and for a gas β comes straight from the equation of state as 1/T.

Trap · holes get bigger

Heat a metal plate with a hole in it and the hole expands, by the same fractional amount as everything else. Reason: expansion is a uniform scaling of the whole object — imagine the disc of metal you removed to make the hole; it would have grown, and the material around it does exactly what that disc would have done. This is why a stuck metal jar lid loosens under hot water, and why a steel ring can be shrink-fitted onto a shaft.

Worked example 1 · expansion joints on a steel bridge

A continuous steel span is 300 m long. Local temperatures run from −5 °C in winter to +40 °C in summer. How much room must the expansion joints allow?

  1. Interval: ΔT = 40 − (−5) = 45 K. (An interval, so °C and K are the same number.)
  2. αsteel = 12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, L₀ = 300 m.
  3. ΔL = αL₀ΔT = (12×10⁻⁶)(300)(45) = (3.6×10⁻³)(45) = 0.162 m.
  4. Sanity check on the alternative: if the joints were welded shut, the stress would be σ = YαΔT = (2.0×10¹¹)(12×10⁻⁶)(45) ≈ 1.1×10⁸ Pa = 110 MPa — a large fraction of the yield strength of structural steel. Bridges buckle for exactly this reason.
ΔL ≈ 0.16 m (16 cm) of travel per 300 m span

Water's anomaly, and why it matters biologically

Between 0 °C and 3.98 °C, liquid water contracts as it is warmed — β is negative there. Above 4 °C it behaves normally. The cause is hydrogen bonding: near the freezing point, water retains patches of the open, tetrahedral, ice-like network, and warming collapses those open cages faster than ordinary vibrational expansion pushes molecules apart. Water therefore has its maximum density (≈1000 kg·m⁻³) at about 4 °C.

Freezing makes it worse: the full ice-Ih lattice is more open still, so ice has a density of about 917 kg·m⁻³ — about 8 % less dense than liquid water, which is the same statement as "ice occupies ≈9 % more volume" (1000/917 = 1.09). Two consequences worth carrying into an exam:

  • Lakes freeze from the top. As surface water cools towards 4 °C it sinks; below 4 °C it becomes less dense and stays on top, then freezes into an insulating lid. The bottom stays at ~4 °C and aquatic life survives the winter. If water behaved normally, lakes would freeze solid from the bottom up.
  • Freezing destroys cells. Ice crystals occupy more volume than the water they came from and grow with mechanical force, so uncontrolled freezing ruptures membranes. This is why cryopreservation uses penetrating cryoprotectants (glycerol, DMSO) and vitrification — cooling fast enough to reach a glassy state without ever nucleating crystals.
§ 04

Heat, internal energy, temperature: three different things

Definition & formula

Internal energy U
The total kinetic plus potential energy of a body's microscopic constituents, measured in the frame where the body as a whole is at rest. It is a state function: it depends only on the current state, never on how the state was reached.
Heat Q
Energy transferred between systems because of a temperature difference. Heat is a process, not a substance — it exists only while it is crossing a boundary. A body contains internal energy; it never contains heat.
Work W
Energy transferred by an organised, macroscopic displacement against a force — the other way of changing U, and the only other way.
Temperature T
A measure of the average translational kinetic energy per molecule; an intensive property, independent of how much stuff there is.
ΔU = Q − Wby the first law; sign convention fixed in §9

Wwork (J) — path function

kBBoltzmann constant, 1.38×10⁻²³ J·K⁻¹

These three words are used interchangeably in ordinary speech and never in physics. The cleanest way to keep them apart is to notice what each one is a property of:

The last row is the whole point. A cup of tea at 90 °C has the higher temperature; a bath at 30 °C has vastly more internal energy, because U scales with the number of molecules and T does not. Put them in contact and energy still flows from the cup to the bath — direction is set by temperature, quantity by internal energy.

Trap · "adding heat raises the temperature"

False in two standard situations, and both appear in exams. (1) Phase change: heat entering melting ice at 0 °C goes into breaking the lattice — potential energy, not kinetic — so U rises with T fixed (§5). (2) The gas does work: in an isothermal expansion all the heat absorbed leaves again as work, so ΔU = 0 and T never moves (§9). Conversely, temperature can rise with no heat at all — adiabatic compression, §9.

Exam point · path functions

You may write ΔU for a change in internal energy, but never "ΔQ" or "ΔW" — there is no such thing as an amount of heat belonging to a state, so there is nothing to take a difference of. Q and W are the transfers themselves. In a cyclic process the system returns to its initial state, so ΔU = 0 exactly, and therefore Qnet = Wnet — the single most useful line in the whole of §11.

§ 05

Specific heat, latent heat, calorimetry

Definition & formula

Specific heat capacity c
The energy required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one kelvin, with no phase change.
Molar heat capacity C
The same quantity per mole rather than per kilogram; for gases it must be labelled with the constraint (CV or Cp, §9), because a gas free to expand spends some of the heat on work.
Latent heat L
The energy per kilogram required to complete a phase change at constant temperature. Latent heat of fusion Lf for solid ⇌ liquid; latent heat of vaporisation Lv for liquid ⇌ gas.
Calorimetry
Energy bookkeeping in a thermally isolated container: what one part loses, the others gain.
Q = m c ΔT no phase change; c roughly constant over the range
Q = m Lphase change only; T constant throughout
Q = n C ΔTmolar form; specify CV or Cp for gases
Σ Qi = 0isolated calorimeter; losses negative, gains positive
1 cal = 4.184 J, 1 Cal (food) = 1 kcal = 4184 Junit conversion — clinical literature uses kcal
Qheat transferred (J)
mmass (kg)
cspecific heat capacity (J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹)
Cmolar heat capacity (J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)
Llatent heat (J·kg⁻¹)
ΔTtemperature change (K)

Heat a substance steadily and plot temperature against energy delivered. The graph is not a straight line but a staircase of sloping runs and flat plateaus. Sloping run: energy is going into molecular kinetic energy, so T climbs, at a rate set by 1/c. Plateau: energy is going into molecular potential energy — pulling molecules out of the lattice or out of the liquid entirely — so T does not move at all. The plateau length is mL; the run slope is 1/(mc).

Why Lv is so much bigger than Lf

Melting only has to loosen the lattice: molecules stay in contact, still hydrogen-bonded to several neighbours, and merely lose their fixed positions. Vaporising has to separate each molecule from all of its neighbours completely — for water that is roughly three or four hydrogen bonds per molecule — and push back the atmosphere to make room for a vapour about 1600 times more voluminous than the liquid. Hence Lv/Lf = 2260/334 ≈ 6.8 for water, and a similar ratio for most substances.

Worked example 2 · calorimetry with a phase change

25 g of ice at −15 °C is dropped into 200 g of water at 30 °C in a calorimeter of negligible heat capacity. Find the final temperature. (cice = 2090, cwater = 4180 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹, Lf = 3.34×10⁵ J·kg⁻¹.)

  1. Never assume the ice all melts. First test it. Energy the warm water can give up before it reaches 0 °C: Qavail = (0.200)(4180)(30) = 25 080 J.
  2. Energy needed to bring the ice to 0 °C: (0.025)(2090)(15) = 784 J. Energy needed to melt it all: (0.025)(3.34×10⁵) = 8350 J. Total = 9134 J.
  3. 9134 J < 25 080 J, so all the ice melts and the final temperature is above 0 °C. Now solve for Tf with ΣQ = 0.
  4. Gained by the ice stream: 784 + 8350 + (0.025)(4180)Tf = 9134 + 104.5 Tf.
    Lost by the warm water: (0.200)(4180)(30 − Tf) = 25 080 − 836 Tf.
  5. Setting them equal: 940.5 Tf = 15 946 ⟹ Tf = 16.955 °C.
Tf ≈ 17.0 °C

Trap · the calorimetry three-step

Every mixed-phase calorimetry problem has the same failure mode: assuming the answer's phase. Do it in this order — (1) compute how much energy is available, (2) compute how much the phase change would cost, (3) compare. If the available energy is less than the cost, the answer is 0 °C with a mixture of ice and water, and the unknown is how much ice melted, not the temperature.

Water's heat capacity is a physiological instrument

Water's c is roughly four times that of air per kilogram and five to ten times that of a metal. Two consequences run through medicine:

  • Thermal inertia. A 70 kg body is mostly water, with c ≈ 3470 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹, so raising the core by 1 K takes (70)(3470) = 2.43×10⁵ J ≈ 58 kcal. That buffer is why core temperature drifts slowly and why fever is metabolically expensive to hold.
  • Blood is the coolant. Because water carries so much energy per degree, a modest cutaneous blood flow moves a large heat load from core to skin. Vasodilation and vasoconstriction are, in engineering terms, a variable-flow-rate liquid cooling loop.

Worked example 3 · sweating as the only remaining exit

A cyclist working hard produces about 1000 W of waste heat. The air is at 35 °C, so radiation and convection cannot help. How fast must sweat evaporate to hold core temperature steady, and what happens if it cannot? (Lv at skin temperature ≈ 2.43×10⁶ J·kg⁻¹; body mass 70 kg, c = 3470 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹.)

  1. Every kilogram of sweat that evaporates removes Lv = 2.43×10⁶ J. Required mass rate: dm/dt = P/Lv = 1000 / 2.43×10⁶ = 4.12×10⁻⁴ kg·s⁻¹.
  2. Per hour: (4.12×10⁻⁴)(3600) = 1.48 kg·h⁻¹ ≈ 1.5 L of sweat per hour — which is why fluid replacement, not willpower, limits endurance performance.
  3. If evaporation is blocked (100 % humidity, occlusive clothing), the 1000 W has nowhere to go: dT/dt = P/(mc) = 1000/[(70)(3470)] = 4.12×10⁻³ K·s⁻¹ = 0.25 K per minute.
  4. Core temperature therefore climbs from 37 °C to the 40 °C danger threshold in 3 / 4.12×10⁻³ ≈ 730 s ≈ 12 minutes.
≈1.5 L·h⁻¹ of sweat; otherwise +0.25 °C per minute, heat stroke in ~12 min

Exam point · sweat must evaporate

Sweat that drips off the body carries away only mcΔT, which is negligible; it is the latent heat of vaporisation that does the work. That single fact explains why humid heat kills and dry heat does not, why fanning helps (it removes the saturated boundary layer, not the heat itself), and why the wet-bulb temperature — not the air temperature — is the physiological limit. A wet-bulb temperature at or above 35 °C is unsurvivable at rest, because skin cannot be cooler than the wet bulb.

§ 06

Conduction, convection, radiation

Definition & formula

Conduction
Energy transfer through a material by molecular collisions and (in metals) by free electrons, with no bulk movement of matter.
Convection
Energy transfer by the bulk flow of a heated fluid; natural if driven by buoyancy, forced if driven by a pump, fan or heart.
Radiation
Energy transfer by electromagnetic waves. It requires no medium, and every body above 0 K does it.
Emissivity e
The ratio of a surface's radiated power to that of an ideal black body at the same temperature; 0 ≤ e ≤ 1, and by Kirchhoff's law it equals the surface's absorptivity at the same wavelength.

Fourier's law has the shape of every transport law you will meet: flow rate = (driving difference) / (resistance). Thicker slab, more resistance; larger area, less. That is why Rth = L/(kA) behaves exactly like electrical resistance and why layered insulation — skin, subcutaneous fat, clothing, trapped air — is analysed by adding resistances in series, not by adding conductivities.

Exam point · insulators are air, held still

Wool, down, fur, fibreglass and foam all have k barely below that of still air, because they are mostly air. Their job is to stop the air convecting. This is why wet clothing is dangerous (water with k = 0.6 replaces air with k = 0.026, a 23-fold jump), why wind chill is real (forced convection strips the boundary layer), and why a vacuum flask needs both a vacuum, to kill conduction and convection, and a silvered wall, to kill radiation.

Radiation: the fourth power and the net exchange

Every surface radiates at eσAT⁴ and simultaneously absorbs whatever its surroundings send it, eσATs⁴ — the same e appears in both because a good emitter is necessarily a good absorber (Kirchhoff). The observable quantity is the difference. Two facts fall out immediately: the transfer reverses sign when the room is hotter than the skin, and because of the fourth power a modest temperature difference produces a surprisingly large net flow.

Worked example 4 · net radiative loss from a resting person

Skin at 33 °C, exposed area 1.8 m², emissivity 0.97 in the infrared, room walls at 20 °C. Find the net radiated power. (σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴.)

  1. Convert to kelvin first — the fourth power makes this non-negotiable. T = 306 K, Ts = 293 K.
  2. 306⁴ = 8.768×10⁹ K⁴; 293⁴ = 7.370×10⁹ K⁴; difference = 1.398×10⁹ K⁴.
  3. eσA = (0.97)(5.67×10⁻⁸)(1.8) = 9.90×10⁻⁸ W·K⁻⁴.
  4. Pnet = (9.90×10⁻⁸)(1.398×10⁹) = 138 W.
  5. Compare with a basal metabolic rate of ≈100 W. Radiation alone would overcool a naked person in a 20 °C room — which is exactly why clothing exists and why an unclothed neonate, with a large surface-to-volume ratio, loses heat dangerously fast.
Pnet ≈ 1.4×10² W outward

Trap · (T⁴ − Ts⁴) ≠ (T − Ts)⁴

Take the fourth power of each temperature then subtract. With the numbers above, (306 − 293)⁴ = 13⁴ = 28 561 K⁴ — five orders of magnitude short of the correct 1.398×10⁹ K⁴. Second trap in the same formula: skin emissivity is 0.97 regardless of visible skin colour, because body-temperature radiation peaks in the far infrared (λmax = 2.90×10⁻³/306 = 9.5 μm), where all human skin is effectively black. Visible colour is irrelevant; this is also how thermal cameras work.

The human body as a worked case

At rest in a 20 °C room, a clothed adult sheds ≈100 W in roughly these proportions: radiation ~60 %, evaporation (sweat plus insensible loss from skin and airways) ~22 %, convection ~15 %, conduction ~3 %. Conduction is negligible because the body normally touches only air and a small area of chair. The proportions swing violently with conditions:

  • Cold air: radiation and convection rise; vasoconstriction increases the insulating thickness L of the skin-plus-fat shell, and shivering adds metabolic heat.
  • Ambient temperature above skin temperature: the (T⁴ − Ts⁴) and ΔT terms go negative — the room now heats you — and evaporation becomes the only outward channel. See Worked example 3.
  • Immersion: k for water is 23× that of air and convection is far more effective, so survival time in cold water is measured in tens of minutes.
  • Clinically: therapeutic hypothermia and surgical warming blankets are just deliberate manipulations of these four channels.

Trap · absolute pressure, absolute temperature

P in PV = nRT is absolute pressure. A tyre gauge reading 200 kPa means 200 kPa above atmospheric, so P = 301 kPa. Likewise a blood-pressure cuff reading 120 mmHg is a gauge pressure. And T is kelvin: "the gas is heated from 20 °C to 40 °C at constant volume, so the pressure doubles" is wrong — the ratio is 313/293 = 1.068, a 6.8 % rise, not 100 %.

Exam point · alveolar gas and Dalton's law

Inspired air at 1 atm is 760 mmHg total, 20.9 % O₂. In the alveoli it is saturated with water vapour, which at 37 °C contributes a fixed 47 mmHg independent of altitude. That leaves 713 mmHg of dry gas to share out, so inspired PO₂ is 0.209 × 713 ≈ 149 mmHg, falling to ≈100 mmHg in the alveolus once oxygen has been extracted and CO₂ (≈40 mmHg) added. The four partial pressures sum back to 760. This is Dalton's law doing clinical work, and it is why the physics and physiology papers can ask the same question.

Where the ideal gas law fails. Two assumptions break first. At high pressure the molecules' own volume is no longer negligible, so the real volume exceeds the ideal prediction; near condensation, intermolecular attraction pulls molecules inward and the real pressure falls below the ideal prediction. Van der Waals' equation (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT patches both, with a for attraction and b for excluded volume. For anything at room temperature and around one atmosphere — which includes every gas in a respiratory or anaesthetic question — the ideal law is good to a per cent or so.

Where the pressure formula comes from

Put one molecule of mass m in a cubical box of side L, moving with x-component vx. Each elastic bounce off the right-hand wall reverses that component, so the momentum delivered is Δp = 2mvx. The molecule returns after travelling 2L, so it hits that wall vx/2L times per second. Average force from one molecule = (2mvx)(vx/2L) = mvx²/L. Sum over N molecules and divide by the wall area L²:

P = N m ⟨vx²⟩ / L³ = (N/V) m ⟨vx²⟩V = L³

Nothing distinguishes x, y and z in a random gas, so ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3, giving P = ⅓(N/V)m⟨v²⟩. Multiply by V and compare with PV = NkBT: the two agree only if ⟨½mv²⟩ = (3/2)kBT. That identification is what temperature is — a measure of mean translational kinetic energy per molecule, with the factor 3/2 coming from three spatial dimensions at ½kBT each.

Numbers worth carrying

At 300 K: kBT = 4.14×10⁻²¹ J ≈ 1/40 eV, and the mean translational energy (3/2)kBT = 6.21×10⁻²¹ J ≈ 0.039 eV — per molecule, whatever the molecule is. Per mole at body temperature, RT = (8.31)(310) ≈ 2.6 kJ·mol⁻¹: the same 2.6 kJ·mol⁻¹ that sets the scale for whether a noncovalent bond survives.

Worked example 5 · molecular speeds in air

At 27 °C, find vrms for N₂ (M = 28.0 g·mol⁻¹) and for O₂ (M = 32.0 g·mol⁻¹), and compare their average translational kinetic energies. (R = 8.31 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, kB = 1.38×10⁻²³ J·K⁻¹.)

  1. T = 27 + 273 = 300 K. Convert molar masses to SI: 0.0280 and 0.0320 kg·mol⁻¹.
  2. 3RT = 3(8.31)(300) = 7479 J·mol⁻¹.
  3. N₂: vrms = √(7479/0.0280) = √(2.671×10⁵) = 517 m·s⁻¹.
  4. O₂: vrms = √(7479/0.0320) = √(2.337×10⁵) = 483 m·s⁻¹.
  5. Cross-check with the ratio rule: v ∝ 1/√M, so vO₂/vN₂ = √(28/32) = 0.935, and (517)(0.935) = 483 ✓.
  6. Kinetic energies: (3/2)kBT = 1.5(1.38×10⁻²³)(300) = 6.21×10⁻²¹ J for both. Same temperature ⇒ same mean translational KE; the heavier molecule simply moves more slowly to carry it.

Trap · what vrms does and does not depend on

It depends on T and M only. Not on pressure, not on density, not on the size of the container. Compress a gas isothermally and every molecule keeps exactly the speed it had; the pressure rises because collisions become more frequent, not more violent. A question that offers "doubling the pressure at constant temperature doubles vrms" is testing this directly. Second trap: to double vrms you must quadruple T, because v ∝ √T.

Equipartition: where 3/2 becomes 5/2

The 3/2 in ⟨½mv²⟩ = (3/2)kBT counts three translational degrees of freedom. A molecule with internal structure has more places to put energy, and equipartition hands each of them ½kBT:

A diatomic molecule has two rotational axes that matter (spinning about the bond axis carries negligible moment of inertia) and one vibrational mode — but the vibration is frozen out at room temperature, because quantum mechanics forbids it from accepting less than one full vibrational quantum, which is much larger than kBT. Heat air to well over 1000 K and CV climbs towards (7/2)R as the vibration wakes up. This failure of classical equipartition at ordinary temperatures was one of the first hard clues that energy is quantised.

The same counting is why CO₂ does not belong in the bottom row: it is a linear molecule, so like a diatomic it has only two rotational axes that matter, and its classical f is 5, not 6. Its measured CV at room temperature is nevertheless a little above (5/2)R, because its low-frequency bending vibration is already partly awake at 300 K — a reminder that equipartition is the classical limit, not a law. Count rotations from the shape of the molecule, never from the number of atoms.

Exam point · Graham's law and diffusion

Because all gases at the same temperature share the same mean kinetic energy, lighter molecules move faster and effuse faster: rate ∝ 1/√M. Helium effuses √8 ≈ 2.8 times faster than oxygen. Be careful transferring this to physiology: gas transfer across the alveolar membrane is governed by Fick's law, where solubility dominates — CO₂ diffuses about 20 times faster than O₂ across the membrane despite being the heavier molecule, because it is far more soluble. Graham's law applies to gas-phase effusion, not to transfer through a liquid barrier.

Why Cp − CV = R

Heat one mole of gas by ΔT at constant volume: it cannot expand, so it does no work, and every joule goes into internal energy — Q = ΔU = CVΔT. Now heat the same mole by the same ΔT at constant pressure: ΔU is identical (for an ideal gas U depends only on T), but the gas must also push the surroundings back, doing W = PΔV = RΔT for one mole. So you must supply CpΔT = CVΔT + RΔT. Divide by ΔT and the Mayer relation drops out. Cp is always the larger, and the gap is exactly the work of expansion.

Exam point · ΔU = nCVΔT is not restricted to constant volume

For an ideal gas, U is a function of T alone, so a change of state from T₁ to T₂ has the same ΔU whatever path it took. You may therefore compute ΔU with nCVΔT for an isobaric, adiabatic or wildly irregular process — the constant-volume label on CV refers only to how the constant is measured. This trick collapses most first-law problems into one line.

Worked example 6 · isothermal expansion

2.0 mol of an ideal gas expands isothermally and reversibly at 300 K from 10.0 L to 30.0 L. Find W, Q and ΔU, and the entropy change of the gas.

  1. Isothermal ⇒ ΔT = 0 ⇒ ΔU = nCVΔT = 0, whatever the gas is made of.
  2. W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = (2.0)(8.31)(300) ln(3.00) = (4986)(1.0986) = 5.48×10³ J.
  3. First law: ΔU = Q − W with ΔU = 0, so Q = W = +5.48 kJ. Every joule absorbed leaves again as work; the gas is a perfect pass-through, which is exactly why no engine can run on a single isothermal stroke.
  4. Entropy of the gas (reversible, isothermal): ΔS = Q/T = 5477/300 = 18.3 J·K⁻¹. Equivalently ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = (2.0)(8.31)(1.0986) = 18.3 J·K⁻¹ ✓ — more volume means more accessible microstates (§10).
  5. The reservoir loses the same 5477 J at the same 300 K, so ΔSreservoir = −18.3 J·K⁻¹ and ΔSuniverse = 0. That zero is the signature of a reversible process.
W = Q = +5.48 kJ, ΔU = 0, ΔSgas = +18.3 J·K⁻¹

Worked example 7 · adiabatic compression, and why a diesel needs no spark plug

Air (γ = 1.40) at 1.00 atm and 300 K is compressed rapidly to 1/18 of its volume. Find the final temperature and pressure.

  1. "Rapidly" is the keyword: there is no time for heat to leave the cylinder, so treat it as adiabatic, Q = 0.
  2. Temperature relation: T₂ = T₁ (V₁/V₂)γ−1 = 300 × 180.40. 180.40 = 3.178, so T₂ = 953 K ≈ 680 °C.
  3. Pressure relation: P₂ = P₁ (V₁/V₂)γ = 1.00 × 181.40 = 57.2 atm.
  4. Consistency check against PV = nRT: P₂V₂/(P₁V₁) = 57.2/18 = 3.18 = T₂/T₁ ✓.
  5. Diesel fuel autoignites at roughly 500 K, well below 953 K, so injecting it at the top of the stroke ignites it without any spark. The energy came from work done on the gas (Wby < 0 ⇒ ΔU > 0), not from any heat supplied.
T₂ ≈ 953 K (680 °C), P₂ ≈ 57 atm — with Q = 0 throughout

Trap · adiabatic is not isothermal

"No heat is exchanged" does not mean "the temperature does not change" — it means the opposite is likely. Adiabatic compression heats (bicycle pump, diesel, descending air over a mountain range); adiabatic expansion cools (aerosol can, rising air forming cloud, a gas cylinder's valve frosting over). Isothermal is the slow case where a reservoir keeps T fixed; adiabatic is the fast case where there is no time for heat to move.

Where this reaches medicine. A quasi-static pressure–volume loop with an enclosed area is exactly how respiratory physiology plots the work of breathing: the lung's compliance loop encloses an area equal to the work done per breath, and the difference between the inflation and deflation limbs — hysteresis — is the energy dissipated against surface tension and tissue friction. The same picture reappears as the cardiac pressure–volume loop, whose area is the stroke work of the left ventricle.

§ 10

The second law and entropy

Definition & formula

Second law — Kelvin–Planck statement
No cyclic process can take heat from a single reservoir and convert it entirely into work. Some heat must always be rejected to a colder reservoir.
Second law — Clausius statement
No cyclic process can transfer heat from a colder body to a hotter one with no other effect. Moving heat "uphill" always costs work.
Entropy S
A state function whose change, for a reversible process, is the heat absorbed divided by the absolute temperature at which it is absorbed. Unit J·K⁻¹.

The same quantity read microscopically: a measure of the number of microscopic arrangements Ω consistent with the macroscopic state.

The first law says energy is conserved; it is completely silent about direction. Nothing in ΔU = Q − W forbids a cup of coffee spontaneously boiling while the kitchen cools by a millikelvin, or a shattered glass reassembling. The second law is the statement that supplies the missing arrow — and it is worth seeing that its two classical forms are one law.

Kelvin–Planck ⟺ Clausius

Suppose you could violate Clausius: a magic box moves Qc from the cold reservoir to the hot one for free. Now run an ordinary engine alongside it, taking Qh from the hot reservoir, producing work W and rejecting exactly Qc to the cold one. The cold reservoir's books balance to zero — it receives Qc and gives back Qc — so the composite device does nothing but extract (Qh − Qc) from a single reservoir and turn it entirely into work. That violates Kelvin–Planck.

Suppose instead you could violate Kelvin–Planck: a machine converts heat from the hot reservoir wholly into work with no rejection. Feed that work into an ordinary refrigerator. The pair takes nothing but heat from the cold reservoir and delivers it to the hot one with no net work input — violating Clausius. Each statement implies the other, so they are two readings of the same restriction.

Exam point · which statement does the device break?

Kelvin–Planck bans one reservoir → pure work (a ship powered by cooling the ocean; a car engine with no exhaust heat). Clausius bans cold → hot with no work (a fridge that runs unplugged). A device that merely beats the Carnot efficiency between two reservoirs breaks neither statement directly, but it can be combined with a reversed Carnot engine to build a Clausius violator — which is exactly how Carnot's theorem is proved.

Entropy as bookkeeping: the same joule is worth less when it is cold

ΔS = Q/T says that the entropy cost of moving a joule depends on the temperature at which it is moved. Let heat Q flow spontaneously from a hot reservoir at Th to a cold one at Tc:

ΔSuniverse = −Q/Th + Q/Tc = Q (1/Tc − 1/Th) > 0because Tc < Th

The total is positive precisely when the flow goes hot → cold. Run the same arithmetic backwards and the total is negative, which the second law forbids. Entropy is not an extra law bolted on; it is the quantity whose sign encodes the direction of every spontaneous process. Two quick numbers: melting 1 kg of ice at 273 K raises its entropy by 3.34×10⁵/273 = 1.22×10³ J·K⁻¹, and a resting human dissipating 100 W at 310 K exports entropy at 100/310 = 0.32 W·K⁻¹.

The statistical reading

Boltzmann's S = kB ln Ω explains why there is a preferred direction, and the explanation is counting, not physics. Consider N gas molecules in a box that you mentally divide in half. Any particular molecule is equally likely to be on either side, so the number of arrangements with all N on the left is 1, while the total number of arrangements is 2N. For N = 100 the probability of finding them all on one side is 2−100 ≈ 10−30. For one mole, N = 6.0×10²³, the probability is 2−N = 10−1.8×10²³ — the exponent alone is a 24-digit number.

Nothing forbids the gas from collecting in one half — it is simply that the states we call "disordered" are overwhelmingly more numerous than the states we call "ordered", and a system wandering at random through its accessible states will be found in the numerous ones. The logarithm in Boltzmann's formula is what turns a multiplicative count (independent systems have Ω1Ω2 arrangements between them) into an additive quantity — which is why entropy, like energy, can simply be summed over parts of a system.

Trap · three things that are not true about entropy

(1) "Entropy always increases." Only for an isolated system, or for the universe. The entropy of an open system can fall freely — water freezing, a crystal growing, a cell dividing — provided the surroundings gain more. (2) "Entropy is disorder." A useful slogan, a bad definition. Entropy counts accessible microstates; a tidy-looking crystal at high temperature can have more entropy than a messy-looking one at low temperature. (3) "ΔS = Q/T works for any process." Only for a reversible transfer at a well-defined T. For an irreversible change, invent any reversible path between the same two states and integrate along it — S is a state function, so the answer is the same.

§ 11

Engines, Carnot, refrigerators — and why you are legal

Definition & formula

Heat engine
A device that operates in a cycle, absorbing Qh from a hot reservoir, delivering work W, and rejecting Qc to a cold reservoir. Because it is cyclic, ΔU = 0 and therefore W = Qh − Qc exactly.

What you want divided by what you pay for: work out over heat in.

Two reversible isotherms joined by two reversible adiabats. It is the only cycle in which heat is exchanged with the reservoirs at no temperature difference, so no entropy is generated.

No engine working between two given reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible one, and all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency — whatever their working substance.

Worked example 8 · a real power plant against its Carnot ceiling

A steam plant takes heat at 540 °C and rejects it to a condenser at 40 °C. It delivers 800 MW of electrical power at a real thermal efficiency of 42 %. Find the Carnot ceiling, the rate at which it burns fuel energy, and the waste-heat load on the river.

  1. Kelvin first: Th = 540 + 273 = 813 K, Tc = 40 + 273 = 313 K.
  2. eCarnot = 1 − 313/813 = 1 − 0.385 = 0.615 = 61.5 %. No engine between these two reservoirs can beat this, whatever its design or working fluid.
  3. Fuel energy rate: Q̇h = W/e = 800/0.42 = 1905 MW.
  4. Waste heat: Q̇c = Q̇h − W = 1905 − 800 = 1105 MW dumped into the cooling water. This is not bad engineering — Kelvin–Planck makes some Qc compulsory — but 1105 MW is why thermal power stations sit on rivers and coasts.
  5. Note what raises efficiency: Th upward helps far more than Tc downward, because Tc is pinned near ambient. Supercritical boilers exist for exactly this reason.
eCarnot = 61.5 %, Q̇h = 1.9×10³ MW, Q̇c = 1.1×10³ MW

Worked example 9 · a refrigerator and the same machine as a heat pump

A refrigerator keeps its interior at 4 °C in a 25 °C kitchen. Find the best possible COP. Then take the same machine, put the cold coil outdoors at 5 °C and the hot coil indoors at 22 °C, and find the best possible heating COP.

  1. Fridge: Tc = 277 K, Th = 298 K, ΔT = 21 K. COPmax = Tc/(Th − Tc) = 277/21 = 13.2.
  2. Real domestic fridges achieve 3–4, because the compressor is irreversible and the coils must run hotter and colder than the spaces they serve in order to move heat at a finite rate. Reversibility and speed are in direct conflict — that is why the Carnot limit is never reached in practice.
  3. Heat pump: Th = 295 K, Tc = 278 K, ΔT = 17 K. COPmax = Th/(Th − Tc) = 295/17 = 17.4.
  4. Check the identity: for these reservoirs COPfridge = 278/17 = 16.4, and 16.4 + 1 = 17.4 ✓.
  5. Physical reading: a COP of 17 means the pump delivers 17 J of heat indoors for every 1 J of electricity, because 16 of those joules were moved from outside rather than created. An electric bar heater has "COP" exactly 1. Nothing here violates conservation.
COPfridge,max = 13.2; COPheat pump,max = 17.4

Trap · the Carnot formula eats kelvin only

Computing 1 − 40/540 from the Celsius readings in Worked example 8 gives "92.6 %", which is not merely wrong but impossible. The ratio Tc/Th has no meaning on a scale with an arbitrary zero. Related trap: efficiency and COP are different ratios — e is bounded above by 1, COP is not bounded at all and tends to infinity as Th → Tc (moving heat across no temperature difference is free).

Carnot in one line

You are charged for the temperature gap. An engine is paid by the gap (e = ΔT/Th); a fridge or heat pump is charged by it (COP = T/ΔT). Widen the gap and engines get better while pumps get worse.

Why a living organism is not a violation of the second law

A human being assembles ordered protein, DNA and membrane from disordered small molecules, and holds that order for decades. Its entropy is clearly far lower than that of the raw materials. This is not a loophole, and the resolution is precise rather than rhetorical.

An organism is an open system. The second law constrains ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings, not ΔSsystem alone. A cell lowers its own entropy by exporting a larger amount: it ingests chemically reduced, low-entropy fuel and O₂ and excretes CO₂, water, urea and — above all — heat at body temperature. A resting adult dissipating 100 W at 310 K exports entropy at 100/310 ≈ 0.32 J·K⁻¹·s⁻¹, about 2.8×10⁴ J·K⁻¹ per day, which swamps the entropy decrease of any biosynthesis it performs. Death is what happens when the export stops; the corpse then does obey the naive expectation and decays towards equilibrium.

And a muscle is not a heat engine. This is the more interesting half of the answer. If skeletal muscle worked between a hot reservoir at body temperature (310 K) and a cold one at room temperature (293 K), Carnot would cap it at 1 − 293/310 = 5.5 %. Measured muscle efficiency is 20–25 %. Muscle is therefore not running on a temperature difference at all: it is an isothermal chemical free-energy converter, taking the Gibbs free energy released by ATP hydrolysis and converting part of it directly into mechanical work at constant temperature. Its ceiling is set by ΔG, not by Tc/Th. That distinction — chemical work versus thermal work — is the reason biochemistry is written in free energy and engineering in efficiency, and it is worth carrying into every question that tries to apply Carnot to a body.

Exam point

The expected answer to "does life violate the second law?" has three moving parts, and a good answer names all three: (1) the organism is open, so only the total entropy is constrained; (2) the entropy exported as low-grade heat and waste products exceeds the internal decrease; (3) the Earth as a whole is driven by a flow of low-entropy photons from a 5800 K Sun, re-radiated as roughly twenty times as many infrared photons at 255 K. Order on Earth is paid for upstream.
